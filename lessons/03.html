Charge is a property of matter, not a synonym for “electron.” Electrons carry −e, protons +e, and neutrons zero net charge. Use e=1.602×10−19 Ce = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}e=1.602×10−19C in course calculations. A positive net charge usually indicates an electron deficit. A negative net charge indicates excess electrons. Ordinary rubbing transfers electrons rather than creating charge.

Quantization, conservation, and charging mechanisms

q=(Np−Ne)e,N=∣q∣e,Δm=Nme.q=(N_p-N_e)e,\quad N=\frac{|q|}{e},\quad \Delta m=N m_e.q=(Np​−Ne​)e,N=e∣q∣​,Δm=Nme​.

For ordinary isolated charged bodies, q is an integer multiple of e. An isolated system conserves total signed charge: ∑qbefore=∑qafter\sum q_{\mathrm{before}} = \sum q_{\mathrm{after}}∑qbefore​=∑qafter​. Neutral means balanced net charge, not absence of charged particles. Charge is frame invariant in the classical situations covered here.

Worked example — primary p. 8. A charge of +0.32 C+ 0.32\,\mathrm{C}+0.32C represents 0.32(1.602×10−19)=1.998×1018\frac{0.32}{\left( 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \right)} = 1.998 \times 10^{18}(1.602×10−19)0.32​=1.998×1018 removed electrons. Its mass decreases by about 1.82×10−12 kg1.82 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{kg}1.82×10−12kg if only electron transfer changes its mass. Neutrino oscillations show that neutrinos are not all massless. The source's blanket zero-rest-mass statement is inaccurate and unnecessary for this charge calculation.

Conduction requires contact and permits charge transfer. Friction transfers charge during rubbing. Induction separates charge without contact with the inducing body. To leave a single conductor with net charge by the standard grounding method, approach the charged rod, connect the ground, remove the ground, then remove the rod. Changing that order can remove the intended final charge. Polarization separates positive and negative charge slightly within a neutral object; attraction does not prove the object has acquired a net charge.

Conductors have mobile charges. Insulators restrict bulk charge motion. Semiconductors have conductivity between these broad categories and strongly depend on material conditions. In electrostatic equilibrium, excess conductor charge lies on its surface; the field inside conducting material is zero. These statements concern equilibrium, not every current-carrying wire.

Charging diagrams and charge-conservation calculations

Names and signs — primary pp. 4–6. The historical word “electric” comes from the Greek word for amber. The coulomb names the charge unit after Coulomb. Franklin's positive and negative labels distinguish the two charge signs. “Positive” does not mean more electrons; an electron deficit gives a positive net charge in the ordinary charging examples.

Source correction. Primary p. 21 repeats the proton's mass for the neutron. Their masses are close but not identical. The rounded values above distinguish them. A neutral object can contain many charged particles; their net charge cancels.

Grounding. Earth acts as a large charge reservoir. Connecting a conductor to ground lets electrons move as the surrounding electric field requires. With a nearby negative rod, electrons leave the grounded conductor. Remove the ground first, then the rod: the conductor remains positive. A positive inducing rod draws electrons from ground and leaves the conductor negative after the same removal sequence.

Conduction versus polarization. A charged metal redistributes mobile electrons over its surface. Charge on an insulating rod does not spread as freely. Neutral paper can still be attracted to a charged comb. Polarization places the nearer opposite-sign charge slightly closer, making its attraction exceed the farther like-sign repulsion. Attraction alone does not prove a net charge.

Identical conducting spheres share charge equally: a labeled extension

For two isolated identical metal spheres that touch, conservation gives Qtotal=Q1+Q2Q_{\mathrm{total}} = Q_{1} + Q_{2}Qtotal​=Q1​+Q2​. Symmetry gives equal final charges after they reach equilibrium. Therefore Qfinal,each=Q1+Q22Q_{\mathrm{final,each}}=\frac{Q_1+Q_2}{2}Qfinal,each​=2Q1​+Q2​​.

For +8 nC+ 8\,\mathrm{nC}+8nC and −2 nC- 2\,\mathrm{nC}−2nC, the total is +6 nC+ 6\,\mathrm{nC}+6nC, so each ends with +3 nC+ 3\,\mathrm{nC}+3nC. The initially negative sphere loses enough electrons to become positive. Unequal spheres do not generally share charge equally.
