Temperature describes thermal state. Heat Q is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. Internal energy U belongs to the system. Heat is not a stored substance or another name for U. Work is a different mode of energy transfer. The source sometimes uses these terms interchangeably; the notes keep them separate.

Temperature scales and differences

TK=TC+273.15,TF=95TC+32,TC=59(TF−32).T_K=T_C+273.15,\quad T_F=\frac95T_C+32,\quad T_C=\frac59(T_F-32).TK​=TC​+273.15,TF​=59​TC​+32,TC​=95​(TF​−32).

Derivation. Water's standard freezing/boiling interval spans 100 Celsius degrees and 180 Fahrenheit degrees. The slope is 180100=95\frac{180}{100} = \frac{9}{5}100180​=59​. The freezing-point offset supplies 32. Kelvin uses the same interval size as Celsius but a different zero.

ΔTK=ΔTC,ΔTF=95ΔTC.\Delta T_K=\Delta T_C,\quad \Delta T_F=\frac95\Delta T_C.ΔTK​=ΔTC​,ΔTF​=59​ΔTC​.

Do not add 273.15 to a temperature CHANGE. A 20°C rise is a 20 K20\,\mathrm{K}20K rise, not 293.15 K293.15\,\mathrm{K}293.15K. Gas-law absolute-temperature ratios require Kelvin. Absolute zero does not mean every quantum-mechanical contribution to energy vanishes.

Thermometers measure temperature through a calibrated property, such as a liquid's volume, gas pressure, or electrical resistance. Mercury's illustrated −39°C to 357°C liquid range explains one thermometer limitation; it is not a universal temperature scale limit.

Companion extension. Water contracts when heated from about 0°C to 4°C at ordinary pressure. Its density reaches a maximum near 4°C. “All matter expands when heated” is not a universal law. A phase diagram plots pressure against temperature. Boundaries separate phases; the triple point joins three phases. A liquid–gas boundary ends at the critical point. Do not confuse a phase diagram with a heating curve.

Heat transfer, specific heat, and thermal equilibrium

Conduction transfers energy through microscopic interactions; metals also have mobile-electron contributions. Convection carries energy with bulk fluid motion. Radiation transfers electromagnetic energy and needs no material medium. The source's explanation that solid molecules are generally far apart is incorrect.

The zeroth law states: if A and B separately share thermal equilibrium with C, A and B share thermal equilibrium. Equality of temperature, not equal mass or equal internal energy, characterizes that equilibrium.

Material tables, thermometer operation, and phase diagrams

The primary and companion decks use different rounded material coefficients. For example, the primary gives steel α=1.2×10−5 K−1\alpha = 1.2 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K}^{-1}α=1.2×10−5K−1. The companion uses 1.1×10−5 K−11.1 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K}^{-1}1.1×10−5K−1. Use the coefficient stated in each problem.

Bimetallic strip. Two bonded metals have different expansion coefficients. Heating makes the higher-expansion metal occupy the outside of the bend. The strip therefore bends toward the lower-expansion metal. A thermostat uses the bend to operate a contact. A liquid thermometer instead uses a calibrated change in liquid volume.

Historical correction. The companion prints 1792 for Celsius's scale. Uppsala University dates his original scale to 1742. His original orientation differed from the modern Celsius convention.

Microscopic model. Solid particles vibrate around equilibrium positions. Liquid particles remain close but can rearrange. Gas particles are widely separated. “Temperature measures average kinetic energy” is a useful introductory model, especially for an ideal gas. It is not a universal formula for the total internal energy of every material.

Derivation∴

Read the slope of a heating curve

During a single phase, Q=mcΔTQ = mc\Delta TQ=mcΔT. At fixed m and approximately constant c, ΔTΔQ=1(mc)\frac{\Delta T}{\Delta Q} = \frac{1}{\left( mc \right)}ΔQΔT​=(mc)1​. A larger heat capacity produces a flatter temperature-versus-energy slope. During an equilibrium phase change, the added energy changes phase fraction, so the temperature plateau has slope zero.

For heating at constant power P, Q=PtQ = PtQ=Pt. Thus dTdt=P(mc)\frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{P}{\left( mc \right)}dtdT​=(mc)P​ within a phase, and a complete phase transition takes Δt=mLP\Delta t = \frac{\mathrm{mL}}{P}Δt=PmL​. These relations require negligible energy losses and the stated constant-power model.

Situational problems: choose the law, then solve

These added practice problems apply the module concepts. They are not copied examination questions. Keep the stated assumptions when you use each result.

Situation 1: A hole in an expanding plate

An unconstrained isotropic metal plate contains a circular hole. It is heated uniformly and remains solid. What happens to the hole to first order?

  1. Identify the geometry. Uniform isotropic free expansion scales every coordinate separation.

  2. Apply the length factor to the opening.

Df=D0(1+αΔT)D_f=D_0(1+\alpha\Delta T)Df​=D0​(1+αΔT)

  1. Separate the measures. A hole’s area changes by approximately 2αΔT, while its diameter changes by αΔT.

  2. Respect the assumptions. Constraints, nonuniform heating, or anisotropy need additional modeling.

Answer: Its diameter increases by the same fractional linear expansion as the plate.

Why the other answers do not work:

  • Its diameter decreases because surrounding metal expands inward. — Unconstrained uniform expansion scales the hole as part of the geometry.
  • Its diameter stays fixed because the hole contains no metal. — The boundary positions move with the expanding solid.
  • Its diameter follows the volume coefficient rather than the linear coefficient. — Diameter is a length, so its first-order change uses the linear coefficient.

Situation 2: Not enough heat to melt all the ice

Ice and water exchange heat in an insulated container. The warm water can reach 0°C but cannot supply enough heat to melt all the ice. Assume ordinary pressure and equilibrium. Which result is possible?

  1. Test the phase-change budget first. Calculate the energy that cooling the warm part to 0°C can release.

  2. Compare with the melting requirement.

Qavailable<miLfQ_{\mathrm{available}}<m_iL_fQavailable​<mi​Lf​

  1. Choose the physical regime. Only part of the ice melts. Both phases remain at 0°C under the stated conditions.

  2. Avoid an invalid algebraic answer. Do not assume complete melting and accept a final temperature inconsistent with the assumed phase.

Answer: A mixture of ice and water at 0°C.

Why the other answers do not work:

  • All the ice must become water at a negative final temperature. — That violates the stated equilibrium phase condition for the mixture.
  • Both components stay at their initial temperatures. — Thermal exchange continues until an allowed equilibrium is reached.
  • The final temperature is always the arithmetic mean. — A phase change and unequal heat capacities invalidate that shortcut.

Situation 3: Heat is not internal energy

A gas is compressed adiabatically by an external agent. The agent does positive work on the gas. Which conclusion follows from the first law?

  1. Define the work convention. Use positive Wby for work done by the gas.

  2. Translate compression into signs.

Q=0,Wby<0Q=0,\qquad W_{\mathrm{by}}<0Q=0,Wby​<0
  1. Apply the first law.
ΔU=Q−Wby=−Wby>0\Delta U=Q-W_{\mathrm{by}}=-W_{\mathrm{by}}>0ΔU=Q−Wby​=−Wby​>0
  1. Interpret the transfer. Adiabatic means no heat transfer; it does not mean no energy transfer.

Answer: Its internal energy increases, although no heat enters.

Why the other answers do not work:

  • Its internal energy must stay fixed because Q=0Q=0Q=0. — Work also transfers energy.
  • Its internal energy decreases because compression is always cooling. — That reverses the work-on-system sign.
  • The gas must undergo a phase change. — The first law does not imply that phase change from the stated information.

Situation 4: Container and liquid both expand

A vessel is initially full of liquid. Both are heated uniformly. The liquid’s volume-expansion coefficient equals the vessel’s internal volume-expansion coefficient. Under the small-expansion model, what happens?

  1. Write the competing volume changes.
ΔVl=βlV0ΔT,ΔVc=βcV0ΔT\Delta V_l=\beta_lV_0\Delta T,\qquad\Delta V_c=\beta_cV_0\Delta TΔVl​=βl​V0​ΔT,ΔVc​=βc​V0​ΔT
  1. Subtract capacity growth.
Voverflow≈V0(βl−βc)ΔTV_{\mathrm{overflow}}\approx V_0(\beta_l-\beta_c)\Delta TVoverflow​≈V0​(βl​−βc​)ΔT

  1. Use the stated equality. The first-order difference is zero.

  2. State the approximation. This result does not assert that every higher-order effect vanishes.

Answer: No first-order overflow occurs.

Why the other answers do not work:

  • The liquid necessarily overflows because all liquids expand. — The vessel’s capacity also changes.
  • The liquid level necessarily falls because the vessel expands. — The liquid’s equal expansion must also be counted.
  • The linear coefficient should be used directly as the volume coefficient. — For an isotropic solid, the first-order volume coefficient is approximately 3α.
