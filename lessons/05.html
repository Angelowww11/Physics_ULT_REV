Electric potential V is energy per charge. Potential energy U belongs to a charge configuration. One volt is one joule per coulomb. A point can have nonzero V and zero E; a zero potential does not imply zero field.

Work, energy, and consistent endpoint labels

For a constant force, W=Fdcos⁡θW = Fd \cos \thetaW=Fdcosθ. More generally W=∫F⋅dlW = \int F \cdot dlW=∫F⋅dl. The net-work theorem is Wnet=ΔKW_{\mathrm{net}} = \Delta KWnet​=ΔK. For the electrostatic force, Wfield=−ΔUW_{\mathrm{field}} = - \Delta UWfield​=−ΔU. A slow external mover with no kinetic-energy change does Wexternal=+ΔUW_{\mathrm{external}} = + \Delta UWexternal​=+ΔU. The external-work equality requires those conditions; it is not true for arbitrary accelerated motion.

ΔU=q(Vb−Va),Wfield=q(Va−Vb),ΔK=−qΔV.\Delta U=q(V_b-V_a),\quad W_{field}=q(V_a-V_b),\quad \Delta K=-q\Delta V.ΔU=q(Vb​−Va​),Wfield​=q(Va​−Vb​),ΔK=−qΔV.

The ΔK\Delta KΔK expression assumes that the electrostatic force supplies all the net work. Include other forces separately when they do work. Some slides define Vab=Va−VbV_{ab} = V_{a} - V_{b}Vab​=Va​−Vb​. These notes define ΔV=Vb−Va\Delta V = V_{b} - V_{a}ΔV=Vb​−Va​ when discussing a move a → b. Field–potential relation and equipotentials

ΔV=−∫abE⋅dl,E=−∇V.\Delta V=-\int_a^b\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l,\quad \mathbf E=-\nabla V.ΔV=−∫ab​E⋅dl,E=−∇V.

For uniform E, ΔV=−Edcos⁡θ\Delta V = - Ed \cos \thetaΔV=−Edcosθ where θ\thetaθ is the angle between E and displacement. If motion is parallel to an equipotential, ΔV=0\Delta V = 0ΔV=0, so electrostatic work is zero. Field lines are perpendicular to equipotential surfaces where E is nonzero. At a field-zero point, there is no unique local field direction to draw.

Equal voltage intervals do not give equal radial intervals.

Derivation∴

Derive why equipotentials are perpendicular to E

For an infinitesimal displacement dl along an equipotential, dV=0dV = 0dV=0. The electrostatic relation gives dV=−E⋅dldV = - E \cdot dldV=−E⋅dl. Therefore E⋅dl=0E \cdot dl = 0E⋅dl=0 for every tangent displacement. Where E is nonzero, E has no tangent component and must point normal to the surface.

For closely spaced equal-voltage contours, ∣E∣≈∣ΔV∣Δsnormal\left| E \right| \approx \frac{\left| \Delta V \right|}{\Delta s_{\mathrm{normal}}}∣E∣≈Δsnormal​∣ΔV∣​. Smaller normal spacing means a stronger field. Use perpendicular spacing, not an arbitrary diagonal separation between two contour points.
