## Thermal physics: separate heat, temperature, and internal energy

Temperature describes thermal state. Heat Q is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. Internal energy U belongs to the system. Heat is not a stored substance or another name for U. Work is a different mode of energy transfer. The source sometimes uses these terms interchangeably; the notes keep them separate.

### Temperature scales and differences

$$T_K=T_C+273.15,\quad T_F=\frac95T_C+32,\quad T_C=\frac59(T_F-32).$$

**Derivation.** Water's standard freezing/boiling interval spans 100 Celsius degrees and 180 Fahrenheit degrees. The slope is $\frac{180}{100} = \frac{9}{5}$. The freezing-point offset supplies 32. Kelvin uses the same interval size as Celsius but a different zero.

$$\Delta T_K=\Delta T_C,\quad \Delta T_F=\frac95\Delta T_C.$$

Do not add 273.15 to a temperature CHANGE. A 20°C rise is a $20\,\mathrm{K}$ rise, not $293.15\,\mathrm{K}$. Gas-law absolute-temperature ratios require Kelvin. Absolute zero does not mean every quantum-mechanical contribution to energy vanishes.

Thermometers measure temperature through a calibrated property, such as a liquid's volume, gas pressure, or electrical resistance. Mercury's illustrated −39°C to 357°C liquid range explains one thermometer limitation; it is not a universal temperature scale limit.

### Linear, area, and volume expansion: derivations and inverses

For moderate changes with approximately constant coefficient, $\Delta L = \alpha L_{0} \Delta T$ and $L = L_{0} \left( 1 + \alpha \Delta T \right)$. The coefficient has units $K^{-1}$ or °$C^{-1}$.

$$\alpha=\frac{L-L_0}{L_0\Delta T},\quad \Delta T=\frac{L-L_0}{\alpha L_0},\quad L_0=\frac{L}{1+\alpha\Delta T}.$$

**Derive area expansion.** Expand both dimensions by the same factor: $\frac{A}{A_{0}} = \left( 1 + \alpha \Delta T \right)^{2} = 1 + 2 \alpha \Delta T + \alpha^{2} \Delta T^{2}$. Neglect the second-order term only when $\left| \alpha \Delta T \right| \ll 1$. Then $\Delta A \approx 2 \alpha A_{0} \Delta T$. The slides call the area coefficient $\gamma$; $\gamma \approx 2 \alpha$.

**Derive volume expansion.** Three dimensions give $\frac{V}{V_{0}} = \left( 1 + \alpha \Delta T \right)^{3}$. Its first-order term is $1 + 3 \alpha \Delta T$. Thus $\beta \approx 3 \alpha$ for an isotropic solid, and $\Delta V \approx \beta V_{0} \Delta T$. Liquids have their own volume coefficient; do not assign them a solid'$s \alpha$ without justification. The finite-power expressions follow the linear dimensional model; a temperature-dependent instantaneous coefficient requires integration.

A hole expands as the surrounding material would expand if the hole were filled with the same material. Heating both a plug and hole made from the same material does not automatically create clearance. Cooling only the plug or heating only the plate changes their relative fit.

**Worked example — primary p. 11.** A $50\,\mathrm{m}$ steel tape at 20°C has $\alpha = 11 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K}^{-1}$. At 35°C, $\Delta L = 0.00825\,\mathrm{m}$ and $L = 50.00825\,\mathrm{m}$. The printed $50.008\,\mathrm{m}$ is rounded. A hot tape has wider-spaced markings; distinguish the indicated reading from the actual length.

### Containers, overflow, water's anomaly, and phase diagrams

For a full container and a small common temperature rise:

$$\Delta V_{overflow}=V_0(\beta_{liquid}-\beta_{container})\Delta T.$$

**Derivation.** Subtract the expanded container capacity $V_{0} \left( 1 + \beta _{\mathrm{container}}\Delta T \right)$ from the expanded liquid volume $V_{0} \left( 1 + \beta _{\mathrm{liquid}}\Delta T \right)$. The original $V_{0}$ terms cancel. A negative result means no positive overflow in this model; the liquid leaves space instead.

**Worked example — primary p. 18.** $V_{0} = 200\,\mathrm{cm}^{3}$; $\Delta T = 80\,\mathrm{K}$; $\beta Hg = 18 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K}^{-1}$; $\alpha _{\mathrm{glass}} = 0.40 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K}^{-1}$. Therefore $\beta _{\mathrm{glass}} = 1.20 \times 10^{-5}$. Overflow: $200 \left( 18 - 1.2 \right) \times 10^{-5} \times 80 = 2.688\,\mathrm{cm}^{3}$. Keep volume units consistent; conversion to $m^{3}$ is not necessary when every volume uses $\mathrm{cm}^{3}$.

**Companion extension.** Water contracts when heated from about 0°C to 4°C at ordinary pressure. Its density reaches a maximum near 4°C. “All matter expands when heated” is not a universal law. A phase diagram plots pressure against temperature. Boundaries separate phases; the triple point joins three phases. A liquid–gas boundary ends at the critical point. Do not confuse a phase diagram with a heating curve.

### Heat transfer, specific heat, and thermal equilibrium

Conduction transfers energy through microscopic interactions; metals also have mobile-electron contributions. Convection carries energy with bulk fluid motion. Radiation transfers electromagnetic energy and needs no material medium. The source's explanation that solid molecules are generally far apart is incorrect.

The zeroth law states: if A and B separately share thermal equilibrium with C, A and B share thermal equilibrium. Equality of temperature, not equal mass or equal internal energy, characterizes that equilibrium.

$$Q=mc(T_f-T_i),\quad c=\frac{Q}{m\Delta T},\quad m=\frac{Q}{c\Delta T},\quad T_f=T_i+\frac{Q}{mc}.$$

The relation assumes no phase change and approximately constant c. Heat capacity $C = mc$ has units $\frac{J}{K}$. Specific heat c has units $\frac{J}{\left( \mathrm{kg} \cdot K \right)}$. With variable c, $Q = m \int c \left( T \right) dT$. The PDFs use $1\,\mathrm{cal} = 4.186\,\mathrm{J}$ and $c_{\mathrm{water}} = 4186\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg}   \mathrm{K}}$. Use the supplied convention consistently. One kcal is $1000\,\mathrm{cal}$; the source uses $1\,\mathrm{Btu} \approx 252\,\mathrm{cal}$.

**Worked example — primary p. 32.** Water of mass $0.625\,\mathrm{kg}$ initially at 75°C loses $7.96 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{J}$. Use $Q = - 79{,}600\,\mathrm{J}$. $T_{f} = 75 - \frac{79{,}600}{\left( 0.625 \times 4186 \right)} \approx 44.575^{\circ} C$, about 44.6°C. A positive loss magnitude must acquire a negative sign in the system energy equation.

### Mixing: derive the common final temperature

For an insulated mixture without phase changes, $\sum mc \left( T_{f} - T_{i} \right) = 0$. Expand the sum and collect Tf:

$$T_f=\frac{\sum_i m_ic_iT_i}{\sum_i m_ic_i}.$$

Include a calorimeter as another heat-capacity term if its energy is not negligible. Without phase change or external heating, Tf lies between the initial temperatures. That is a useful sign check.

**Worked example — primary p. 33.** Iron: $0.065\,\mathrm{kg}$ at 525°C, $c = 444\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg}   \mathrm{K}}$. Water: $0.635\,\mathrm{kg}$ at 15°C, $c = 4186$. The weighted formula gives $T_{f} \approx 20.478^{\circ} C$, about 20.5°C. Neither the simple arithmetic average nor the mass-weighted average alone is correct when c differs.

### Latent heat and every stage of an ice-to-steam problem

During an equilibrium phase transition at the specified pressure, $Q = \pm \mathrm{mL}$. Melting and vaporization absorb heat; freezing and condensation release heat. Lf belongs to solid–liquid transitions. Lv belongs to liquid–gas transitions. Primary p. 38 incorrectly repeats melting/freezing labels for vaporization.

**Decision method.** Bring material to the phase boundary with $mc\Delta T$. Use mL for the transition. Then use the new phase's specific heat. Never apply a single water c across ice, liquid, and steam.

**Worked example — primary p. 41.** Convert $0.100\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice at −40°C to steam at 110°C. Use $c_{\mathrm{ice}} = 2090$, $c_{\mathrm{water}} = 4186$, $c_{\mathrm{steam}} = 2110\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg}   \mathrm{K}}$, $L_{f} = 3.35 \times 10^{5}$ and $L_{v} = 2.26 \times 10^{6}\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg}}$.

$$Q=0.1(2090)(40)+0.1(335000)+0.1(4186)(100)+0.1(2260000)+0.1(2110)(10)=311830\ \mathrm J.$$

The five contributions are 8360, 33,500, 41,860, 226,000, and $2110\,\mathrm{J}$. The result rounds to $3.12 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J}$. The latent-heat stage dominates here.

For ice mixed with warm water, compare the available cooling energy with $mL_f$ before assuming all ice melts. If the energy is insufficient, the final mixture can stay at 0°C with some ice remaining. If all ice melts, subtract the melting energy before solving the later temperature rise. The companion's milk-and-ice case uses this energy-budget logic.

**Worked extension — test the final phase first.** Mix $0.050\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice at 0°C with $0.200\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water at 30°C. Use $c_{\mathrm{water}} = 4186$ and $L_{f} = 335{,}000$ in SI units. The water can release $0.200 \left( 4186 \right) \left( 30 \right) = 25{,}116\,\mathrm{J}$ before reaching 0°C. Melting all the ice needs $0.050 \left( 335{,}000 \right) = 16{,}750\,\mathrm{J}$. All the ice therefore melts. The remaining $8366\,\mathrm{J}$ warms $0.250\,\mathrm{kg}$ of liquid water, giving $T_{f} = \frac{8366}{\left( 0.250 \times 4186 \right)} \approx 7.994^{\circ} C$.

Change only the ice mass to $0.100\,\mathrm{kg}$. Melting it all needs $33{,}500\,\mathrm{J}$, more than $25{,}116\,\mathrm{J}$. The final temperature stays 0°C. Melted mass: $\frac{25{,}116}{335{,}000} \approx 0.07497\,\mathrm{kg}$; about $0.02503\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice remains. Do not report a negative final liquid temperature from an equation that incorrectly assumes complete melting.

### First law and two sign conventions

The primary uses $\Delta U = Q - W_{\mathrm{by}}$. The companion uses $\Delta U = Q + W_{\mathrm{on}}$. They agree because $W_{\mathrm{on}} = - W_{\mathrm{by}}$. Q is positive into the system. A closed system exchanges energy but not matter; an isolated system exchanges neither.

**Worked example — primary p. 45.** $Q_{\mathrm{net}} = 40 - 25 = 15\,\mathrm{J}$. $W_{\mathrm{by,net}}=10-4=6\,\mathrm{J}$. $\Delta U = 15 - 6 = 9\,\mathrm{J}$. Adding the $4\,\mathrm{J}$ of compression to $W_{\mathrm{by}}$ would reverse its meaning.

**Companion example.** Heat input $1500\,\mathrm{J}$, heat loss $400\,\mathrm{J}$, expansion work $600\,\mathrm{J}$, and paddle-wheel work ON the gas $250\,\mathrm{J}$ give $\Delta U = 1500 - 400 - 600 + 250 = 750\,\mathrm{J}$.

**Extension — steady conduction.** Fourier's one-dimensional law gives heat rate $H = \frac{kA \left( T_{h} - T_{c} \right)}{L}$ for a uniform slab. Derive $Q = Ht$; rearrange $k = \frac{HL}{\left( A\Delta T \right)}$. For stacked slabs, thermal resistance is $\frac{L}{\left( kA \right)}$, so $H = \frac{\Delta T}{\sum R_{\mathrm{thermal}}}$. This is the conduction relation shown in the scanned companion, not an electrical resistance formula.
