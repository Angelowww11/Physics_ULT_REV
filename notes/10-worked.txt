### Wavelengths, refractive index, and the source's physical models

**Source context — primary pp. 4–11.** All electromagnetic frequencies travel at c in vacuum. Visible light forms only a small part of the spectrum. Newton's historical particle description and Huygens' wave description precede modern wave–particle behavior. Young's interference experiment supports wave behavior. Einstein's photoelectric explanation connects photon energy to frequency.

**Ray approximation.** Rays describe propagation directions in geometric optics. In a uniform medium they travel along straight lines. In a medium with a varying refractive index, they can curve. The approximation works when the wavelength is small relative to the relevant geometric features. A ray is not a literal thin material strand.

:::example Visible-light frequency range to wavelength range
**Source:** Primary p. 8. **Given:** f ranges from $4.0 \times 10^{14}$ to $7.9 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Use $c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}$.

1. Start with $c = f\lambda$ and isolate $\lambda = \frac{c}{f}$.
2. Use the lowest frequency for the longest wavelength: $\lambda _{\mathrm{max}} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^{8}}{\left( 4.0 \times 10^{14} \right)} = 7.50 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}$.
3. Convert meters to nanometers: $7.50 \times 10^{-7} \times 10^{9} = 750\,\mathrm{nm}$.
4. Use the highest frequency for the shortest wavelength: $\lambda _{\mathrm{min}} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^{8}}{\left( 7.9 \times 10^{14} \right)} \approx 3.79747 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} = 379.747\,\mathrm{nm}$.
5. Report about 380–$750\,\mathrm{nm}$ in increasing wavelength order.

**Check.** Increasing frequency decreases wavelength. Reversing the endpoint order without stating the inverse relation makes the interval misleading.
:::

{{figure:m10-main:20|The primary refractive-index table. These are approximate material values; use the values given in a specific problem.}}

The table gives air 1.0003, ice 1.31, water 1.33, alcohol 1.36, fused quartz 1.46, crown glass 1.52, and diamond 2.42. Refractive index depends on material conditions and wavelength. The later water–glass–air exercise explicitly uses 1.3, 1.5, and 1.0 instead. Use those stated exercise values.

:::derivation Frequency stays fixed while speed and wavelength change
At a stationary interface, the oscillation rate must match on both sides. Otherwise, the boundary phase cannot remain matched over time. Thus $f_{1} = f_{2}$. Use $v = f\lambda$ and $n = \frac{c}{v}$:

$$\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}=\frac{v_2}{v_1}=\frac{n_1}{n_2},
\qquad \lambda_{medium}=\frac{\lambda_0}{n}.$$

For a labeled example, $600\,\mathrm{nm}$ vacuum light entering $n = 1.50$ glass has $v = 2.00 \times 10^{8}\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}$ and $\lambda _{\mathrm{glass}} = 400\,\mathrm{nm}$. Its frequency remains $5.00 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$. The shorter in-glass wavelength does not mean the photon acquires a higher frequency at this stationary boundary.
:::

### Photoelectric equations with the unit conversions shown

:::example Photon energy, kinetic energy, and stopping voltage
**Source starting point:** Primary p. 10 introduces $E_{\gamma} = hf$. The work-function and stopping-voltage calculations are labeled extensions.

**Given:** $\lambda_{0} = 500\,\mathrm{nm}$, $\phi = 2.00\,\mathrm{eV}$, $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J} \cdot s$, $c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}$, and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

1. Convert $\lambda_{0} = 500 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m}$.
2. Calculate $E_{\gamma} = \frac{hc}{\lambda_{0}} = 3.9756 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.
3. Convert to electronvolts: $E_{\gamma} = \frac{3.9756}{1.602} = 2.48165\,\mathrm{eV}$.
4. Subtract the work function in the same unit: $K_{\mathrm{max}} = 2.48165 - 2.00 = 0.481648\,\mathrm{eV}$.
5. Divide Kmax in joules by e to obtain a stopping-potential magnitude of $0.481648\,\mathrm{V}$.
6. Use $\lambda thresh_{\mathrm{old}} = \frac{hc}{\phi} \approx 620.4\,\mathrm{nm}$ with these rounded constants.

**Check.** An energy of one electronvolt equals e times a potential difference of one volt. It is an energy unit, not a voltage unit. Here $\lambda_{0}$ is shorter than the threshold wavelength, so single-photon emission is energetically possible.
:::

:::derivation Threshold and stopping-potential rearrangements
Use conservation of energy for one emitted electron: $hf = \phi + K_{\mathrm{max}}$. At threshold, $K_{\mathrm{max}} = 0$, so $f_{0} = \frac{\phi}{h}$. With vacuum wavelength $\lambda_{0} = \frac{c}{f}$, the longest threshold wavelength is $\lambda thresh_{\mathrm{old}} = \frac{hc}{\phi}$.

For the stopping-potential magnitude, $eV_{\mathrm{stop}} = K_{\mathrm{max}}$. Therefore:

$$V_{stop}=\frac he f-\frac\phi e,
\qquad \phi=hf-eV_{stop}.$$

A Vstop-versus-frequency graph has slope $\frac{h}{e}$ and a zero-voltage intercept at $f_{0}$. At fixed frequency above threshold, ordinary intensity changes affect photon arrival rate rather than each photon's energy. Primary p. 10's frequency-independent kinetic-energy statement is incorrect.
:::

### Reflection and refraction: complete the source angle calculations

**Reflection geometry.** Measure the incident and reflected angles from the same surface normal. If the incident ray is 30° above a flat surface, its incidence angle is 60°. Its reflected ray is also 60° from the normal, or 30° from the surface. Diffuse reflection comes from different microscopic normal directions on a rough surface; it does not replace the local reflection law.

:::derivation Reflection from a stationary-path calculation
Let the endpoints lie at heights $h_{1}$ and $h_{2}$ above a horizontal mirror, separated horizontally by d. Let the reflection point have horizontal coordinate x. In the same uniform medium, minimizing travel time also makes the optical path stationary.

$$L(x)=\sqrt{x^2+h_1^2}+\sqrt{(d-x)^2+h_2^2}.$$

1. Differentiate: $\frac{dL}{dx} = \frac{x}{\sqrt{\left( x^{2} + h_{1}^{2} \right)}} - \frac{\left( d - x \right)}{\sqrt{\left( \left( d - x \right)^{2} + h_{2}^{2} \right)}}$.
2. Set $\frac{dL}{dx} = 0$.
3. Recognize the two ratios as $\sin θ_{i}$ and $\sin θ_{r}$, with angles from the normal.
4. Obtain $\sin θ_{i} = \sin θ_{r}$. For ordinary reflection angles between 0° and 90°, $θ_{i} = θ_{r}$.

This is a labeled derivation of the source's reflection law, not a new mirror-image equation.
:::

:::derivation Snell's law from stationary travel time
For endpoints in two uniform media separated by a flat interface, write:

$$t(x)=\frac{n_1}{c}\sqrt{x^2+h_1^2}
+\frac{n_2}{c}\sqrt{(d-x)^2+h_2^2}.$$

1. Differentiate with respect to the crossing position x.
2. Set $\frac{dt}{dx} = 0$ and multiply by c.
3. Replace the geometric ratios by the sines of the normal-based angles.

$$n_1\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+h_1^2}}
-n_2\frac{d-x}{\sqrt{(d-x)^2+h_2^2}}=0
\Rightarrow n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2.$$

The same law follows from matching tangential wavevector components. Both derivations assume stationary media and a stationary planar interface. Different speeds require the n-weighted travel time; minimizing the unweighted geometric length would give the wrong refraction law.
:::

:::example Air into water at 45°
**Source:** Primary p. 25. Use $n_{1} = 1.00$ for air and $n_{2} = 1.33$ for water.

1. Write $1.00 \sin 45^{\circ} = 1.33 \sin \theta_{2}$.
2. Divide by 1.33: $\sin \theta_{2} = 0.531659$.
3. Use inverse sine in degree mode: $\theta_{2} \approx 32.1176^{\circ}$.
4. Report about 32.1° from the normal.

**Check.** The ray enters the higher-index medium and bends toward the normal, so its normal-based angle decreases. Do not multiply the angle 45° directly by an index ratio.
:::

:::example Water, glass, and air: calculate all four requested angles
**Sources:** Primary pp. 26–27. **Given:** Water $n = 1.3$, glass $n = 1.5$, air $n = 1.0$, and initial incidence 20° from the normal.

1. Reflection in water gives $\theta_{A} = 20^{\circ}$.
2. Apply Snell at the first boundary: $1.3 \sin 20^{\circ} = 1.5 \sin \theta_{B}$.
3. Calculate $\theta_{B} = \sin^{-1} \left[ \left( \frac{1.3}{1.5} \right) \sin 20^{\circ} \right] \approx 17.2426^{\circ}$.
4. Use the parallel glass faces: $\theta_{C} = \theta_{B} \approx 17.2426^{\circ}$.
5. Apply Snell at the second boundary: $1.5 \sin \theta_{C} = 1.0 \sin \theta_{D}$.
6. Substitute the first-boundary relation to avoid early rounding: $\sin \theta_{D} = 1.3 \sin 20^{\circ}$.
7. Calculate $\theta_{D} \approx 26.3994^{\circ}$.

**Source correction.** The printed exit angle 20° is incorrect for the stated first and final indices. Parallel faces restore the original angle only when the first and final media have the same refractive index. Check each interface for total internal reflection before applying a transmitted-ray formula.
:::

### Total internal reflection, optical fibers, and useful inverse forms

:::derivation Critical angle and the domain test
**Sources:** Primary pp. 22 and 28–31. At the threshold, the refracted angle is 90°. Insert it into Snell's law:

$$n_1\sin\theta_c=n_2\sin90^\circ=n_2
\Rightarrow\theta_c=\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right).$$

1. Check $n_{1} > n_{2}$. Without a higher-to-lower index transition, there is no ordinary critical angle.
2. Calculate $θ_{c}$ from the normal.
3. Compare the actual incidence angle with $θ_{c}$.
4. Use refraction for $θ_{i} < θ_{c}$, a grazing transmitted ray at $θ_{i} = θ_{c}$, and total internal reflection for $θ_{i} > θ_{c}$.

If $\sin \theta_{2} = \left( \frac{n_{1}}{n_{2}} \right) \sin \theta_{1}$ exceeds one, no real propagating refracted ray satisfies this elementary model. Do not force the calculator to return a transmitted angle.
:::

:::example Glass–air and water–air thresholds
**Labeled numerical extensions of the source law.**

1. For $n_{1} = 1.50$ glass and $n_{2} = 1.00$ air, $θ_{c} = \sin^{-1} \left( \frac{1}{1.50} \right) = 41.8103^{\circ}$.
2. At $θ_{i} = 30^{\circ}$, $\sin \theta_{2} = 1.50 \sin 30^{\circ} = 0.75$, so $\theta_{2} \approx 48.5904^{\circ}$.
3. At $θ_{i} = 50^{\circ}$, the proposed transmitted sine is $1.14907 > 1$. Total internal reflection occurs.
4. For water $n = 1.33$ to air, $θ_{c} = \sin^{-1} \left( \frac{1}{1.33} \right) \approx 48.7535^{\circ}$.

**Check.** Reversing the direction to air → glass removes the total-internal-reflection condition. An “optically denser” medium means a larger refractive index here, not necessarily a larger mass density.
:::

**Fiber applications — primary pp. 30–31.** A higher-index core and lower-index cladding support total internal reflection for suitable rays. Fiber links carry information encoded in light. The source lists telephone and internet links, cable systems, medical imaging, and engineering inspection. Real fibers have losses from absorption, scattering, and bending. The cladding's role is optical confinement; it does not make every fiber perfectly lossless.

:::derivation Apparent depth at a flat surface: a labeled small-angle extension
Let an object lie a true depth h inside index $n_{1}$. An observer in index $n_{2}$ extends a refracted ray backward and sees apparent depth happ.

1. Draw a ray reaching the interface at horizontal offset x. Then $\tan \theta_{1} = \frac{x}{h}$ and $\tan \theta_{2} = x$/happ.
2. For small normal-based angles, use $\sin \theta \approx \tan \theta \approx \theta$ in radians.
3. Insert the geometric ratios into Snell's law: $\frac{n_{1} x}{h} \approx n_{2} x$/happ.
4. Cancel x and solve happ ≈ $h \left( \frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}} \right)$.

For a $1.00\,\mathrm{m}$ deep object in water $n_{1} = 1.33$ viewed from air $n_{2} = 1.00$, happ ≈ $0.752\,\mathrm{m}$. The small-angle restriction matters. This extension does not replace exact ray tracing at a large viewing angle.
:::
