### Work signs, coordinate choices, and the battery model

The companion'$s U = qEs$ uses a position coordinate s measured opposite to its leftward field. It is not a direction-free rule. With +x along a uniform field, $V \left( x \right) = V \left( 0 \right) - E_{x}$ and $U \left( x \right) = U \left( 0 \right) - qE_{x}$. The reference constant does not affect a force or energy difference.

| Displacement | Charge sign | $\Delta V$ | $\Delta U = q\Delta V$ | $W_{\mathrm{field}} = - \Delta U$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Along E | Positive | Negative | Negative | Positive |
| Along E | Negative | Negative | Positive | Negative |
| Against E | Positive | Positive | Positive | Negative |
| Against E | Negative | Positive | Negative | Positive |

The table describes the electrostatic work during a specified displacement. It does not say that every particle initially moves in its force direction. A particle can initially move against its force and slow down.

**Battery model — primary pp. 30–31; companion p. 6.** Chemical processes move charge through the source against electrostatic forces. The supplied energy per positive charge is the emf, $\mathcal{E} = \frac{W_{\mathrm{chemical}}}{q}$. An ideal DC battery maintains a fixed terminal voltage. A real battery's terminal voltage depends on current and internal resistance. A generator can produce a changing voltage, so “all electrical sources supply a constant potential difference” is too broad.

:::example The companion proton problem, with the actual slide values
**Source:** Companion p. 3. **Given:** $q = + 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$, $m = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $E = 1.0 \times 10^{3}\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$, $d = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}$, and $v_{i} = 0$.

1. Identify parallel force and displacement. $W_{\mathrm{field}} = qEd$ is positive.
2. Calculate $W_{\mathrm{field}} = \left( 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \right) \left( 1.0 \times 10^{3} \right) \left( 0.20 \right) = 3.20 \times 10^{-17}\,\mathrm{J}$.
3. Set the work equal to the kinetic-energy gain: $\tfrac{1}{2} mv_{f}^{2} = W_{\mathrm{field}}$.
4. Isolate and calculate the final speed.

$$v_f=\sqrt{\frac{2W_{field}}m}
=\sqrt{\frac{6.40\times10^{-17}}{1.67\times10^{-27}}}
=1.95765\times10^5\ \mathrm{m/s}.$$

5. Check the potential difference: $\Delta V = - Ed = - 200\,\mathrm{V}$.
6. Check the energy independently: $\Delta U = q\Delta V = - 3.20 \times 10^{-17}\,\mathrm{J}$, so $\Delta K = - \Delta U$.

**Correction.** The earlier notes used $10^{5}\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$. The supplied companion uses $10^{3}\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$. This solution now matches the slide and its $1.96 \times 10^{5}\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}$ answer.
:::

### Complete the symbolic charge-assembly examples

{{figure:m5-main:17|The source geometry for bringing a third charge from infinity. Keep the first two sources fixed.}}

:::derivation External work to bring +e to $x = 2 a$
**Source:** Primary p. 17. Fixed charges are −e at $x = 0$ and +e at $x = a$. Bring a third +e from infinity to $x = 2 a$ quasistatically.

1. Find its final distances from the sources: 2a and a.
2. Add the existing-source potentials at the destination: $V_{\mathrm{existing}} = k \left( - \frac{e}{\left( 2 a \right)} + \frac{e}{a} \right)$.
3. Simplify to $V_{\mathrm{existing}} = \frac{ke}{\left( 2 a \right)}$.
4. Multiply by the added charge and use $V_{\mathrm{existing}} \left( \infty \right) = 0$.

$$W_{external}=q_3\Delta V_{existing}
=e\left(\frac{ke}{2a}-0\right)
=\frac{ke^2}{2a}.$$

The electric field does the opposite work, $- \frac{ke^{2}}{\left( 2 a \right)}$. Do not include the unchanged interaction energy between the first two charges in this work difference.
:::

:::derivation Total energy of the final three-charge configuration
**Source:** Primary p. 25. Use the same final positions: −e at 0, +e at a, and +e at 2a.

1. List each unique pair once: (1,2), (1,3), and (2,3).
2. Use distances a, 2a, and a respectively.
3. Retain each product's sign before adding.

$$\begin{aligned}
U_{12}&=-\frac{ke^2}{a},&
U_{13}&=-\frac{ke^2}{2a},&
U_{23}&=+\frac{ke^2}{a},\\
U_{total}&=U_{12}+U_{13}+U_{23}
=-\frac{ke^2}{2a}.
\end{aligned}$$

**Check the distinction.** Initially, the two fixed charges store $- \frac{ke^{2}}{a}$. Adding the third charge changes that energy by $+ \frac{ke^{2}}{\left( 2 a \right)}$. The final total remains negative, $- \frac{ke^{2}}{\left( 2 a \right)}$. Total configuration energy and work for one assembly stage answer different questions.
:::

:::example Three numerical charges: show every pair contribution
**Source:** Primary p. 26. **Given:** $+ 4\,\mathrm{nC}$ at $x = 0$, $- 3\,\mathrm{nC}$ at $x = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}$, and $+ 2\,\mathrm{nC}$ at $x = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}$. Use $k = 9 \times 10^{9}$.

| Pair | Distance | Pair energy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $+ 4\,\mathrm{nC}$ and $- 3\,\mathrm{nC}$ | $0.20\,\mathrm{m}$ | $- 5.40 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{J}$ |
| $+ 4\,\mathrm{nC}$ and $+ 2\,\mathrm{nC}$ | $0.10\,\mathrm{m}$ | $+ 7.20 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{J}$ |
| $- 3\,\mathrm{nC}$ and $+ 2\,\mathrm{nC}$ | $0.10\,\mathrm{m}$ | $- 5.40 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{J}$ |

1. Use $U_{ij} = \frac{kq_{i} q_{j}}{r_{ij}}$ for each row. The denominator is distance, not distance squared.
2. Add the signed energies: $U_{\mathrm{total}} = \left( - 5.40 + 7.20 - 5.40 \right) \times 10^{-7} = - 3.60 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{J}$.
3. Check that no pair appears twice and no self-interaction appears.

**Interpretation.** Separating all charges to infinity quasistatically requires a net external work of $+ 3.60 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{J}$, with the chosen zero-energy reference.
:::

### Complete the companion potential and dipole problems

:::example Opposite charges and the meaning of negative potential energy
**Source:** Companion p. 4. **Given:** $q_{1} = + 2.0\,\mathrm{\mu C}$, $q_{2} = - 3.0\,\mathrm{\mu C}$, $r = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}$. Use $k = 9 \times 10^{9}$.

1. Multiply the signed charges: $q_{1} q_{2} = - 6.0 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C}^{2}$.
2. Calculate $U = \frac{kq_{1} q_{2}}{r} = - 0.540\,\mathrm{J}$.
3. Use $U \left( \infty \right) = 0$. Increasing the separation to infinity changes U by $+ 0.540\,\mathrm{J}$.

**Check.** Opposite charges attract. Slow separation therefore requires positive external work even though the initial energy is negative.
:::

:::example Potentials at two distances from the same source
**Source:** Companion p. 6. **Given:** $Q = + 3.0\,\mathrm{\mu C}$, $r_{A} = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}$, $r_{B} = 0.60\,\mathrm{m}$. Use $k = 9 \times 10^{9}$.

1. Calculate $V_{A} = \frac{kQ}{r_{A}} = 90{,}000\,\mathrm{V}$.
2. Calculate $V_{B} = \frac{kQ}{r_{B}} = 45{,}000\,\mathrm{V}$.
3. Apply the source's requested order: $V_{AB} = V_{A} - V_{B} = + 45{,}000\,\mathrm{V}$.
4. For a move A → B, use $\Delta V = V_{B} - V_{A} = - 45{,}000\,\mathrm{V}$ instead.

**Check.** Doubling the distance halves this positive point-source potential. Endpoint order determines the sign of a potential difference.
:::

:::example The two omitted single-charge potential calculations
**Primary p. 33:** $Q = 2.50 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{C}$; $r = 1.75\,\mathrm{m}$. With $k = 9 \times 10^{9}$, $V = \frac{kQ}{r} = \frac{0.225}{1.75} = 0.128571\,\mathrm{V}$, about $0.129\,\mathrm{V}$.

**Companion p. 5:** $Q = + 2.0\,\mathrm{\mu C}$; $r = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}$. $V = \frac{\left( 9 \times 10^{9} \right) \left( 2.0 \times 10^{-6} \right)}{0.30} = 60{,}000\,\mathrm{V}$.

**Check.** Both calculations use $V \propto \frac{1}{r}$. An inverse-square denominator would calculate a field magnitude instead of a potential.
:::

{{figure:m5-dyn:7|The companion dipole problem: a lies between the charges; b is to the left; c has equal 13 cm source distances.}}

:::example Dipole potential at a, b, and c
**Source:** Companion p. 7. **Given:** $+ 12\,\mathrm{nC}$ on the left and $- 12\,\mathrm{nC}$ on the right, separated by $0.10\,\mathrm{m}$. Use $k = 9 \times 10^{9}$ and $V \left( \infty \right) = 0$.

1. Use the signed scalar sum $V = kq \left( \frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{positive}}} - \frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{negative}}} \right)$.
2. At a, the distances are $0.060\,\mathrm{m}$ from the positive charge and $0.040\,\mathrm{m}$ from the negative charge.
3. At b, the distances are $0.040\,\mathrm{m}$ from the positive charge and $0.140\,\mathrm{m}$ from the negative charge.
4. At c, both distances are $0.130\,\mathrm{m}$.

$$\begin{aligned}
V_a&=108\left(\frac1{0.060}-\frac1{0.040}\right)=-900\ \mathrm V,\\
V_b&=108\left(\frac1{0.040}-\frac1{0.140}\right)=1928.57\ \mathrm V,\\
V_c&=108\left(\frac1{0.130}-\frac1{0.130}\right)=0.
\end{aligned}$$

**Check.** Point a is closer to the negative charge, so its potential is negative. Point b is closer to the positive charge, so its potential is positive. Zero potential at c does not imply zero electric field.
:::

:::derivation Derive why equipotentials are perpendicular to E
For an infinitesimal displacement dl along an equipotential, $dV = 0$. The electrostatic relation gives $dV = - E \cdot dl$. Therefore $E \cdot dl = 0$ for every tangent displacement. Where E is nonzero, E has no tangent component and must point normal to the surface.

For closely spaced equal-voltage contours, $\left| E \right| \approx \frac{\left| \Delta V \right|}{\Delta s_{\mathrm{normal}}}$. Smaller normal spacing means a stronger field. Use perpendicular spacing, not an arbitrary diagonal separation between two contour points.
:::
