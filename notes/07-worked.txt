### Finish the current, resistivity, and resistor-identification material

**Current carriers.** The primary's “flow of electrons” definition describes a metal, but current more generally means net charge flow. Positive and negative ions can also carry current. A closed circuit provides a continuous conducting path. An open branch carries no steady conduction current in this model. A short circuit provides a low-resistance path; an actual source's internal resistance still limits its current.

**Circuit symbols — primary p. 37.** The longer battery line marks the positive terminal. An A in a circle denotes an ammeter in the measured branch. A V in a circle denotes a voltmeter connected across two nodes. A connection dot identifies joined wires. Label the nodes before deciding whether two components share a voltage.

**Current and the body — primary p. 15.** The source table associates increasing current with sensation, muscle effects, disrupted breathing, and heart effects. Its ranges are illustrative, not universal thresholds. Effects also depend on the current path and contact duration. [OSHA's electrical-safety material](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/OSHA3075.pdf) explains these dependencies. The original table remains available in the source reader.

:::derivation Drift, collision time, and microscopic Ohm's law
**Source starting point:** Primary p. 7. The additional algebra uses a simple relaxation-time model.

1. Between collisions, the carrier acceleration is $a = \frac{qE}{m}$.
2. The model's mean drift velocity is $v_{d} = \frac{qE\tau _{\mathrm{collision}}}{m}$.
3. Insert vd into $J = nqv_{d}$.
4. Compare the result with $J = \sigma E$.

$$\mathbf J=\frac{nq^2\tau_{collision}}m\mathbf E,
\qquad \sigma=\frac{nq^2\tau_{collision}}m,
\qquad \rho=\frac{m}{nq^2\tau_{collision}}.$$

The squared charge makes $\sigma$ positive for either carrier sign. For electrons, vd opposes E while J points along E. This is a microscopic model for suitable materials; it is not an exact conductivity theory for every substance.
:::

:::example All three primary current calculations
**Source:** Primary p. 17.

1. For $29.3\,\mathrm{C}$ passing in $7.84\,\mathrm{s}$, $I_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{Q}{\Delta t} = \frac{29.3}{7.84} = 3.73724\,\mathrm{A}$, about $3.74\,\mathrm{A}$.
2. For a constant calculator current of $0.010\,\mathrm{A}$ for $60\,\mathrm{s}$, $Q = I\Delta t = 0.600\,\mathrm{C}$.
3. For $15\,\mathrm{C}$ passing in $3\,\mathrm{s}$, $I_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{15}{3} = 5\,\mathrm{A}$.

**Check.** Coulombs divided by seconds give amperes. These are average values unless the flow is constant. For a varying current, use $Q = \int I \left( t \right) dt$.
:::

:::example Carrier concentration with signed vectors
**Source:** Primary p. 16. **Given:** $J_{x} = + 50.0\,\frac{\mathrm{A}}{\mathrm{m}^{2}}$, $q = - 0.10\,\mathrm{C}$, and $v_{dx} = - 2.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}$.

1. Use $J_{x} = nqv_{dx}$, keeping both negative signs.
2. Calculate $qv_{dx} = \left( - 0.10 \right) \left( - 2.0 \times 10^{-4} \right) = + \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{C} \cdot m}{s}$.
3. Divide $n = \frac{50.0}{\left( 2.0 \times 10^{-5} \right)} = 2.5 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}^{-3}$.

**Check.** A carrier count per volume must be positive. The supplied $- 0.10\,\mathrm{C}$ is a hypothetical carrier charge, not an electron's charge.
:::

{{figure:m7-main:21|The primary resistivity and temperature-coefficient table. Values depend on material composition and the reference temperature.}}

The companion also lists conductors, semiconductors, and insulators. Metals generally have low resistivity. Insulators have much higher resistivity. Semiconductor resistivity strongly depends on temperature and impurities. Its graph shows that superconducting resistivity becomes zero below a material-dependent critical temperature under suitable conditions. Do not extrapolate the room-temperature linear resistance model through that transition.

:::example Copper resistivity at 70°C
**Source:** Companion pp. 3–4. **Given:** $\rho_{0} = 1.68 \times 10^{-8} \Omega \cdot m$ at 20°C; $\alpha = 0.0039^{\circ} C^{-1}$.

1. Find $\Delta T = 70 - 20 = 50^{\circ} C$.
2. Calculate the dimensionless correction: $1 + \alpha \Delta T = 1 + 0.0039 \left( 50 \right) = 1.195$.
3. Calculate $\rho = \rho_{0} \left( 1 + \alpha \Delta T \right) = 2.0076 \times 10^{-8} \Omega \cdot m$.
4. Round to the source's $2.01 \times 10^{-8} \Omega \cdot m$.

**Check.** Copper's positive $\alpha$ predicts increased resistivity when heated. Use the supplied $1.68 \times 10^{-8}$ value rather than substituting a different table value halfway through.
:::

{{figure:m7-main:23|The source shows a six-band resistor. Its band roles differ from a four-band resistor.}}

**Four, five, and six bands.** Four bands use two significant digits, a multiplier, and tolerance. Five bands use three significant digits, a multiplier, and tolerance. Six bands add a temperature coefficient, commonly expressed in parts per million per kelvin. The source's pictured six-band resistor is green–blue–black–red–gold–yellow.

:::example Read the pictured six-band resistor
**Source:** Primary p. 23.

1. Read the first three digits: green 5, blue 6, black 0. The significant number is 560.
2. Apply the red multiplier, $10^{2}$: $R_{\mathrm{nominal}} = 560 \left( 100 \right) = 56{,}000 \Omega = 56 k\Omega$.
3. Apply the gold tolerance, ±5%: $\Delta R = 0.05 \left( 56{,}000 \right) = 2800 \Omega$.
4. Find the tolerance interval: 53.2 kΩ to 58.8 kΩ.
5. Read the yellow temperature coefficient as $\frac{25\,\mathrm{ppm}}{K}$ in the displayed code convention.
6. For a $20\,\mathrm{K}$ change, the fractional temperature shift is $25 \times 10^{-6} \left( 20 \right) = 0.0005$, or 0.05%.

**Check.** Manufacturing tolerance and temperature drift describe different effects. Do not treat the sixth band as another multiplier or another significant digit.
:::

### Complete the primary Ohm's-law and power exercises

:::example Three missing Ohm's-law answers
**Sources:** Primary pp. 30–32. Assume the stated resistance remains constant.

| Problem | Equation choice and conversion | Solution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 250 Ω bulb at $110\,\mathrm{V}$ | $I = \frac{V}{R}$ | $I = \frac{110}{250} = 0.440\,\mathrm{A}$ |
| Toaster at $220\,\mathrm{V}$ carrying $0.50\,\mathrm{A}$ | $R = \frac{V}{I}$ | $R = \frac{220}{0.50} = 440 \Omega$ |
| 1200 Ω resistor carrying $30\,\mathrm{mA}$ | $I = 0.030\,\mathrm{A}$; $V = IR$ | $V = 0.030 \left( 1200 \right) = 36\,\mathrm{V}$ |

**Check.** Convert milliamperes before using ohms. A physical incandescent bulb changes resistance as it heats; the source exercises use an assigned steady resistance.
:::

:::example Power and energy: choose the form that uses the given values
**Sources:** Primary pp. 39–40; companion pp. 6–7.

1. For $R = 120 \Omega$ and $I = 0.9\,\mathrm{A}$, use $P = I^{2} R = 0.9^{2} \left( 120 \right) = 97.2\,\mathrm{W}$.
2. For $P = 30\,\mathrm{W}$ and $V = 110\,\mathrm{V}$, use $I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{30}{110} = 0.272727\,\mathrm{A}$.
3. For the companion heater, use $R = 20 \Omega$ and $V = 120\,\mathrm{V}$: $I = 6\,\mathrm{A}$ and $P = VI = 720\,\mathrm{W}$.
4. Convert 15 minutes to $900\,\mathrm{s}$, then calculate $E_{\mathrm{energy}} = Pt = 720 \left( 900 \right) = 648{,}000\,\mathrm{J}$.
5. Convert independently: $0.720\,\mathrm{kW} \times 0.25 h = 0.18\,\mathrm{kWh}$.
6. Raise the heater resistance to 30 Ω at fixed $120\,\mathrm{V}$. The new power is $\frac{V^{2}}{R} = 480\,\mathrm{W}$.

**Source correction.** Primary p. 40 labels its $30\,\mathrm{W}$ given value as R. Watts measure power, so the symbol must be P. The resistor formulas $P = I^{2} R$ and $\frac{V^{2}}{R}$ require the resistor's voltage and current.
:::

:::example Real battery: current, terminal voltage, and the complete power budget
**Sources:** Primary pp. 43–44; companion p. 6. **Given:** $\mathcal{E} = 12\,\mathrm{V}$, internal resistance $r = 2 \Omega$, and external load $R = 4 \Omega$.

1. Add the series resistances: $R_{\mathrm{total}} = R + r = 6 \Omega$.
2. Calculate $I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{\left( R + r \right)} = \frac{12}{6} = 2\,\mathrm{A}$.
3. Calculate terminal voltage: $V_{\mathrm{terminal}} = \mathcal{E} - Ir = 12 - 2 \left( 2 \right) = 8\,\mathrm{V}$.
4. Check the load voltage: $IR = 2 \left( 4 \right) = 8\,\mathrm{V}$.
5. Calculate chemical-to-electrical conversion rate: $P_{\mathrm{source}} = \mathcal{E} I = 24\,\mathrm{W}$.
6. Calculate internal heating: $P_{\mathrm{internal}} = I^{2} r = 8\,\mathrm{W}$.
7. Calculate useful load power: $P_{\mathrm{load}} = I^{2} R = 16\,\mathrm{W}$.

**Check.** $24\,\mathrm{W} = 8\,\mathrm{W} + 16\,\mathrm{W}$. The ideal voltmeter reads $8\,\mathrm{V}$; the ideal series ammeter reads $2\,\mathrm{A}$.

For charging at a specified current entering the positive terminal, the required terminal voltage is $\mathcal{E} + Ir$. The incoming power splits into chemical storage $\mathcal{E} I$ and internal heating $I^{2} r$.
:::

### Recover every series, parallel, and mixed-network result

:::example The primary 18 kΩ circuit, including its unprinted branch results
**Source:** Primary p. 53. **Given:** 3 kΩ, 10 kΩ, and 5 kΩ in series across $9\,\mathrm{V}$.

1. Add the resistances: $R_{\mathrm{eq}} = 18 k\Omega$.
2. Calculate $I = \frac{9}{18{,}000} = 0.000500\,\mathrm{A} = 0.500\,\mathrm{mA}$.
3. Calculate voltage drops: $1.50\,\mathrm{V}$ across 3 kΩ, $5.00\,\mathrm{V}$ across 10 kΩ, and $2.50\,\mathrm{V}$ across 5 kΩ.
4. Check their sum: $1.50 + 5.00 + 2.50 = 9.00\,\mathrm{V}$.

**Check.** Every series resistor carries $0.500\,\mathrm{mA}$. The largest resistance receives the largest voltage drop.
:::

:::example Primary 2 Ω, 3 Ω, and 5 Ω network at $50\,\mathrm{V}$
**Sources:** Primary pp. 54–56.

**Series.** $R_{\mathrm{eq}} = 2 + 3 + 5 = 10 \Omega$. The common current is $\frac{50}{10} = 5\,\mathrm{A}$. The voltage drops are $10\,\mathrm{V}$, $15\,\mathrm{V}$, and $25\,\mathrm{V}$. Their sum is $50\,\mathrm{V}$. Total power is $250\,\mathrm{W}$.

**Parallel.** Every branch has $50\,\mathrm{V}$. Calculate each current separately: $I_{2} \Omega = 25\,\mathrm{A}$, $I_{3} \Omega = \frac{50}{3} A$, and $I_{5} \Omega = 10\,\mathrm{A}$. Their sum is $\frac{155}{3} A$, about $51.6667\,\mathrm{A}$.

$$\frac1{R_{eq}}=\frac12+\frac13+\frac15=\frac{31}{30},
\qquad R_{eq}=\frac{30}{31}\ \Omega\approx0.967742\ \Omega.$$

**Check.** The parallel equivalent is smaller than 2 Ω. It draws more total current than the series connection at the same source voltage.
:::

### Kirchhoff examples: derive the signs and solve both supplied networks

**Source correction — primary p. 66.** Different independent loops do not have to use the same traversal direction. Each loop can use either direction. Keep the signs consistent within that traversal. Reversing one complete loop equation multiplies it by −1 without changing the physical solution.

**Independent equations.** Repeating the junction equation at both ends of the same branches does not create new information. The outer loop is a combination of the two inner loops in the source example. Use enough independent equations for the unknown branch currents.

{{figure:m7-main:69|The primary single-loop circuit. A negative current reverses the displayed clockwise arrow.}}

:::example Primary single loop with opposing $6\,\mathrm{V}$ and $12\,\mathrm{V}$ batteries
**Source:** Primary p. 69. Assume clockwise current, as drawn. The resistors are 8 Ω and 10 Ω.

1. Traverse the top source from negative to positive: $+ 6\,\mathrm{V}$.
2. Traverse the 8 Ω resistor with the assumed current: −8I.
3. Traverse the bottom source from positive to negative: $- 12\,\mathrm{V}$.
4. Traverse the 10 Ω resistor with the assumed current: −10I.
5. Set the sum to zero: $6 - 8 I - 12 - 10 I = 0$.
6. Solve $- 6 - 18 I = 0$, giving $I = - \frac{1}{3} A$.

**Interpretation.** The actual current is $0.3333\,\mathrm{A}$ counterclockwise. The negative answer corrects the initial arrow; it does not invalidate Kirchhoff's rules.
:::

{{figure:m7-dyn:15|The separate companion two-loop network. Its component values differ from the primary network.}}

:::example Companion two-loop circuit: full elimination and voltage recovery
**Sources:** Companion pp. 14–16. **Given arrows:** $I_{1}$ rises through 2 Ω, $I_{2}$ rises through the 1 Ω and 3 Ω branch, and $I_{3}$ falls through 5 Ω. Sources are $16\,\mathrm{V}$ and $6\,\mathrm{V}$.

1. Write KCL at the upper node: $I_{3} = I_{1} + I_{2}$.
2. Write the left loop: $16 - 2 I_{1} - 5 I_{3} = 0$.
3. Write the right loop: $5 I_{3} + I_{2} + 3 I_{2} - 6 = 0$.
4. Substitute $I_{3} = I_{1} + I_{2}$ and collect terms.

$$\begin{aligned}
7I_1+5I_2&=16,\\
5I_1+9I_2&=6.
\end{aligned}$$

5. Multiply the first equation by 5 and the second by 7. Subtract the second from the first.

$$\begin{aligned}
35I_1+25I_2&=80,\\
35I_1+63I_2&=42,\\
-38I_2&=38\Rightarrow I_2=-1\ \mathrm A.
\end{aligned}$$

6. Back-substitute: $7 I_{1} + 5 \left( - 1 \right) = 16$, so $I_{1} = 3\,\mathrm{A}$.
7. Recover $I_{3} = 3 + \left( - 1 \right) = 2\,\mathrm{A}$ and $I_{4} = I_{2} = - 1\,\mathrm{A}$.
8. Calculate voltage-drop magnitudes: $6\,\mathrm{V}$ across 2 Ω; $1\,\mathrm{V}$ across 1 Ω; $10\,\mathrm{V}$ across 5 Ω; $3\,\mathrm{V}$ across 3 Ω.

**Checks.** Left loop: $16 - 6 - 10 = 0$. Right equation: $10 - 1 - 3 - 6 = 0$. $I_{2}$ and $I_{4}$ actually flow downward. Source power also balances: the $16\,\mathrm{V}$ source supplies $48\,\mathrm{W}$, the $6\,\mathrm{V}$ source absorbs $6\,\mathrm{W}$, and resistors dissipate $42\,\mathrm{W}$.
:::

### RC circuits: complete logarithms, limits, and switching checks

:::derivation Discharge from the differential equation to the time formula
**Sources:** Primary pp. 77–79. Let q be the charge remaining on the initially positive plate. The discharge-current magnitude is $- \frac{dq}{dt}$.

1. Use the loop equation $R \left( \frac{dq}{dt} \right) + \frac{q}{C} = 0$ under this charge convention.
2. Separate variables: $\frac{dq}{q} = - \frac{dt}{\left( RC \right)}$.
3. Integrate from $Q_{0}$ to q(t) and from 0 to t.
4. Exponentiate, divide by C for voltage, then isolate time when needed.

$$\ln\frac{q}{Q_0}=-\frac{t}{RC}
\Rightarrow q=Q_0e^{-t/RC},
\qquad V_C=V_0e^{-t/RC},
\qquad t=RC\ln\frac{V_0}{V_C}.$$

The logarithm uses a dimensionless ratio. For a decay fraction $f = \frac{V_{C}}{V_{0}}$, use $0 < f \le 1$. Reaching exactly zero takes infinite time in the ideal exponential model.
:::

:::example The source's $35\,\mathrm{\mu F}$, 120 Ω discharge problem
**Source:** Primary p. 79. **Find:** Time to fall to 10% of the initial voltage.

1. Convert $C = 35 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{F}$.
2. Calculate $\tau = RC = 120 \left( 35 \times 10^{-6} \right) = 0.00420\,\mathrm{s}$.
3. Set $\frac{V_{C}}{V_{0}} = 0.10$, not 10.
4. Take logarithms: $\ln \left( 0.10 \right) = - \frac{t}{0.00420}$.
5. Solve $t = - 0.00420 \ln \left( 0.10 \right) = 0.00967086\,\mathrm{s} = 9.67\,\mathrm{ms}$.

**Check.** Ten percent remaining requires about 2.303 time constants. The initial voltage cancels because the question specifies a fraction.
:::

**Initial and final conditions.** An initially uncharged capacitor has $V_{C} \left( 0 \right) = 0$ and initially behaves as a zero-voltage element in the charging circuit. At DC steady state, its current tends to zero. A precharged capacitor is not initially a zero-voltage element. Use $V_{C} \left( t \right) = V_{f} + \left( V_{\mathrm{initial}} - V_{f} \right) \exp \left( - \frac{t}{\tau} \right)$ when the initial voltage is nonzero.

**Timing applications.** Wipers, traffic-light timers, camera flashes, and pacemaker timing circuits use charging or discharging intervals. The elementary RC model explains a time scale; it does not represent every control function inside those devices.
