### Finish the current, resistivity, and resistor-identification material

**Current carriers.** The primary's “flow of electrons” definition describes a metal, but current more generally means net charge flow. Positive and negative ions can also carry current. A closed circuit provides a continuous conducting path. An open branch carries no steady conduction current in this model. A short circuit provides a low-resistance path; an actual source's internal resistance still limits its current.

**Circuit symbols — primary p. 37.** The longer battery line marks the positive terminal. An A in a circle denotes an ammeter in the measured branch. A V in a circle denotes a voltmeter connected across two nodes. A connection dot identifies joined wires. Label the nodes before deciding whether two components share a voltage.

**Current and the body — primary p. 15.** The source table associates increasing current with sensation, muscle effects, disrupted breathing, and heart effects. Its ranges are illustrative, not universal thresholds. Effects also depend on the current path and contact duration. [OSHA's electrical-safety material](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/OSHA3075.pdf) explains these dependencies. The original table remains available in the source reader.

:::derivation Drift, collision time, and microscopic Ohm's law
**Source starting point:** Primary p. 7. The additional algebra uses a simple relaxation-time model.

1. Between collisions, the carrier acceleration is a = qE/m.
2. The model's mean drift velocity is vd = qEτcollision/m.
3. Insert vd into J = nqvd.
4. Compare the result with J = σE.

$$\mathbf J=\frac{nq^2\tau_{collision}}m\mathbf E,
\qquad \sigma=\frac{nq^2\tau_{collision}}m,
\qquad \rho=\frac{m}{nq^2\tau_{collision}}.$$

The squared charge makes σ positive for either carrier sign. For electrons, vd opposes E while J points along E. This is a microscopic model for suitable materials; it is not an exact conductivity theory for every substance.
:::

:::example All three primary current calculations
**Source:** Primary p. 17.

1. For 29.3 C passing in 7.84 s, Iavg = Q/Δt = 29.3/7.84 = 3.73724 A, about 3.74 A.
2. For a constant calculator current of 0.010 A for 60 s, Q = IΔt = 0.600 C.
3. For 15 C passing in 3 s, Iavg = 15/3 = 5 A.

**Check.** Coulombs divided by seconds give amperes. These are average values unless the flow is constant. For a varying current, use Q = ∫I(t)dt.
:::

:::example Carrier concentration with signed vectors
**Source:** Primary p. 16. **Given:** Jx = +50.0 A/m², q = −0.10 C, and vdx = −2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m/s.

1. Use Jx = nqvdx, keeping both negative signs.
2. Calculate qvdx = (−0.10)(−2.0 × 10⁻⁴) = +2.0 × 10⁻⁵ C·m/s.
3. Divide n = 50.0/(2.0 × 10⁻⁵) = 2.5 × 10⁶ m⁻³.

**Check.** A carrier count per volume must be positive. The supplied −0.10 C is a hypothetical carrier charge, not an electron's charge.
:::

{{figure:m7-main:21|The primary resistivity and temperature-coefficient table. Values depend on material composition and the reference temperature.}}

The companion also lists conductors, semiconductors, and insulators. Metals generally have low resistivity. Insulators have much higher resistivity. Semiconductor resistivity strongly depends on temperature and impurities. Its graph shows that superconducting resistivity becomes zero below a material-dependent critical temperature under suitable conditions. Do not extrapolate the room-temperature linear resistance model through that transition.

:::example Copper resistivity at 70°C
**Source:** Companion pp. 3–4. **Given:** ρ₀ = 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m at 20°C; α = 0.0039°C⁻¹.

1. Find ΔT = 70−20 = 50°C.
2. Calculate the dimensionless correction: 1+αΔT = 1+0.0039(50) = 1.195.
3. Calculate ρ = ρ₀(1+αΔT) = 2.0076 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m.
4. Round to the source's 2.01 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m.

**Check.** Copper's positive α predicts increased resistivity when heated. Use the supplied 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ value rather than substituting a different table value halfway through.
:::

{{figure:m7-main:23|The source shows a six-band resistor. Its band roles differ from a four-band resistor.}}

**Four, five, and six bands.** Four bands use two significant digits, a multiplier, and tolerance. Five bands use three significant digits, a multiplier, and tolerance. Six bands add a temperature coefficient, commonly expressed in parts per million per kelvin. The source's pictured six-band resistor is green–blue–black–red–gold–yellow.

:::example Read the pictured six-band resistor
**Source:** Primary p. 23.

1. Read the first three digits: green 5, blue 6, black 0. The significant number is 560.
2. Apply the red multiplier, 10²: Rnominal = 560(100) = 56,000 Ω = 56 kΩ.
3. Apply the gold tolerance, ±5%: ΔR = 0.05(56,000) = 2800 Ω.
4. Find the tolerance interval: 53.2 kΩ to 58.8 kΩ.
5. Read the yellow temperature coefficient as 25 ppm/K in the displayed code convention.
6. For a 20 K change, the fractional temperature shift is 25 × 10⁻⁶(20) = 0.0005, or 0.05%.

**Check.** Manufacturing tolerance and temperature drift describe different effects. Do not treat the sixth band as another multiplier or another significant digit.
:::

**Check.** Convert milliamperes before using ohms. A physical incandescent bulb changes resistance as it heats; the source exercises use an assigned steady resistance.
:::

:::example Power and energy: choose the form that uses the given values
**Sources:** Primary pp. 39–40; companion pp. 6–7.

1. For R = 120 Ω and I = 0.9 A, use P = I²R = 0.9²(120) = 97.2 W.
2. For P = 30 W and V = 110 V, use I = P/V = 30/110 = 0.272727 A.
3. For the companion heater, use R = 20 Ω and V = 120 V: I = 6 A and P = VI = 720 W.
4. Convert 15 minutes to 900 s, then calculate Eenergy = Pt = 720(900) = 648,000 J.
5. Convert independently: 0.720 kW × 0.25 h = 0.18 kWh.
6. Raise the heater resistance to 30 Ω at fixed 120 V. The new power is V²/R = 480 W.

**Source correction.** Primary p. 40 labels its 30 W given value as R. Watts measure power, so the symbol must be P. The resistor formulas P = I²R and V²/R require the resistor's voltage and current.
:::

:::example Real battery: current, terminal voltage, and the complete power budget
**Sources:** Primary pp. 43–44; companion p. 6. **Given:** ℰ = 12 V, internal resistance r = 2 Ω, and external load R = 4 Ω.

1. Add the series resistances: Rtotal = R+r = 6 Ω.
2. Calculate I = ℰ/(R+r) = 12/6 = 2 A.
3. Calculate terminal voltage: Vterminal = ℰ−Ir = 12−2(2) = 8 V.
4. Check the load voltage: IR = 2(4) = 8 V.
5. Calculate chemical-to-electrical conversion rate: Psource = ℰI = 24 W.
6. Calculate internal heating: Pinternal = I²r = 8 W.
7. Calculate useful load power: Pload = I²R = 16 W.

**Check.** 24 W = 8 W + 16 W. The ideal voltmeter reads 8 V; the ideal series ammeter reads 2 A.

For charging at a specified current entering the positive terminal, the required terminal voltage is ℰ+Ir. The incoming power splits into chemical storage ℰI and internal heating I²r.
:::

### Recover every series, parallel, and mixed-network result

:::example The primary 18 kΩ circuit, including its unprinted branch results
**Source:** Primary p. 53. **Given:** 3 kΩ, 10 kΩ, and 5 kΩ in series across 9 V.

1. Add the resistances: Req = 18 kΩ.
2. Calculate I = 9/18,000 = 0.000500 A = 0.500 mA.
3. Calculate voltage drops: 1.50 V across 3 kΩ, 5.00 V across 10 kΩ, and 2.50 V across 5 kΩ.
4. Check their sum: 1.50+5.00+2.50 = 9.00 V.

**Check.** Every series resistor carries 0.500 mA. The largest resistance receives the largest voltage drop.
:::

:::example Primary 2 Ω, 3 Ω, and 5 Ω network at 50 V
**Sources:** Primary pp. 54–56.

**Series.** Req = 2+3+5 = 10 Ω. The common current is 50/10 = 5 A. The voltage drops are 10 V, 15 V, and 25 V. Their sum is 50 V. Total power is 250 W.

**Parallel.** Every branch has 50 V. Calculate each current separately: I₂Ω = 25 A, I₃Ω = 50/3 A, and I₅Ω = 10 A. Their sum is 155/3 A, about 51.6667 A.

$$\frac1{R_{eq}}=\frac12+\frac13+\frac15=\frac{31}{30},
\qquad R_{eq}=\frac{30}{31}\ \Omega\approx0.967742\ \Omega.$$

**Check.** The parallel equivalent is smaller than 2 Ω. It draws more total current than the series connection at the same source voltage.

:::example Companion mixed resistor network
**Sources:** Companion pp. 10–11. **Given topology:** 90 Ω in parallel with 45 Ω; that group in series with 10 Ω; that branch in parallel with 25 Ω.

1. Reduce the inner parallel group: R₂₃ = (90×45)/(90+45) = 30 Ω.
2. Add its series resistor: R₁₂₃ = 10+30 = 40 Ω.
3. Combine the two outer branches: Req = (40×25)/(40+25) = 200/13 Ω ≈ 15.3846 Ω.
4. Check that the final equivalent is smaller than either outer parallel branch, 25 Ω and 40 Ω.

**Reason for the order.** The 10 Ω resistor shares the inner group's branch current. It does not share both terminal nodes with either 90 Ω or 45 Ω.

### Kirchhoff examples: derive the signs and solve both supplied networks

**Source correction — primary p. 66.** Different independent loops do not have to use the same traversal direction. Each loop can use either direction. Keep the signs consistent within that traversal. Reversing one complete loop equation multiplies it by −1 without changing the physical solution.

**Independent equations.** Repeating the junction equation at both ends of the same branches does not create new information. The outer loop is a combination of the two inner loops in the source example. Use enough independent equations for the unknown branch currents.

{{figure:m7-main:69|The primary single-loop circuit. A negative current reverses the displayed clockwise arrow.}}

:::example Primary single loop with opposing 6 V and 12 V batteries
**Source:** Primary p. 69. Assume clockwise current, as drawn. The resistors are 8 Ω and 10 Ω.

1. Traverse the top source from negative to positive: +6 V.
2. Traverse the 8 Ω resistor with the assumed current: −8I.
3. Traverse the bottom source from positive to negative: −12 V.
4. Traverse the 10 Ω resistor with the assumed current: −10I.
5. Set the sum to zero: 6−8I−12−10I = 0.
6. Solve −6−18I = 0, giving I = −1/3 A.

**Interpretation.** The actual current is 0.3333 A counterclockwise. The negative answer corrects the initial arrow; it does not invalidate Kirchhoff's rules.
:::

{{figure:m7-dyn:15|The separate companion two-loop network. Its component values differ from the primary network.}}

:::example Companion two-loop circuit: full elimination and voltage recovery
**Sources:** Companion pp. 14–16. **Given arrows:** I₁ rises through 2 Ω, I₂ rises through the 1 Ω and 3 Ω branch, and I₃ falls through 5 Ω. Sources are 16 V and 6 V.

1. Write KCL at the upper node: I₃ = I₁+I₂.
2. Write the left loop: 16−2I₁−5I₃ = 0.
3. Write the right loop: 5I₃+I₂+3I₂−6 = 0.
4. Substitute I₃ = I₁+I₂ and collect terms.

$$\begin{aligned}
7I_1+5I_2&=16,\\
5I_1+9I_2&=6.
\end{aligned}$$

5. Multiply the first equation by 5 and the second by 7. Subtract the second from the first.

$$\begin{aligned}
35I_1+25I_2&=80,\\
35I_1+63I_2&=42,\\
-38I_2&=38\Rightarrow I_2=-1\ \mathrm A.
\end{aligned}$$

6. Back-substitute: 7I₁+5(−1) = 16, so I₁ = 3 A.
7. Recover I₃ = 3+(−1) = 2 A and I₄ = I₂ = −1 A.
8. Calculate voltage-drop magnitudes: 6 V across 2 Ω; 1 V across 1 Ω; 10 V across 5 Ω; 3 V across 3 Ω.

**Checks.** Left loop: 16−6−10 = 0. Right equation: 10−1−3−6 = 0. I₂ and I₄ actually flow downward. Source power also balances: the 16 V source supplies 48 W, the 6 V source absorbs 6 W, and resistors dissipate 42 W.
:::

### RC circuits: complete logarithms, limits, and switching checks

:::derivation Discharge from the differential equation to the time formula
**Sources:** Primary pp. 77–79. Let q be the charge remaining on the initially positive plate. The discharge-current magnitude is −dq/dt.

1. Use the loop equation R(dq/dt)+q/C = 0 under this charge convention.
2. Separate variables: dq/q = −dt/(RC).
3. Integrate from Q₀ to q(t) and from 0 to t.
4. Exponentiate, divide by C for voltage, then isolate time when needed.

$$\ln\frac{q}{Q_0}=-\frac{t}{RC}
\Rightarrow q=Q_0e^{-t/RC},
\qquad V_C=V_0e^{-t/RC},
\qquad t=RC\ln\frac{V_0}{V_C}.$$

The logarithm uses a dimensionless ratio. For a decay fraction f = VC/V₀, use 0 < f ≤ 1. Reaching exactly zero takes infinite time in the ideal exponential model.
:::

:::example The source's 35 μF, 120 Ω discharge problem
**Source:** Primary p. 79. **Find:** Time to fall to 10% of the initial voltage.

1. Convert C = 35 × 10⁻⁶ F.
2. Calculate τ = RC = 120(35 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.00420 s.
3. Set VC/V₀ = 0.10, not 10.
4. Take logarithms: ln(0.10) = −t/0.00420.
5. Solve t = −0.00420 ln(0.10) = 0.00967086 s = 9.67 ms.

**Check.** Ten percent remaining requires about 2.303 time constants. The initial voltage cancels because the question specifies a fraction.
:::

**Initial and final conditions.** An initially uncharged capacitor has VC(0) = 0 and initially behaves as a zero-voltage element in the charging circuit. At DC steady state, its current tends to zero. A precharged capacitor is not initially a zero-voltage element. Use VC(t) = Vf+(Vinitial−Vf)e⁻ᵗᐟτ when the initial voltage is nonzero.

**Timing applications.** Wipers, traffic-light timers, camera flashes, and pacemaker timing circuits use charging or discharging intervals. The elementary RC model explains a time scale; it does not represent every control function inside those devices.
