### Worked applications: models, calculations, and interpretation

These worked examples apply the module concepts. Each example states its conditions, explains the model, and shows the complete working.

<section class="worked-application">

<h4 id="worked-m5h1">Worked application 1: Zero potential with a nonzero field</h4>

At the midpoint of an equal positive–negative charge pair, choose zero potential at infinity.

The midpoint illustrates zero potential with a nonzero electric field for an equal positive–negative charge pair.

1. **Add signed scalar potentials.**

   $$V=\frac{kq}{r}+\frac{k(-q)}r=0$$

2. **Add the vector fields.** Both midpoint fields point from the positive source toward the negative source.

3. **Relate field to a spatial change, not a value.**

   $$\vec E=-\nabla V$$

4. **State the distinction.** A zero potential value is compatible with a nonzero local slope and field.

**Result:** $V=0$, but $\vec E\ne0$.

</section>

<section class="worked-application">

<h4 id="worked-m5h2">Worked application 2: A negative charge moves to higher potential</h4>

A negative charge moves from lower to higher electric potential with only the electrostatic force doing work.

Energy conservation determines the kinetic-energy change as the negative charge moves to higher potential.

1. **Keep the charge sign.**

   $$q<0,\quad\Delta V>0$$

2. **Find potential-energy change.**

   $$\Delta U=q\Delta V<0$$

3. **Use energy conservation.**

   $$\Delta K=-\Delta U>0$$

4. **Check the force model.** This conclusion uses the statement that no other force does work.

**Result:** It increases.

</section>

<section class="worked-application">

<h4 id="worked-m5h3">Worked application 3: Quasistatic external work</h4>

An external agent moves a charge slowly between two points in an electrostatic field, with negligible kinetic-energy change.

The work balance relates external work, electric work, and potential-energy change during slow motion.

1. **Write the work–energy relation.**

   $$\Delta K=W_{\mathrm{ext}}+W_{\mathrm{field}}\approx0$$

2. **Use the conservative-field relation.**

   $$W_{\mathrm{field}}=-\Delta U$$

3. **Combine.**

   $$W_{\mathrm{ext}}=\Delta U$$

4. **Check the sign.** The external agent can extract energy when the required external work is negative.

**Result:** $W_{\mathrm{ext}}=\Delta U=-W_{\mathrm{field}}$.

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m5h4">Worked application 4: Changing the potential reference</h4>

The same constant is added to every electric-potential value in a region.

A common shift of the potential reference leaves measurable fields and endpoint work unchanged.

1. **Compare endpoint differences.**

   $$(V_b+C)-(V_a+C)=V_b-V_a$$

2. **Compare fields.**

   $$-\nabla(V+C)=-\nabla V$$

3. **Interpret the reference.** Individual energy or potential zero choices can shift without changing endpoint work or the field.

4. **Check the physical result.** Changing the zero of potential does not change any measured potential difference.

**Result:** Electric fields and work for moves between fixed endpoints.

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m5h5">Worked application 5: Assembling three source charges</h4>

A system contains three distinct point charges. A student adds a pair-energy contribution for both ordered pairs (1,2) and (2,1).

The pair-energy sum counts each distinct pair once, without counting both ordered versions.

1. **List the distinct pairs.**

   $$U=k\left(\frac{q_1q_2}{r_{12}}+\frac{q_1q_3}{r_{13}}+\frac{q_2q_3}{r_{23}}\right)$$

2. **Retain signs.** Opposite-sign pairs contribute negative terms with the usual infinity reference.

3. **Check the count.** Three charges have three distinct pairs, not six.

4. **Check the count.** A three-charge system has three unordered pairs, not six.

**Result:** Count each distinct unordered pair once.

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m5h6">Worked application 6: Electron energy from a voltage rise</h4>

An electron starts from rest and moves to a point 400 V higher in potential, with only the electrostatic force doing work. Use a nonrelativistic model, $|e|=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$, and $m_e=9.11\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$. The calculation determines its final speed.

1. **Find the signed potential-energy change.**

   $$\Delta U=q\Delta V=(-e)(400)<0$$

2. **Convert the energy decrease to kinetic energy.**

   $$\frac12m_ev^2=e(400)$$

3. **Solve for speed.**

   $$v=\sqrt{\frac{2e(400)}{m_e}}$$

4. **Check the approximation.** The result is much smaller than c, so the stated nonrelativistic model is consistent.

5. **State the result.**

   $$1.18609\times10^{7}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$$

**Result:** $1.18609\times10^{7}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m5h7">Worked application 7: Potential and a negative test charge</h4>

Fixed sources +3.0 nC and −2.0 nC are respectively 0.20 m and 0.10 m from a point. Place a −4.0 μC test charge there without changing the sources. With zero potential at infinity, the calculation determines its potential energy. Use $k=8.99\times10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}$.

1. **Add signed source potentials.**

   $$V=k\left(\frac{3.0\times10^{-9}}{0.20}-\frac{2.0\times10^{-9}}{0.10}\right)=-44.95\,\mathrm V$$

2. **Multiply by the signed test charge.**

   $$U=qV=(-4.0\times10^{-6})(-44.95)$$

3. **Interpret the positive result.** Both the potential and test charge are negative, so their product is positive under the chosen reference.

4. **State the result.**

   $$1.798\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm J$$

**Result:** $1.798\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm J$

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m5h8">Worked application 8: Signed energy of a triangular assembly</h4>

Three charges +2.0 nC, +2.0 nC, and −2.0 nC occupy the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side 0.10 m. The calculation determines their total interaction potential energy relative to infinite separation. Use $k=8.99\times10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}$.

1. **Write the three distinct pairs.**

   $$U=\frac ka(q^2-q^2-q^2)$$

2. **Combine the signs.**

   $$U=-\frac{kq^2}{a}$$

3. **Convert nanocoulombs before evaluating.**

   $$U=-\frac{(8.99\times10^9)(2.0\times10^{-9})^2}{0.10}$$

4. **Interpret the sign.** The assembly has lower interaction energy than the separated charges under this reference.

5. **State the result.**

   $$-3.596\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm J$$

**Result:** $-3.596\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm J$

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m5h9">Worked application 9: Field work during radial motion</h4>

A +2.0 μC test charge moves from 0.40 m to 0.20 m from a fixed −3.0 nC source. The calculation determines the work done by the electric field. Use $k=8.99\times10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}$ and ignore changes to the source.

1. **Write the two potentials.**

   $$V_a=kQ/r_a,\qquad V_b=kQ/r_b$$

2. **Use the field-work sign.**

   $$W_{\mathrm{field}}=q(V_a-V_b)=qkQ\left(\frac1{r_a}-\frac1{r_b}\right)$$

3. **Substitute all signed quantities.**

   $$W_{\mathrm{field}}=(2.0\times10^{-6})(8.99\times10^9)(-3.0\times10^{-9})\left(\frac1{0.40}-\frac1{0.20}\right)$$

4. **Check the physics.** Attraction assists the positive charge moving closer, so field work is positive.

5. **State the result.**

   $$1.3485\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm J$$

**Result:** $1.3485\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm J$

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m5h10">Worked application 10: External work with a two-dimensional displacement</h4>

A uniform electrostatic field has $E_x=300\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$ and $E_y=400\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$. A −2.0 μC charge is moved quasistatically by $\Delta x=+0.20\,\mathrm m$ and $\Delta y=-0.10\,\mathrm m$. The calculation determines external work, with negligible kinetic-energy change.

1. **Calculate the field dot displacement.**

   $$\vec E\cdot\Delta\vec r=300(0.20)+400(-0.10)=20\,\mathrm V$$

2. **Find potential difference.**

   $$\Delta V=-\vec E\cdot\Delta\vec r=-20\,\mathrm V$$

3. **Find potential-energy change.**

   $$\Delta U=q\Delta V=(-2.0\times10^{-6})(-20)$$

4. **Use the quasistatic external-work relation.** The external agent adds energy while opposing the field’s negative work.

   $$W_{\mathrm{ext}}=\Delta U$$

5. **State the result.**

   $$4\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm J$$

**Result:** $4\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm J$

</section>

<h4 id="solution-m5h11">Worked application 11: Potential from unequal opposite sources · the potential at the origin</h4>

Fixed charges $+Q$ and $-2Q$ lie at $x=-a$ and $x=+a$. Use $Q=2\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm C$, $a=0.06\,\mathrm m$, and zero potential at infinity. Use $k=8.99\times10^9$ in SI units. An ideal test particle has charge $+1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$ and mass $1.673\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}$. For the speed question, consider a permitted path from rest at infinity to the origin with no other work. The calculation determines the potential at the origin.

1. **Identify the model and the conditions.** Electric potential adds as a signed scalar. Use charge times potential for test-particle potential energy, and conserve mechanical energy for speed.

2. **Add signed scalar contributions at the origin.**

   $$V(0)=kQ/a-2kQ/a=-kQ/a=-299.667\,\mathrm V$$

3. **Use the actual distances at x equal to a/2.**

   $$V(a/2)=\frac{kQ}{3a/2}-\frac{2kQ}{a/2}=-998.889\,\mathrm V$$

4. **Relate potential energy and kinetic energy.**

   $$U=q_tV,\quad K_f=-q_tV(0)$$

5. **Solve the requested quantity.**

   $$V(0)=-kQ/a=-299.667\,\mathrm V$$

6. **Check the physical result.** The negative source has twice the magnitude at the same distance.

7. **State the result.**

   $$-299.667\,\mathrm V$$

**Result:** $-299.667\,\mathrm V$

</section>

<h4 id="solution-m5h21">Worked application 21: Work in a two-dimensional uniform field · the signed endpoint potential change</h4>

A uniform electrostatic field is $\vec E=(120,-80)\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$. A particle of charge $-2.0\,\mu\mathrm C$ and mass $2.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{kg}$ moves through $\Delta\vec r=(0.3,0.4)\,\mathrm m$. Its initial speed is 15 m/s. Only the electric force does work. Use the given endpoint displacement, not its path length. The calculation determines the signed endpoint potential change.

1. **Identify the model and the conditions.** Use the dot product for field work. Potential change is minus field dotted with displacement, and kinetic-energy change is field work.

2. **Calculate the dot product.**

   $$\vec E\cdot\Delta\vec r=120(0.3)-80(0.4)=4\,\mathrm V$$

3. **Keep all energy signs.**

   $$\Delta V=-\vec E\cdot\Delta\vec r,\quad\Delta U=q\Delta V,\quad W_E=-\Delta U$$

4. **Use the energy-speed relation.**

   $$v_f^2=v_0^2+2W_E/m$$

5. **Solve the requested quantity.**

   $$\Delta V=-4=-4\,\mathrm V$$

6. **Check the physical result.** Both Cartesian contributions enter the dot product with their stated signs.

7. **State the result.**

   $$-4\,\mathrm V$$

**Result:** $-4\,\mathrm V$

</section>

   $$E_x=-\partial V/\partial x=150,\quad E_y=-\partial V/\partial y=-80\quad(\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}})$$

3. **Evaluate the endpoint difference.**

   $$\Delta V=(-150)(0.20)+(80)(0.10)=-22\,\mathrm V$$

   $$W_E=-q\Delta V=4.4\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm J,\quad v_f^2=v_i^2+2W_E/m$$

5. **Solve the requested quantity.**

   $$E_x=-(-150)=150\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$$

6. **Check the physical result.** Potential decreases as x increases, so the x field points in the positive x direction.

7. **State the result.**

   $$150\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$$

   $$W_E=-q\Delta V=5.2\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm J,\quad v_f^2=v_i^2+2W_E/m$$

5. **Apply the conclusion.** The gradient and potential differences do not depend on a constant reference offset.

**Result:** The field, endpoint work, and predicted speed remain unchanged.
