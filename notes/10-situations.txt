### Worked applications: models, calculations, and interpretation

These worked examples apply the module concepts. Each example states its conditions, explains the model, and shows the complete working.

<section class="worked-application">

<h4 id="worked-m10h1">Worked application 1: Constant frequency during refraction</h4>

Monochromatic light crosses a stationary boundary from air into ordinary transparent glass.

The stationary-boundary model distinguishes unchanged frequency and photon energy from changed speed and wavelength.

1. **Use the wave relation.**

   $$v=f\lambda$$

2. **Use the refractive-index definition.**

   $$v=c/n$$

3. **Keep frequency fixed at the stationary boundary.**

   $$\lambda_2/\lambda_1=v_2/v_1=n_1/n_2$$

4. **Check photon energy.**

   $$E_\gamma=hf$$

**Result:** Frequency and photon energy.

</section>

<section class="worked-application">

<h4 id="worked-m10h2">Worked application 2: The angle convention in Snell’s law</h4>

A ray in air makes an angle of 20° with a flat glass surface.

The angle measured from the glass surface converts to the incident angle measured from the normal.

1. **Draw the normal.** Draw a line perpendicular to the interface.

2. **Convert the angle.**

   $$\theta_i=90^\circ-20^\circ=70^\circ$$

3. **Use the normal-based law.**

   $$n_1\sin\theta_i=n_2\sin\theta_t$$

4. **Keep a consistent convention.** Measure reflected and transmitted angles from their interface normals too.

**Result:** $70^\circ$

</section>

<section class="worked-application">

<h4 id="worked-m10h3">Worked application 3: At and above the critical angle</h4>

A ray travels from a higher-index medium into a lower-index medium.

The critical-angle condition determines the refracted ray direction in the ideal ray model.

1. **Start from Snell’s law.**

   $$n_1\sin\theta_i=n_2\sin\theta_t$$

2. **Set the limiting transmitted angle.**

   $$\theta_t=90^\circ$$

3. **Solve the critical angle.**

   $$\sin\theta_c=n_2/n_1\quad(n_1>n_2)$$

4. **Check the boundary case.** Above this incident angle, Snell’s law gives no propagating transmitted ray.

**Result:** The refracted ray runs along the boundary at 90° to the normal.

</section>

<section class="worked-application">

<h4 id="worked-m10h4">Worked application 4: Parallel layers and a missing exit ray</h4>

Light crosses parallel transparent layers. You calculate the final angle using $n_i\sin\theta_i=n_f\sin\theta_f$ and obtain $\sin\theta_f>1$.

The transmission condition explains a calculated final sine greater than one across parallel layers.

1. **Keep the parallel-boundary invariant.**

   $$n\sin\theta=\text{constant}$$

2. **Check each interface.** A real transmitted angle requires the sine magnitude to be at most one.

3. **Test the high-to-low limit.**

   $$\theta_i>\sin^{-1}(n_2/n_1)\ \Rightarrow\ \text{total internal reflection}$$

4. **State the limitation.** The direct initial-to-final formula does not guarantee that an exit ray exists.

**Result:** There is no transmitted exit ray through that limiting interface; check total internal reflection.

</section>

<section class="worked-application">

<h4 id="worked-m10h5">Worked application 5: Intensity below the photoelectric threshold</h4>

In the ordinary one-photon photoelectric model, monochromatic light has photon energy below a metal’s work function. Its intensity doubles while frequency stays fixed.

The one-photon energy threshold explains the result of doubling intensity at fixed, below-threshold frequency.

1. **Calculate energy per photon.**

   $$E_\gamma=hf$$

2. **Apply the threshold condition.**

   $$hf<\phi$$

3. **Separate intensity and frequency.** At fixed frequency, increased intensity increases photon arrival rate.

4. **Apply the stated model.** The photons still lack the energy needed to release an electron. Multiphoton processes are outside this question.

**Result:** No photoemission occurs in this model.

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m10h6">Worked application 6: Wavelength inside water</h4>

Light with vacuum wavelength 400 nm enters water with refractive index 4/3. The boundary is stationary. The calculation determines its wavelength in water.

1. **Keep the frequency fixed.**

   $$f=c/\lambda_0$$

2. **Use phase speed in the medium.**

   $$v=c/n$$

3. **Combine the relations.**

   $$\lambda=v/f=\lambda_0/n$$

4. **Substitute.**

   $$\lambda=400/(4/3)=300\,\mathrm{nm}$$

5. **Check energy.** The frequency and photon energy stay the same across this stationary boundary.

6. **State the result.**

   $$300\,\mathrm{nm}$$

**Result:** $300\,\mathrm{nm}$

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m10h7">Worked application 7: Refraction from a surface angle</h4>

A ray in air ($n=1.00$) strikes glass ($n=1.50$) at 30° to the surface. The calculation determines the transmitted angle to the normal.

1. **Convert the incident angle.**

   $$\theta_1=90^\circ-30^\circ=60^\circ$$

2. **Apply Snell’s law.**

   $$(1.00)\sin60^\circ=(1.50)\sin\theta_2$$

3. **Solve the sine first.**

   $$\sin\theta_2=\sin60^\circ/1.50=0.577350$$

4. **Take the inverse sine.**

   $$\theta_2=\sin^{-1}(0.577350)$$

5. **Check the direction.** The transmitted angle is less than 60°, so the ray bends toward the normal in glass.

6. **State the result.**

   $$35.2644\,{}^\circ$$

**Result:** $35.2644\,{}^\circ$

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m10h8">Worked application 8: An exit ray through three media</h4>

A ray in water ($n=4/3$) crosses glass ($n=1.50$) and then air ($n=1.00$). Both boundaries are parallel. The water angle is 40° to the normal. The calculation determines the final air angle to the normal. Check that transmission is possible.

1. **Find the parallel-layer invariant.**

   $$n_w\sin\theta_w=\frac43\sin40^\circ=0.857050$$

2. **Check the glass angle.**

   $$\sin\theta_g=0.857050/1.50=0.571367$$

3. **Check the final interface.**

   $$\sin\theta_a=0.857050/1.00<1$$

4. **Calculate the exit angle.**

   $$\theta_a=\sin^{-1}(0.857050)$$

5. **Check total internal reflection.** The glass-to-air interface transmits here. A larger starting angle can prevent an exit ray.

6. **State the result.**

   $$58.987\,{}^\circ$$

**Result:** $58.987\,{}^\circ$

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m10h9">Worked application 9: Photoelectric stopping potential</h4>

Light of wavelength 350 nm strikes a metal with work function 2.20 eV. The calculation determines the stopping-potential magnitude in the ordinary one-photon model. Use $h=6.626\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$, $c=3.00\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, and $e=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$.

1. **Convert wavelength.**

   $$\lambda=350\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm m$$

2. **Calculate photon energy in electronvolts.**

   $$E_\gamma(\mathrm{eV})=\frac{hc}{\lambda e}=3.54521\,\mathrm{eV}$$

3. **Check threshold and subtract the work function.**

   $$K_{\max}=3.54521-2.20=1.34521\,\mathrm{eV}>0$$

4. **Use the stopping relation.**

   $$eV_{\mathrm{stop}}=K_{\max}$$

5. **Check the units.** An electron kinetic energy of 1.34521 eV corresponds to a stopping-potential magnitude of 1.34521 V.

6. **State the result.**

   $$1.34521\,\mathrm V$$

**Result:** $1.34521\,\mathrm V$

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m10h10">Worked application 10: the critical angle from wave speed</h4>

Light travels at $2.00\times10^{8}$ m/s inside a transparent medium. It approaches a boundary with air ($n=1.00$) from inside. Use vacuum $c=3.00\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The calculation determines the critical angle to the normal.

1. **Infer the refractive index.**

   $$n_1=c/v=(3.00\times10^8)/(2.00\times10^8)=1.50$$

2. **Check the high-to-low condition.**

   $$n_1=1.50>n_2=1.00$$

3. **Use the critical-angle relation.**

   $$\sin\theta_c=n_2/n_1=2/3$$

4. **Calculate the normal angle.**

   $$\theta_c=\sin^{-1}(2/3)$$

5. **Check the limiting ray.** At this angle, the transmitted ray is along the boundary. Larger incident angles give total internal reflection in the ideal ray model.

6. **State the result.**

   $$41.8103\,{}^\circ$$

**Result:** $41.8103\,{}^\circ$

</section>

<h4 id="solution-m10h11">Worked application 11: Critical angle and fiber acceptance · the core-to-cladding critical angle to the interface normal</h4>

An ideal straight step-index fiber has core index 1.48 and cladding index 1.44. Its flat entrance face is perpendicular to the fiber axis and lies in air of index 1.00. Consider meridional rays and vacuum wavelength $5.5\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m$. Use the boundary ray at the limit of total internal reflection to define the maximum acceptance angle to the axis. The calculation determines the core-to-cladding critical angle to the interface normal.

1. **Identify the model and the conditions.** Combine Snell’s law at the entrance with the critical-angle condition at the core–cladding interface. Keep the interface-normal and fiber-axis angles distinct.

2. **Find the core-to-cladding critical angle.**

   $$\theta_c=\sin^{-1}(n_{\rm clad}/n_{\rm core})=76.6488{}^\circ$$

3. **Relate the internal axial angle to the sidewall normal.**

   $$\theta_{\rm axis,max}=90^\circ-\theta_c$$

4. **Apply entrance refraction.**

   $$n_a\sin\theta_a=\sqrt{n_{\rm core}^2-n_{\rm clad}^2}\equiv\mathrm{NA}$$

5. **Solve the requested quantity.**

   $$\theta_c=\sin^{-1}(1.44/1.48)$$

6. **Check the physical result.** The critical angle is large because the core and cladding indices are close.

7. **State the result.**

   $$76.6488\,{}^\circ$$

**Result:** $76.6488\,{}^\circ$

</section>

<h4 id="solution-m10h21">Worked application 21: A ray through a parallel glass slab · the internal angle to the normal</h4>

A ray in air enters a parallel-sided glass slab of thickness 0.02 m and refractive index 1.5. Its incident angle is 35° to the normal. Both outside regions are air. Vacuum wavelength is $6\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m$. Ignore reflection loss and dispersion. Use $c=3.00\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The lateral displacement is the perpendicular separation of the emerging ray from the undeflected incident line. The calculation determines the internal angle to the normal.

1. **Identify the model and the conditions.** Refraction changes the internal path. Parallel faces restore the original external direction but can shift the ray sideways.

2. **Find the angle inside the slab.**

   $$\sin r=\sin i/n_g\ \Rightarrow\ r=22.4814{}^\circ$$

3. **Use the internal path length.**

   $$\ell=d/\cos r$$

4. **Resolve the ray offset geometrically.**

   $$\delta=\ell\sin(i-r)=d\sin(i-r)/\cos r$$

5. **Use the material phase speed.**

   $$t=\ell/(c/n_g)=n_gd/(c\cos r)$$

6. **Solve the requested quantity.**

   $$r=\sin^{-1}(\sin35^\circ/1.5)$$

7. **Check the physical result.** The glass angle is below the air incidence angle.

8. **State the result.**

   $$22.4814\,{}^\circ$$

**Result:** $22.4814\,{}^\circ$

</section>

   $$K_{\max,\rm eV}=E_\gamma/e-\phi_{\rm eV}=0.757387>0,\quad N_e=\eta N_{\rm abs}$$

   $$E_\gamma=hc/\lambda=4.41733\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J$$
