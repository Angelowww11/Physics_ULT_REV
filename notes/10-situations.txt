### Situational problems: choose the law, then solve

These added practice problems apply the module concepts. They are not copied examination questions. Keep the stated assumptions when you use each result.

#### Situation 1: What stays constant during refraction?

Monochromatic light crosses a stationary boundary from air into ordinary transparent glass. Which quantities remain unchanged across the boundary?

1. **Use the wave relation.**

   $$v=f\lambda$$

2. **Use the refractive-index definition.**

   $$v=c/n$$

3. **Keep frequency fixed at the stationary boundary.**

   $$\lambda_2/\lambda_1=v_2/v_1=n_1/n_2$$

4. **Check photon energy.**

   $$E_\gamma=hf$$

**Answer:** Frequency and photon energy.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **Speed and wavelength.** — Both usually decrease when light enters a higher-index medium.
- **Wavelength and photon energy.** — Wavelength changes because speed changes while frequency stays fixed.
- **Frequency and speed.** — The phase speed depends on the medium.

#### Situation 2: Which angle belongs in Snell’s law?

A ray in air makes an angle of 20° with a flat glass surface. Which incident angle belongs in Snell’s law?

1. **Draw the normal.** Draw a line perpendicular to the interface.

2. **Convert the angle.**

   $$\theta_i=90^\circ-20^\circ=70^\circ$$

3. **Use the normal-based law.**

   $$n_1\sin\theta_i=n_2\sin\theta_t$$

4. **Keep a consistent convention.** Measure reflected and transmitted angles from their interface normals too.

**Answer:** $70^\circ$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$20^\circ$** — This is the angle to the surface, not its normal.
- **$40^\circ$** — Doubling the surface angle does not give the incident angle.
- **$110^\circ$** — The incident angle is the acute angle to the appropriate normal.

#### Situation 3: At and above the critical angle

A ray travels from a higher-index medium into a lower-index medium. What happens at the critical angle, in the usual ideal ray model?

1. **Start from Snell’s law.**

   $$n_1\sin\theta_i=n_2\sin\theta_t$$

2. **Set the limiting transmitted angle.**

   $$\theta_t=90^\circ$$

3. **Solve the critical angle.**

   $$\sin\theta_c=n_2/n_1\quad(n_1>n_2)$$

4. **Check the boundary case.** Above this incident angle, Snell’s law gives no propagating transmitted ray.

**Answer:** The refracted ray runs along the boundary at 90° to the normal.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **The incident angle is 90°.** — The transmitted angle is 90° at the critical condition.
- **The ray bends to the normal.** — High-to-low refraction bends away from the normal.
- **The same critical angle applies from low index to high index.** — The high-to-low condition is necessary for this critical-angle relation.

#### Situation 4: Parallel layers and a missing exit ray

Light crosses parallel transparent layers. You calculate the final angle using $n_i\sin\theta_i=n_f\sin\theta_f$ and obtain $\sin\theta_f>1$. What is the correct interpretation?

1. **Keep the parallel-boundary invariant.**

   $$n\sin\theta=\text{constant}$$

2. **Check each interface.** A real transmitted angle requires the sine magnitude to be at most one.

3. **Test the high-to-low limit.**

   $$\theta_i>\sin^{-1}(n_2/n_1)\ \Rightarrow\ \text{total internal reflection}$$

4. **State the limitation.** The direct initial-to-final formula does not guarantee that an exit ray exists.

**Answer:** There is no transmitted exit ray through that limiting interface; check total internal reflection.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **Round the sine to one and report 90°.** — Clamping changes the physical conditions and hides total internal reflection.
- **Ignore the intermediate layers and always report an angle.** — The telescoped relation is conditional on transmission at each boundary.
- **Use the absolute value of the inverse-sine argument.** — The argument already exceeds its allowed magnitude.

#### Situation 5: Intensity below the photoelectric threshold

In the ordinary one-photon photoelectric model, monochromatic light has photon energy below a metal’s work function. Its intensity doubles while frequency stays fixed. What happens?

1. **Calculate energy per photon.**

   $$E_\gamma=hf$$

2. **Apply the threshold condition.**

   $$hf<\phi$$

3. **Separate intensity and frequency.** At fixed frequency, increased intensity increases photon arrival rate.

4. **Apply the stated model.** The photons still lack the energy needed to release an electron. Multiphoton processes are outside this question.

**Answer:** No photoemission occurs in this model.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **The maximum electron kinetic energy doubles.** — Maximum kinetic energy depends on photon frequency and work function.
- **Emission starts because total incident energy per second doubles.** — The one-photon threshold concerns the energy of an individual absorbed photon.
- **The work function becomes half as large.** — Intensity does not change the specified work function in this model.

#### Situation 6: Wavelength inside water

Light with vacuum wavelength 400 nm enters water with refractive index 4/3. The boundary is stationary. Find its wavelength in water.

1. **Keep the frequency fixed.**

   $$f=c/\lambda_0$$

2. **Use phase speed in the medium.**

   $$v=c/n$$

3. **Combine the relations.**

   $$\lambda=v/f=\lambda_0/n$$

4. **Substitute.**

   $$\lambda=400/(4/3)=300\,\mathrm{nm}$$

5. **Check energy.** The frequency and photon energy stay the same across this stationary boundary.

6. **State the result.**

   $$300\,\mathrm{nm}$$

**Answer:** $300\,\mathrm{nm}$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$400\,\mathrm{nm}$** — This keeps wavelength fixed although phase speed changes.
- **$533.333\,\mathrm{nm}$** — This multiplies by the refractive index instead of dividing.
- **$225\,\mathrm{nm}$** — This applies the index reduction twice.

#### Situation 7: Refraction from a surface angle

A ray in air ($n=1.00$) strikes glass ($n=1.50$) at 30° to the surface. Find the transmitted angle to the normal.

1. **Convert the incident angle.**

   $$\theta_1=90^\circ-30^\circ=60^\circ$$

2. **Apply Snell’s law.**

   $$(1.00)\sin60^\circ=(1.50)\sin\theta_2$$

3. **Solve the sine first.**

   $$\sin\theta_2=\sin60^\circ/1.50=0.577350$$

4. **Take the inverse sine.**

   $$\theta_2=\sin^{-1}(0.577350)$$

5. **Check the direction.** The transmitted angle is less than 60°, so the ray bends toward the normal in glass.

6. **State the result.**

   $$35.2644\,{}^\circ$$

**Answer:** $35.2644\,{}^\circ$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$19.4712\,{}^\circ$** — This treats the surface angle as the incident normal angle.
- **$60\,{}^\circ$** — This ignores refraction.
- **$54.7356\,{}^\circ$** — This gives the transmitted surface angle, not the requested normal angle.

#### Situation 8: An exit ray through three media

A ray in water ($n=4/3$) crosses glass ($n=1.50$) and then air ($n=1.00$). Both boundaries are parallel. The water angle is 40° to the normal. Find the final air angle to the normal. Check that transmission is possible.

1. **Find the parallel-layer invariant.**

   $$n_w\sin\theta_w=\frac43\sin40^\circ=0.857050$$

2. **Check the glass angle.**

   $$\sin\theta_g=0.857050/1.50=0.571367$$

3. **Check the final interface.**

   $$\sin\theta_a=0.857050/1.00<1$$

4. **Calculate the exit angle.**

   $$\theta_a=\sin^{-1}(0.857050)$$

5. **Check total internal reflection.** The glass-to-air interface transmits here. A larger starting angle can prevent an exit ray.

6. **State the result.**

   $$58.987\,{}^\circ$$

**Answer:** $58.987\,{}^\circ$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$40\,{}^\circ$** — This assumes equal starting and ending indices.
- **$34.8456\,{}^\circ$** — This is the intermediate glass angle.
- **$25.374\,{}^\circ$** — This ignores the initial water index and reports a glass-like angle.

#### Situation 9: Photoelectric stopping potential

Light of wavelength 350 nm strikes a metal with work function 2.20 eV. Find the stopping-potential magnitude in the ordinary one-photon model. Use $h=6.626\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$, $c=3.00\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, and $e=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$.

1. **Convert wavelength.**

   $$\lambda=350\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm m$$

2. **Calculate photon energy in electronvolts.**

   $$E_\gamma(\mathrm{eV})=\frac{hc}{\lambda e}=3.54521\,\mathrm{eV}$$

3. **Check threshold and subtract the work function.**

   $$K_{\max}=3.54521-2.20=1.34521\,\mathrm{eV}>0$$

4. **Use the stopping relation.**

   $$eV_{\mathrm{stop}}=K_{\max}$$

5. **Check the units.** An electron kinetic energy of 1.34521 eV corresponds to a stopping-potential magnitude of 1.34521 V.

6. **State the result.**

   $$1.34521\,\mathrm V$$

**Answer:** $1.34521\,\mathrm V$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$3.54521\,\mathrm V$** — This is photon energy in eV before subtracting the work function.
- **$2.2\,\mathrm V$** — This uses the work function alone.
- **$5.74521\,\mathrm V$** — This adds the work function instead of subtracting it.

#### Situation 10: Find the critical angle from wave speed

Light travels at $2.00\times10^{8}$ m/s inside a transparent medium. It approaches a boundary with air ($n=1.00$) from inside. Use vacuum $c=3.00\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Find the critical angle to the normal.

1. **Infer the refractive index.**

   $$n_1=c/v=(3.00\times10^8)/(2.00\times10^8)=1.50$$

2. **Check the high-to-low condition.**

   $$n_1=1.50>n_2=1.00$$

3. **Use the critical-angle relation.**

   $$\sin\theta_c=n_2/n_1=2/3$$

4. **Calculate the normal angle.**

   $$\theta_c=\sin^{-1}(2/3)$$

5. **Check the limiting ray.** At this angle, the transmitted ray is along the boundary. Larger incident angles give total internal reflection in the ideal ray model.

6. **State the result.**

   $$41.8103\,{}^\circ$$

**Answer:** $41.8103\,{}^\circ$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$48.1897\,{}^\circ$** — This is the complementary angle to the surface.
- **$26.3878\,{}^\circ$** — This squares the refractive index without justification.
- **$90\,{}^\circ$** — The transmitted angle, not the critical incident angle, is 90°.

<h4 id="solution-m10h11">Situation 11: Critical angle and fiber acceptance · Find the core-to-cladding critical angle to the interface normal</h4>

An ideal straight step-index fiber has core index 1.48 and cladding index 1.44. Its flat entrance face is perpendicular to the fiber axis and lies in air of index 1.00. Consider meridional rays and vacuum wavelength $5.5\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m$. Use the boundary ray at the limit of total internal reflection to define the maximum acceptance angle to the axis. Find the core-to-cladding critical angle to the interface normal.

1. **Identify the model and the conditions.** Combine Snell’s law at the entrance with the critical-angle condition at the core–cladding interface. Keep the interface-normal and fiber-axis angles distinct.

2. **Find the core-to-cladding critical angle.**

   $$\theta_c=\sin^{-1}(n_{\rm clad}/n_{\rm core})=76.6488{}^\circ$$

3. **Relate the internal axial angle to the sidewall normal.**

   $$\theta_{\rm axis,max}=90^\circ-\theta_c$$

4. **Apply entrance refraction.**

   $$n_a\sin\theta_a=\sqrt{n_{\rm core}^2-n_{\rm clad}^2}\equiv\mathrm{NA}$$

5. **Solve the requested quantity.**

   $$\theta_c=\sin^{-1}(1.44/1.48)$$

6. **Check the physical result.** The critical angle is large because the core and cladding indices are close.

7. **State the result.**

   $$76.6488\,{}^\circ$$

**Answer:** $76.6488\,{}^\circ$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$13.3512\,{}^\circ$** — This gives the complementary internal angle to the fiber axis.
- **$42.5066\,{}^\circ$** — This uses an air boundary instead of the core–cladding boundary.
- **$19.9841\,{}^\circ$** — This is the external acceptance angle rather than the internal critical angle.

<h4 id="solution-m10h13">Situation 13: Critical angle and fiber acceptance · Find the numerical aperture</h4>

An ideal straight step-index fiber has core index 1.48 and cladding index 1.44. Its flat entrance face is perpendicular to the fiber axis and lies in air of index 1.00. Consider meridional rays and vacuum wavelength $5.5\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m$. Use the boundary ray at the limit of total internal reflection to define the maximum acceptance angle to the axis. <h4 id="solution-m10h15">Situation 15: Critical angle and fiber acceptance · model check</h4>

An ideal straight step-index fiber has core index 1.48 and cladding index 1.44. Its flat entrance face is perpendicular to the fiber axis and lies in air of index 1.00. Consider meridional rays and vacuum wavelength $5.5\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m$. Use the boundary ray at the limit of total internal reflection to define the maximum acceptance angle to the axis. Why is the core index higher than the cladding index in this guidance model?

1. **Identify what stays fixed.** Combine Snell’s law at the entrance with the critical-angle condition at the core–cladding interface. Keep the interface-normal and fiber-axis angles distinct.

2. **Find the core-to-cladding critical angle.**

   $$\theta_c=\sin^{-1}(n_{\rm clad}/n_{\rm core})=76.6488{}^\circ$$

3. **Relate the internal axial angle to the sidewall normal.**

   $$\theta_{\rm axis,max}=90^\circ-\theta_c$$

4. **Apply entrance refraction.**

   $$n_a\sin\theta_a=\sqrt{n_{\rm core}^2-n_{\rm clad}^2}\equiv\mathrm{NA}$$

5. **Apply the conclusion.** The high-to-low condition permits a critical angle.

**Answer:** A ray must travel from higher to lower index at the sidewall for total internal reflection.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **Total internal reflection occurs only from lower to higher index.** — That reverses the required condition.

- **Guided rays must strike every interface at zero incident angle.** — Sidewall guidance uses incidence above the critical angle.

<h4 id="solution-m10h21">Situation 21: A ray through a parallel glass slab · Find the internal angle to the normal</h4>

A ray in air enters a parallel-sided glass slab of thickness 0.02 m and refractive index 1.5. Its incident angle is 35° to the normal. Both outside regions are air. Vacuum wavelength is $6\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m$. Ignore reflection loss and dispersion. Use $c=3.00\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The lateral displacement is the perpendicular separation of the emerging ray from the undeflected incident line. Find the internal angle to the normal.

<h4 id="solution-m10h31">Situation 31: Infer a work function from two colors · Find the metal work function in electronvolts</h4>

A metal illuminated at vacuum wavelength $3.5\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m$ has stopping-potential magnitude 1.2 V. The same metal is then illuminated at $3\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m$. Use the one-photon photoelectric model, $h=6.626\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$, $c=3.00\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $e=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$, and $m_e=9.11\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$. Find the metal work function in electronvolts.

1. **Identify the model and the conditions.** First infer the metal’s work function from the known color and stopping voltage. Keep that work function fixed for the second color.

2. **Infer work function in electronvolts.**

   $$\phi_{\rm eV}=hc/(e\lambda_1)-V_1=2.34521\,\mathrm{eV}$$

3. **Use the threshold definition.**

   $$\lambda_{\rm th}=hc/(e\phi_{\rm eV})$$

4. **Calculate the second maximum kinetic energy.**

   $$K_{2,\max,\rm eV}=hc/(e\lambda_2)-\phi_{\rm eV}=1.79087\,\mathrm{eV}$$

5. **Solve the requested quantity.**

   $$\phi_{\rm eV}=3.54521-1.2=2.34521\,\mathrm{eV}$$

6. **Check the physical result.** A work function is a property of the specified metal surface in this model.

7. **State the result.**

   $$2.34521\,\mathrm{eV}$$

**Answer:** $2.34521\,\mathrm{eV}$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$3.54521\,\mathrm{eV}$** — This gives first-color photon energy before subtracting the measured electron kinetic energy.
- **$4.74521\,\mathrm{eV}$** — The work function is photon energy minus maximum kinetic energy.
- **$1.2\,\mathrm{eV}$** — The stopping voltage corresponds to electron kinetic energy, not work function.

1. **Identify the model and the conditions.** First infer the metal’s work function from the known color and stopping voltage. Keep that work function fixed for the second color.

2. **Infer work function in electronvolts.**

   $$\phi_{\rm eV}=hc/(e\lambda_1)-V_1=2.10206\,\mathrm{eV}$$

   $$\lambda_{\rm th}=hc/(e\phi_{\rm eV})$$

4. **Calculate the second maximum kinetic energy.**

   $$K_{2,\max,\rm eV}=hc/(e\lambda_2)-\phi_{\rm eV}=1.77551\,\mathrm{eV}$$

   $$5.9029\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m$$

**Answer:** $5.9029\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

6. **Check the physical result.** Photon energy depends on wavelength, not total laser power.

7. **State the result.**

   $$4.41733\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J$$

**Answer:** $4.41733\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$2\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm J$** — This is the whole pulse energy, not one photon’s energy.
- **$2.6504\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J$** — Absorption fraction changes the number absorbed, not the energy of each photon.

Use $h=6.626\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$, $c=3.00\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, and $e=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$. Find the maximum emitted-electron kinetic energy in electronvolts.

1. **Identify the model and the conditions.** Separate energy per photon, total absorbed photon number, and emission yield. Only the photon excess above the work function sets maximum electron kinetic energy.

2. **Calculate energy per photon.**

   $$E_\gamma=hc/\lambda=4.41733\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J$$

3. **Convert absorbed energy to absorbed photon count.**

   $$N_{\rm abs}=f_{\rm abs}Pt/E_\gamma=2.71657\times10^{13}$$

4. **Check threshold and electron yield separately.**

   $$K_{\max,\rm eV}=E_\gamma/e-\phi_{\rm eV}=0.757387>0,\quad N_e=\eta N_{\rm abs}$$

5. **Solve the requested quantity.**

   $$K_{\max,\rm eV}=E_\gamma/e-\phi=0.757387\,\mathrm{eV}$$

6. **Check the physical result.** Both parameter sets are above the specified one-photon threshold.

7. **State the result.**

   $$0.757387\,\mathrm{eV}$$

**Answer:** $0.757387\,\mathrm{eV}$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$2.75739\,\mathrm{eV}$** — This omits the work function.
- **$4.75739\,\mathrm{eV}$** — The electron must spend energy escaping; the work function is subtracted.
- **$0.454432\,\mathrm{eV}$** — Absorption fraction changes event count, not excess energy per absorbed photon.

<h4 id="solution-m10h55">Situation 55: Photon energy and emitted charge · model check</h4>

A monochromatic laser delivers 0.002 W for 0.01 s at vacuum wavelength $4.5\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m$. A metal absorbs fraction 0.6 of the incident energy. Its work function is 2 eV. Assume one-photon photoemission and an electron yield of 0.2 emitted electrons per absorbed photon on average. Ignore charging of the metal. Use $h=6.626\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$, $c=3.00\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, and $e=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$. If laser power doubles at fixed wavelength and all stated fractions remain constant, what changes?

1. **Identify what stays fixed.** Separate energy per photon, total absorbed photon number, and emission yield. Only the photon excess above the work function sets maximum electron kinetic energy.

2. **Calculate energy per photon.**

   $$E_\gamma=hc/\lambda=4.41733\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J$$

3. **Convert absorbed energy to absorbed photon count.**

   $$N_{\rm abs}=f_{\rm abs}Pt/E_\gamma=2.71657\times10^{13}$$

4. **Check threshold and electron yield separately.**

   $$K_{\max,\rm eV}=E_\gamma/e-\phi_{\rm eV}=0.757387>0,\quad N_e=\eta N_{\rm abs}$$

5. **Apply the conclusion.** Photon count doubles while the energy per photon and work function stay fixed.

**Answer:** The expected emitted charge doubles, but maximum electron kinetic energy stays the same.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **Maximum electron kinetic energy doubles and emitted charge stays fixed.** — Intensity controls photon arrival rate, not photon energy at fixed wavelength.
- **Both photon energy and work function double.** — Neither is set by the laser power in the stated model.
- **No quantity changes because wavelength is unchanged.** — The total photon count and expected emitted charge depend on pulse energy.
