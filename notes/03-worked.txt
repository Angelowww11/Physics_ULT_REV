### Charging diagrams and charge-conservation calculations

**Names and signs — primary pp. 4–6.** The historical word “electric” comes from the Greek word for amber. The coulomb names the charge unit after Coulomb. Franklin's positive and negative labels distinguish the two charge signs. “Positive” does not mean more electrons; an electron deficit gives a positive net charge in the ordinary charging examples.

| Particle | Approximate mass, kg | Charge, C |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electron | $9.11 \times 10^{-31}$ | $- 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ |
| Proton | $1.673 \times 10^{-27}$ | $+ 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ |
| Neutron | $1.675 \times 10^{-27}$ | 0 |

**Source correction.** Primary p. 21 repeats the proton's mass for the neutron. Their masses are close but not identical. The rounded values above distinguish them. A neutral object can contain many charged particles; their net charge cancels.

**Grounding.** Earth acts as a large charge reservoir. Connecting a conductor to ground lets electrons move as the surrounding electric field requires. With a nearby negative rod, electrons leave the grounded conductor. Remove the ground first, then the rod: the conductor remains positive. A positive inducing rod draws electrons from ground and leaves the conductor negative after the same removal sequence.

{{figure:m3-main:13|The original induction sequence. Distinguish charge separation from a final net charge.}}

**Conduction versus polarization.** A charged metal redistributes mobile electrons over its surface. Charge on an insulating rod does not spread as freely. Neutral paper can still be attracted to a charged comb. Polarization places the nearer opposite-sign charge slightly closer, making its attraction exceed the farther like-sign repulsion. Attraction alone does not prove a net charge.

:::example Count the transferred electrons and check the charge sign
**Source:** Primary p. 8. **Given:** Initially neutral body; final charge $+ 0.32\,\mathrm{C}$; $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$.

1. Identify an electron deficit because the final charge is positive.
2. Use $N = \frac{\left| Q \right|}{e}$, with both quantities in coulombs.
3. Calculate $N = \frac{0.32}{\left( 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \right)} = 1.99750 \times 10^{18}$ electrons removed.
4. Check the sign: removing negative charge produces a positive change in net charge.
5. If asked for mass change, use $\Delta m = - Nm_{e}$. With $m_{e} = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $\Delta m \approx - 1.82 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{kg}$.

**Check.** Do not use a negative electron count. The count is positive; the transfer direction and charge sign carry the physical information.
:::

:::derivation Identical conducting spheres share charge equally: a labeled extension
For two isolated identical metal spheres that touch, conservation gives $Q_{\mathrm{total}} = Q_{1} + Q_{2}$. Symmetry gives equal final charges after they reach equilibrium. Therefore $Q_{\mathrm{final,each}}=\frac{Q_1+Q_2}{2}$.

For $+ 8\,\mathrm{nC}$ and $- 2\,\mathrm{nC}$, the total is $+ 6\,\mathrm{nC}$, so each ends with $+ 3\,\mathrm{nC}$. The initially negative sphere loses enough electrons to become positive. Unequal spheres do not generally share charge equally. Widely separated spherical conductors have equal potentials at equilibrium, giving approximately $\frac{Q_{1}}{R_{1}} = \frac{Q_{2}}{R_{2}}$ before disconnection in that ideal model.
:::

### Complete the source force problems in one and two dimensions

:::example Two charges: equal force magnitudes, opposite directions
**Sources:** Primary p. 22; companion p. 4. **Given:** $q_{1} = + 25\,\mathrm{nC}$, $q_{2} = - 75\,\mathrm{nC}$, and $r = 3.0\,\mathrm{cm}$.

1. Convert charges to $+ 25 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C}$ and $- 75 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C}$.
2. Convert distance to $0.030\,\mathrm{m}$, then square it: $r^{2} = 9.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}^{2}$.
3. Substitute magnitudes in Coulomb's law.

$$F=\frac{(8.99\times10^9)(25\times10^{-9})(75\times10^{-9})}{(0.030)^2}
=0.0187292\ \mathrm N.$$

4. Choose attraction because the signs differ. Each charge feels a force toward the other charge.
5. Apply Newton's third law: $\mathbf F_{1\to2}=-\mathbf F_{2\to1}$ as vectors.

**Check.** The larger-magnitude charge does not receive a larger pair-force magnitude. Both magnitudes round to $0.019\,\mathrm{N}$.
:::

:::example Three charges on the x-axis
**Sources:** Primary pp. 24–25. **Find:** Force on $+ 5.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ at $x = 0$. Sources are $+ 1.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ at $x = 0.020\,\mathrm{m}$ and $- 3.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ at $x = 0.040\,\mathrm{m}$.

1. Select +x to the right. The positive source repels the target left; the negative source attracts it right.
2. Calculate the leftward magnitude: $F_{1} = \frac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \left( 5 \times 10^{-9} \right) \left( 1 \times 10^{-9} \right)}{\left( 0.020 \right)^{2}} = 1.12375 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{N}$.
3. Calculate the rightward magnitude: $F_{2} = \frac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \left( 5 \times 10^{-9} \right) \left( 3 \times 10^{-9} \right)}{\left( 0.040 \right)^{2}} = 8.428125 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{N}$.
4. Add signed components: $F_{x} = - 1.12375 \times 10^{-4} + 8.428125 \times 10^{-5} = - 2.809375 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{N}$.
5. Report $28.09\,\mathrm{\mu N}$ left.

**Check.** The farther charge is three times larger, but its squared distance is four times larger. Its force is only $\frac{3}{4}$ of the nearer source's force.
:::

:::example Symmetric force pair, with the companion's conflicting labels resolved
**Source:** Companion p. 5. **Use the drawing:** $q_{1} = q_{2} = + 2.0\,\mathrm{\mu C}$ at (0, ±$0.30\,\mathrm{m}$), and $Q = + 4.0\,\mathrm{\mu C}$ at ($0.40\,\mathrm{m}$, 0). This is the set consistent with the printed $0.46\,\mathrm{N}$ answer.

1. Find each source-to-target distance: $r = \sqrt{\left( 0.30^{2} + 0.40^{2} \right)} = 0.50\,\mathrm{m}$.
2. Calculate each magnitude: $F = \frac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \left( 2.0 \times 10^{-6} \right) \left( 4.0 \times 10^{-6} \right)}{\left( 0.50 \right)^{2}} = 0.28768\,\mathrm{N}$.
3. Resolve each horizontal component: The horizontal component from each source is $F \left( \frac{0.40}{0.50} \right) = 0.230144\,\mathrm{N}$.
4. Cancel the equal and opposite vertical components, ±$F \left( \frac{0.30}{0.50} \right)$.
5. Add the horizontal components: $F_{\mathrm{net}} = 2 \left( 0.230144 \right) = 0.460288\,\mathrm{N}$ in +x.

**Ambiguity check.** The text instead prints $y = \pm 30\,\mathrm{m}$ and $Q = 40\,\mathrm{\mu C}$. Keeping $40\,\mathrm{\mu C}$ but using the drawing's $0.30\,\mathrm{m}$ gives $4.60288\,\mathrm{N}$. Using both literal printed text values gives about $2.13 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{N}$. These are different problems. State the chosen values; do not force an inconsistent question to match an answer key.
:::

:::derivation Compare electric and gravitational forces without retaining the distance
**Source:** Primary p. 21. Use $F_{e} = \frac{ke^{2}}{r^{2}}$ and $F_{g} = \frac{Gm_{e} m_{p}}{r^{2}}$ for a proton–electron pair.

$$\frac{F_e}{F_g}=\frac{ke^2/r^2}{Gm_em_p/r^2}
=\frac{ke^2}{Gm_em_p}\approx2.27\times10^{39}.$$

The squared distance cancels. At $r = 5.3 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m}$, $F_{e} \approx 8.21 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}$ and $F_{g} \approx 3.62 \times 10^{-47}\,\mathrm{N}$. Both attract. The ratio is enormous even though both formulas have inverse-square distance dependence.
:::
