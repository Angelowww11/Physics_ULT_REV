## Current and DC circuits: label nodes, currents, and polarity

Current is signed charge flow per time: $I = \frac{dQ}{dt}$, or $\frac{Q}{\Delta t}$ for a constant average flow. One ampere is one $\frac{C}{s}$. Conventional current points as positive charge would move. Electron drift in a metal points the opposite way. Random microscopic motion alone gives no net current. Current through a branch is a signed scalar; current density J is a vector.

### Derive drift current and macroscopic resistance

In time $\Delta t$, carriers drift through volume $Av_{d} \Delta t$. With number density n and charge magnitude |q|, charge crossing the area is $nAv_{d} \Delta t \left| q \right|$. Divide by $\Delta t$:

$$I=n|q|Av_d,\quad J=I/A=n|q|v_d,\quad \mathbf J=nq\mathbf v_d.$$

The vector expression keeps the signed charge. Carrier concentration has units $m^{-3}$, not $\frac{C}{m^{3}}$. Current from several carrier populations adds. J parallel to E is the simple isotropic ohmic model; it is not a universal statement for every medium and magnetic-field condition.

**Worked examples — primary pp. 16–17.** $29.3\,\mathrm{C}$ in $7.84\,\mathrm{s}$ gives $3.737\,\mathrm{A}$. A $0.010\,\mathrm{A}$ calculator for $60\,\mathrm{s}$ transfers $0.600\,\mathrm{C}$. $J = 50\,\frac{\mathrm{A}}{\mathrm{m}^{2}}$, $q = - 0.10\,\mathrm{C}$, and $v_{d} = - 2.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}$ give $n = \frac{J}{\left( qv_{d} \right)} = 2.5 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}^{-3}$. The unusually large individual charge is the source's hypothetical value, not an electron charge.

For an ohmic isotropic material, $E = \rho J$ and conductivity $\sigma = \frac{1}{\rho}$. Substitute $E = \frac{V}{L}$ and $J = \frac{I}{A}$ for a uniform wire:

$$\frac VL=\rho\frac IA\Rightarrow V=IR,\quad R=\frac{\rho L}{A},\quad \rho=\frac{RA}{L}.$$

Resistivity $\rho$ has units Ω·m; resistance R has units Ω. For a circular wire $A = \pi r^{2} = \frac{\pi d^{2}}{4}$. Doubling LENGTH at fixed area doubles R. Doubling DIAMETER at fixed length divides R by four. For the same wire stretched without volume change, $A = \frac{V_{\mathrm{wire}}}{L}$, so $R = \frac{\rho L^{2}}{V_{\mathrm{wire}}}$ if resistivity stays constant.

### Temperature, ohmic behavior, and resistor identification

Near a reference temperature, $R = R_{0} \left[ 1 + \alpha \left( T - T_{0} \right) \right]$, and similarly for resistivity. Rearrange $\alpha = \frac{\left( \frac{R}{R_{0}} - 1 \right)}{\left( T - T_{0} \right)}$. This linear approximation can fail over a large temperature range. Do not confuse the resistance-temperature coefficient with thermal-expansion $\alpha$. $R_{0}$ is resistance when used in the R equation, despite the source's occasional “resistivity” label.

Ohm's law means a linear V–I relation under stable material conditions. A lamp heating up need not have a constant resistance. Carbon/semiconductor temperature behavior can differ from metals.

The source includes resistor color bands. For a four-band resistor, the first two colors give digits, the third gives a power-of-ten multiplier, and the fourth gives tolerance. Digit sequence: black 0, brown 1, red 2, orange 3, yellow 4, green 5, blue 6, violet 7, gray 8, white 9. Gold/silver commonly represent multipliers $\frac{10^{-1}}{10^{-2}}$ or tolerances ±5%/±10%. Read the band roles and orientation, not only the colors.

### Electrical power, energy, and internal resistance

An amount dq crossing voltage V transfers energy $dW = Vdq$. Divide by dt:

$$P=VI,\quad P_R=I^2R=V_R^2/R,\quad E_{energy}=Pt.$$

The resistor substitutions require Ohm's law. A kilowatt-hour is an ENERGY unit: $1\,\mathrm{kWh} = 3.6 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{J}$. It is not “kilowatts per hour.” At fixed V, increasing R lowers resistor power; at fixed I, increasing R raises it.

A real source has emf $\mathcal{E}$ and internal resistance r. For discharging current I, terminal voltage $V = \mathcal{E} - Ir$ and $I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{\left( R + r \right)}$. During charging with current driven into the positive terminal, $V = \mathcal{E} + Ir$ under that stated convention. Emf is energy supplied per charge, not a mechanical force.

**Worked example — companion heating element.** $R = 20 \Omega$ on $120\,\mathrm{V}$ gives $I = 6\,\mathrm{A}$ and $P = 720\,\mathrm{W}$. In $15\,\mathrm{min} = 900\,\mathrm{s}$, energy: $648{,}000\,\mathrm{J} = 0.18\,\mathrm{kWh}$. Increasing R to $30\,\Omega$ at the same voltage gives $480\,\mathrm{W}$, not greater power.

### Derive series and parallel resistor rules

Series resistors have a common current if there is no branch at their connecting node. $V_{\mathrm{tot}} = \sum IR_{i}$ gives $R_{\mathrm{eq}} = \sum R_{i}$. Therefore $\frac{V_{i}}{V_{\mathrm{tot}}} = \frac{R_{i}}{R_{\mathrm{eq}}}$: the voltage-divider relation.

Parallel resistors share the same two nodes and voltage. $I_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_i\frac{V}{R_i}$ gives $\frac1{R_{\mathrm{eq}}}=\sum_i\frac1{R_i}$. For two, $R_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{R_{1} R_{2}}{\left( R_{1} + R_{2} \right)}$. Their current ratio is $\frac{I_{1}}{I_{2}} = \frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}}$. An equivalent parallel resistance must be smaller than the smallest positive branch resistance.

**Worked example.** Put $3\,\Omega$ and $6\,\Omega$ in parallel across $12\,\mathrm{V}$. $R_{\mathrm{eq}} = 2 \Omega$; branch currents are $4\,\mathrm{A}$ and $2\,\mathrm{A}$; total is $6\,\mathrm{A}$. Total power: $12 \times 6 = 72\,\mathrm{W}$, also $4^{2} \times 3 + 2^{2} \times 6 = 72\,\mathrm{W}$. A series $4\,\Omega$ added before that group gives $6\,\Omega$ overall. The source current becomes $2\,\mathrm{A}$, the parallel-group drop becomes $4\,\mathrm{V}$, and its branch currents become $\frac{4}{3} A$ and $\frac{2}{3} A$.

An ideal ammeter goes in SERIES and has zero resistance. An ideal voltmeter goes in PARALLEL and has infinite resistance. An ideal wire shorts points into one node. A shorted resistor has zero voltage, not its previous unmodified branch voltage. Do not reduce an arbitrary bridge as series/parallel when the required node conditions fail.

### Kirchhoff rules: derive the equations and keep the signs

KCL follows conservation of charge at a steady node: $\sum I_{\mathrm{in}} = \sum I_{\mathrm{out}}$. KVL follows energy balance around a lumped DC loop: $\sum \Delta V = 0$. A resistor traversed with the chosen current gives −IR; traversed against it gives +IR. Across an ideal battery, negative-to-positive gives $+ \mathcal{E}$ and the reverse gives $- \mathcal{E}$.

Choose arbitrary current directions, write independent equations, and solve them simultaneously. A negative answer reverses the assumed direction; it is not a negative physical resistance. Do not change an assumed arrow midway through the algebra.

**Worked example — companion p. 14 type.** A loop has a $12\,\mathrm{V}$ aiding source, an opposing $6\,\mathrm{V}$ source, and 6 Ω total resistance. KVL gives $12 - 6 - 6 I = 0$, so $I = 1\,\mathrm{A}$. If both sources aided, the same resistance would give $3\,\mathrm{A}$.

**Primary multi-branch example, pp. 70–74.** Preserve the source's chosen arrows. Its independent equations are $I_{3} = I_{1} + I_{2}$, $30 I_{1} + 41 I_{3} = 45$, and $- 30 I_{1} + 21 I_{2} = 80$. Solving without early rounding gives $I_{1} = - \frac{2335}{2721} \approx - 0.85814\,\mathrm{A}$, $I_{2} \approx 2.58361\,\mathrm{A}$, and $I_{3} \approx 1.72547\,\mathrm{A}$. Substitute all three into the ORIGINAL equations. The negative $I_{1}$ reverses its chosen arrow. The printed intermediate rounded expressions obscure this small difference.

**Show the elimination.** Substitute $I_{3} = I_{1} + I_{2}$ into the first loop. This produces $71 I_{1} + 41 I_{2} = 45$. Keep $- 30 I_{1} + 21 I_{2} = 80$ as the second loop. Multiply the first equation by 30 and the second by 71. Add them to remove $I_{1}$:

$$\begin{aligned}2130I_1+1230I_2&=1350,\\-2130I_1+1491I_2&=5680,\\2721I_2&=7030.\end{aligned}$$

Thus $I_{2} = \frac{7030}{2721}$. Back-substitution gives $I_{1} = \frac{\left( 21 I_{2} - 80 \right)}{30} = - \frac{2335}{2721}$ and $I_{3} = \frac{4695}{2721}$. Check KCL: $- 0.858140 + 2.583609 = 1.725469\,\mathrm{A}$. Check the loops with full precision: $30 I_{1} + 41 I_{3} = 45\,\mathrm{V}$ and $- 30 I_{1} + 21 I_{2} = 80\,\mathrm{V}$. A small residual after using rounded displayed currents is a rounding effect; a large residual needs corrected signs or algebra.

### RC charging and discharging: derive the exponentials

For an initially uncharged capacitor C charged through R from constant emf $\mathcal{E}$, KVL gives $\mathcal{E} - R \left( \frac{dq}{dt} \right) - \frac{q}{C} = 0$. Separate $\frac{dq}{\left( C\mathcal{E} - q \right)} = \frac{dt}{\left( RC \right)}$. Integrate with $q \left( 0 \right) = 0$:

$$q=C\mathcal E(1-e^{-t/RC}),\quad V_C=\mathcal E(1-e^{-t/RC}),\quad I=\frac{\mathcal E}{R}e^{-t/RC}.$$

For discharging through R, $R \left( \frac{dq}{dt} \right) + \frac{q}{C} = 0$, so $q = Q_{0} \exp \left( - \frac{t}{\left( RC \right)} \right)$ and $V_{C} = V_{0} \exp \left( - \frac{t}{\left( RC \right)} \right)$. The signed current depends on its reference arrow; its magnitude is $\frac{V_{0} \exp \left( - \frac{t}{\left( RC \right)} \right)}{R}$. The capacitor voltage cannot jump instantaneously in this ideal finite-current model.

$$\tau_{RC}=RC,\quad t_{discharge}=-RC\ln f,\quad t_{charge}=-RC\ln(1-f).$$

At one $\tau$, discharge leaves 36.8%; charging reaches 63.2%. “63%” is rounded, so exactly 63.000% is not exactly one $\tau$. For an arbitrary initial voltage $V_{0}$, $V_{C} \left( t \right) = V_{f} + \left( V_{0} - V_{f} \right) \exp \left( - \frac{t}{\left( RC \right)} \right)$. A network's relevant R is the resistance seen by the capacitor with independent ideal sources appropriately deactivated, not always the sum of all printed resistors.

**Worked example — primary p. 79.** $C = 35\,\mathrm{\mu F}$, $R = 120 \Omega$. $\tau = 0.00420\,\mathrm{s}$. To reach 10% of the initial voltage, $t = - 0.00420 \ln \left( 0.10 \right) = 0.009671\,\mathrm{s} = 9.67\,\mathrm{ms}$. Do not enter 10 in place of 0.10.

**Show the charging integration.** The separated equation integrates from $q = 0$ to q(t), and from time 0 to t. The minus sign comes from differentiating $C\mathcal{E} - q$:

$$-\ln\left(\frac{C\mathcal E-q}{C\mathcal E}\right)=\frac{t}{RC}\Rightarrow 1-\frac q{C\mathcal E}=e^{-t/RC}.$$

For $R = 2.0 k\Omega$, $C = 10\,\mathrm{\mu F}$, and $\mathcal{E} = 12\,\mathrm{V}$, $\tau = 0.020\,\mathrm{s}$ and $Q_{\mathrm{final}} = 120\,\mathrm{\mu C}$. At $t = 0.020\,\mathrm{s}$, $q \approx 75.854\,\mathrm{\mu C}$, $V_{C} \approx 7.58545\,\mathrm{V}$, and $I \approx 2.20728\,\mathrm{mA}$. Check KVL: $RI + V_{C} = 4.41455 + 7.58545 = 12\,\mathrm{V}$. To reach 90%, solve $t = - 0.020 \ln \left( 0.10 \right) \approx 0.04605\,\mathrm{s}$.

**Transient energy extension.** During a complete ideal RC charge from zero, the battery supplies $\int \mathcal{E} I dt = C\mathcal{E}^{2}$. The capacitor stores $\frac{C\mathcal{E}^{2}}{2}$. The resistor dissipates the remaining $\frac{C\mathcal{E}^{2}}{2}$, independent of positive R in this model. A smaller R changes the time and peak power; it does not remove that total dissipated energy.
