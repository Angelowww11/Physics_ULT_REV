### Material tables, thermometer operation, and phase diagrams

The primary and companion decks use different rounded material coefficients. For example, the primary gives steel α = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹. The companion uses 1.1 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹. Use the coefficient stated in each problem.

{{figure:m2-main:15|Primary linear-expansion coefficients. The units describe a fractional length change per kelvin.}}

{{figure:m2-main:17|Primary volume-expansion coefficients. For isotropic solids, the listed β values are three times α.}}

**Bimetallic strip.** Two bonded metals have different expansion coefficients. Heating makes the higher-expansion metal occupy the outside of the bend. The strip therefore bends toward the lower-expansion metal. A thermostat uses the bend to operate a contact. A liquid thermometer instead uses a calibrated change in liquid volume.

**Historical correction.** The companion prints 1792 for Celsius's scale. [Uppsala University](https://www.uu.se/en/about-uu/history/prominent-people/anders-celsius) dates his original scale to 1742. His original orientation differed from the modern Celsius convention.

**Microscopic model.** Solid particles vibrate around equilibrium positions. Liquid particles remain close but can rearrange. Gas particles are widely separated. “Temperature measures average kinetic energy” is a useful introductory model, especially for an ideal gas. It is not a universal formula for the total internal energy of every material.

:::derivation Derive the temperature-scale conversions from a straight line
Use TF = aTC + b for the two fixed points at standard pressure.

1. Insert the freezing point: 32 = a(0) + b, so b = 32.
2. Insert the boiling point: 212 = 100a + 32.
3. Subtract 32 and divide by 100: a = 180/100 = 9/5.
4. Subtract the relation at two temperatures. The constant offset cancels.

$$T_F=\frac95T_C+32,
\qquad T_C=\frac59(T_F-32),
\qquad \Delta T_F=\frac95\Delta T_C.$$

For 37°C, TF = 98.6°F and TK = 310.15 K. For a 2°C increase, ΔTF = 3.6°F and ΔTK = 2 K. Do not add 32 or 273.15 to a temperature difference.
:::

**Water phase diagram.** The companion marks the triple point near 0.01°C and 0.006 atm. Its critical point lies near 374°C and 218 atm. The solid–liquid boundary has a negative slope in this region. Increasing pressure can favor the denser liquid over ordinary ice.

**Carbon-dioxide phase diagram.** The companion marks a triple point near −56°C and 5 atm, and a critical point near 31°C and 73 atm. At 1 atm, solid CO₂ sublimes instead of passing through a stable liquid phase. The values on the diagram are rounded. A heating curve has different axes: temperature versus added energy or heating time.

**Phase names.** Melting: solid → liquid. Freezing: liquid → solid. Vaporization: liquid → gas. Condensation: gas → liquid. Sublimation: solid → gas. Deposition: gas → solid.

:::example The missing expanding-pipe problem
**Sources:** Companion p. 3; alternate companion p. 3. **Given:** L₀ = 55 m, Ti = 5°C, Tf = 155°C, αsteel = 1.1 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹.

1. Find ΔT = 155 − 5 = 150 K.
2. Select linear expansion because the question asks for a length change.
3. Calculate ΔL = αL₀ΔT = (1.1 × 10⁻⁵)(55)(150) = 0.09075 m.
4. Convert to centimeters: 0.09075 m × 100 = 9.075 cm, about 9.1 cm.
5. Add the initial length only if asked for final length: L = 55.09075 m.

**Check.** The pipe expands because both α and ΔT are positive. Do not report 55.09075 m as the amount of expansion.
:::

:::derivation Derive the changing density of an expanding body
Assume the mass stays constant and use V = V₀(1 + βΔT).

$$\rho=\frac{m}{V}=\frac{m}{V_0(1+\beta\Delta T)}
=\frac{\rho_0}{1+\beta\Delta T}.$$

For small |βΔT|, expand the reciprocal to first order: ρ ≈ ρ₀(1−βΔT). A positive volume expansion lowers density when mass stays fixed. This is an algebraic consequence of the source laws, not an additional universal constant-β assumption for water near 4°C.
:::

### Complete the heat and mixing problems from both decks

:::example The two fever examples use different physical models
**Primary p. 31:** The mass is 80 kg and the body is approximated as water, c = 4186 J/(kg·K).

1. Calculate ΔT = 39 − 37 = 2 K.
2. Calculate Q = mcΔT = 80(4186)(2) = 669,760 J.
3. Divide by 1000 to obtain 669.76 kJ.

**Companion p. 9; alternate companion p. 7:** The mass is 70 kg and cbody = 3400 J/(kg·K).

1. Use the same 2 K temperature increase.
2. Calculate Q = 70(3400)(2) = 476,000 J, about 4.8 × 10⁵ J.

**Check.** The second problem gives a body-specific heat. Replacing it with water's specific heat changes the stated model. These are classroom energy estimates, not medical instructions.
:::

:::example Iron and water: show the algebra before the temperature answer
**Source:** Primary p. 33. **Given:** miron = 0.065 kg, ciron = 444 J/(kg·K), Tiron = 525°C; mwater = 0.635 kg, cwater = 4186, Twater = 15°C.

1. Assume an insulated system, negligible container heat capacity, and a final liquid-water state.
2. Write both signed heat terms using the same final temperature.
3. Expand the brackets and collect the terms containing Tf.

$$\begin{aligned}
0.065(444)(T_f-525)+0.635(4186)(T_f-15)&=0,\\
28.86T_f-15151.5+2658.11T_f-39871.65&=0,\\
2686.97T_f&=55023.15,\\
T_f&=20.4778^\circ\mathrm C.
\end{aligned}$$

4. Check that 15°C < Tf < 525°C.
5. Calculate heat gained by the water and heat lost by the iron. Both magnitudes are about 14.56 kJ.

**Why the sign works.** Tf−15 is positive; Tf−525 is negative. Both signs come from the same ΔT definition.
:::

:::example Melt 2.0 kg of ice already at its melting point
**Sources:** Companion p. 10; alternate companion p. 8. Use the problem's Lf = 334,000 J/kg.

1. Identify a phase change at constant temperature, so use Q = mLf.
2. Calculate Q = 2.0(334,000) = 668,000 J.
3. Report 668 kJ absorbed.

**Check.** There is no mcΔT term because the ice starts at 0°C and the question ends with liquid water at 0°C. Freezing the same mass reverses the heat sign.
:::

:::example How much ice cools the cola to 0°C?
**Sources:** Companion p. 11; alternate companion pp. 8–9. **Given:** 0.25 kg of cola approximated as water at 25°C; ice starts at −20°C. Neglect the glass. Use cwater = 4190, cice = 2090 J/(kg·K), and Lf = 334,000 J/kg.

1. Calculate the energy the cola releases on cooling: Qcola = 0.25(4190)(25) = 26,187.5 J.
2. Find the energy needed per kilogram of ice to warm to 0°C: cice(20) = 41,800 J/kg.
3. Add the energy per kilogram needed to melt it: 41,800 + 334,000 = 375,800 J/kg.
4. Equate the gained and lost energy magnitudes, then isolate the ice mass.

$$m_{ice}=\frac{m_{cola}c_{water}(25-0)}{c_{ice}(0-(-20))+L_f}
=\frac{26187.5}{375800}=0.0696847\ \mathrm{kg}.$$

5. Convert to grams: about 69.7 g, which rounds to the slide's 70 g.

**Boundary check.** This is the ice mass that can just warm and completely melt while the final mixture reaches 0°C. More ice can remain unmelted. Less ice produces a final temperature above 0°C under this model.
:::

### First-law cases, heating curves, and conduction calculations

:::example Separate heat from work in the source's conceptual cases
**Source:** Primary p. 43.

- Soup on an electric stove receives energy through heat transfer from the hot pan.
- Hands near a fire receive thermal radiation; warm-air convection can also contribute.
- A re-entering spacecraft transfers macroscopic mechanical energy into internal energy through interaction with the atmosphere. Compression and drag are central; a hot atmosphere can also exchange heat with the surface.

**Take note.** A temperature increase alone does not tell you whether the energy enters as heat or as work. Specify the system and the transfer mechanism.
:::

:::example Complete the two first-law numerical cases
**Primary p. 44:** Q = +3000 J and Wby = +2500 J. Therefore ΔU = Q−Wby = +500 J.

**Primary p. 45:** The net heat is 40−25 = +15 J. The net work by the system is 10−4 = +6 J. Therefore ΔU = 15−6 = +9 J.

**Companion p. 8; alternate companion p. 6:** The net heat is 1500−400 = 1100 J. The net work on the gas is −600+250 = −350 J. Therefore ΔU = Q+Won = 1100−350 = +750 J.

**Check.** The primary and companion sign conventions agree. Their work variables have opposite definitions.
:::

:::derivation Read the slope of a heating curve
During a single phase, Q = mcΔT. At fixed m and approximately constant c, ΔT/ΔQ = 1/(mc). A larger heat capacity produces a flatter temperature-versus-energy slope. During an equilibrium phase change, the added energy changes phase fraction, so the temperature plateau has slope zero.

For heating at constant power P, Q = Pt. Thus dT/dt = P/(mc) within a phase, and a complete phase transition takes Δt = mL/P. These relations require negligible energy losses and the stated constant-power model.
:::

:::example Apply the companion's conduction equation
**Source law:** Companion p. 11; alternate companion p. 9. The numbers below form a labeled extension.

**Given:** k = 0.80 W/(m·K), A = 2.0 m², thickness L = 0.10 m, and face temperatures 30°C and 10°C.

1. Find ΔT = 20 K.
2. Calculate the steady heat rate: H = kAΔT/L = 0.80(2.0)(20)/0.10 = 320 W.
3. For 60 s, calculate transferred energy Q = Ht = 320(60) = 19,200 J.
4. Check the rate unit: W/(m·K) × m² × K/m = W.

**Assumptions.** The slab is uniform. The temperature profile has reached steady state. Ignore side losses and contact resistance.
:::
