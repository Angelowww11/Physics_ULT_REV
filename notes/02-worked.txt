### Material tables, thermometer operation, and phase diagrams

The primary and companion decks use different rounded material coefficients. For example, the primary gives steel $\alpha = 1.2 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K}^{-1}$. The companion uses $1.1 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K}^{-1}$. Use the coefficient stated in each problem.

{{figure:m2-main:15|Primary linear-expansion coefficients. The units describe a fractional length change per kelvin.}}

{{figure:m2-main:17|Primary volume-expansion coefficients. For isotropic solids, the listed β values are three times α.}}

**Bimetallic strip.** Two bonded metals have different expansion coefficients. Heating makes the higher-expansion metal occupy the outside of the bend. The strip therefore bends toward the lower-expansion metal. A thermostat uses the bend to operate a contact. A liquid thermometer instead uses a calibrated change in liquid volume.

**Historical correction.** The companion prints 1792 for Celsius's scale. [Uppsala University](https://www.uu.se/en/about-uu/history/prominent-people/anders-celsius) dates his original scale to 1742. His original orientation differed from the modern Celsius convention.

**Microscopic model.** Solid particles vibrate around equilibrium positions. Liquid particles remain close but can rearrange. Gas particles are widely separated. “Temperature measures average kinetic energy” is a useful introductory model, especially for an ideal gas. It is not a universal formula for the total internal energy of every material.

:::derivation Derive the temperature-scale conversions from a straight line
Use $T_{F} = aT_{C} + b$ for the two fixed points at standard pressure.

1. Insert the freezing point: $32 = a \left( 0 \right) + b$, so $b = 32$.
2. Insert the boiling point: $212 = 100 a + 32$.
3. Subtract 32 and divide by 100: $a = \frac{180}{100} = \frac{9}{5}$.
4. Subtract the relation at two temperatures. The constant offset cancels.

$$T_F=\frac95T_C+32,
\qquad T_C=\frac59(T_F-32),
\qquad \Delta T_F=\frac95\Delta T_C.$$

For 37°C, $T_{F} = 98.6^{\circ} F$ and $T_{K} = 310.15\,\mathrm{K}$. For a 2°C increase, $\Delta T_{F} = 3.6^{\circ} F$ and $\Delta T_{K} = 2\,\mathrm{K}$. Do not add 32 or 273.15 to a temperature difference.
:::

**Water phase diagram.** The companion marks the triple point near 0.01°C and $0.006\,\mathrm{atm}$. Its critical point lies near 374°C and $218\,\mathrm{atm}$. The solid–liquid boundary has a negative slope in this region. Increasing pressure can favor the denser liquid over ordinary ice.

**Carbon-dioxide phase diagram.** The companion marks a triple point near −56°C and $5\,\mathrm{atm}$, and a critical point near 31°C and $73\,\mathrm{atm}$. At $1\,\mathrm{atm}$, solid CO₂ sublimes instead of passing through a stable liquid phase. The values on the diagram are rounded. A heating curve has different axes: temperature versus added energy or heating time.

**Phase names.** Melting: solid → liquid. Freezing: liquid → solid. Vaporization: liquid → gas. Condensation: gas → liquid. Sublimation: solid → gas. Deposition: gas → solid.

:::example The missing expanding-pipe problem
**Sources:** Companion p. 3; alternate companion p. 3. **Given:** $L_{0} = 55\,\mathrm{m}$, $T_{i} = 5^{\circ} C$, $T_{f} = 155^{\circ} C$, $\alpha _{\mathrm{steel}} = 1.1 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K}^{-1}$.

1. Find $\Delta T = 155 - 5 = 150\,\mathrm{K}$.
2. Select linear expansion because the question asks for a length change.
3. Calculate $\Delta L = \alpha L_{0} \Delta T = \left( 1.1 \times 10^{-5} \right) \left( 55 \right) \left( 150 \right) = 0.09075\,\mathrm{m}$.
4. Convert to centimeters: $0.09075\,\mathrm{m} \times 100 = 9.075\,\mathrm{cm}$, about $9.1\,\mathrm{cm}$.
5. Add the initial length only if asked for final length: $L = 55.09075\,\mathrm{m}$.

**Check.** The pipe expands because both $\alpha$ and $\Delta T$ are positive. Do not report $55.09075\,\mathrm{m}$ as the amount of expansion.
:::

:::derivation Derive the changing density of an expanding body
Assume the mass stays constant and use $V = V_{0} \left( 1 + \beta \Delta T \right)$.

$$\rho=\frac{m}{V}=\frac{m}{V_0(1+\beta\Delta T)}
=\frac{\rho_0}{1+\beta\Delta T}.$$

For small $\left| \beta \Delta T \right|$, expand the reciprocal to first order: $\rho \approx \rho_{0} \left( 1 - \beta \Delta T \right)$. A positive volume expansion lowers density when mass stays fixed. This is an algebraic consequence of the source laws, not an additional universal constant$- \beta$ assumption for water near 4°C.
:::

### Complete the heat and mixing problems from both decks

:::example The two fever examples use different physical models
**Primary p. 31:** The mass is $80\,\mathrm{kg}$ and the body is approximated as water, $c = 4186\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg}   \mathrm{K}}$.

1. Calculate $\Delta T = 39 - 37 = 2\,\mathrm{K}$.
2. Calculate $Q = mc\Delta T = 80 \left( 4186 \right) \left( 2 \right) = 669{,}760\,\mathrm{J}$.
3. Divide by 1000 to obtain $669.76\,\mathrm{kJ}$.

**Companion p. 9; alternate companion p. 7:** The mass is $70\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $c_{\mathrm{body}} = 3400\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg}   \mathrm{K}}$.

1. Use the same $2\,\mathrm{K}$ temperature increase.
2. Calculate $Q = 70 \left( 3400 \right) \left( 2 \right) = 476{,}000\,\mathrm{J}$, about $4.8 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J}$.

**Check.** The second problem gives a body-specific heat. Replacing it with water's specific heat changes the stated model. These are classroom energy estimates, not medical instructions.
:::

:::example Iron and water: show the algebra before the temperature answer
**Source:** Primary p. 33. **Given:** $m_{\mathrm{iron}} = 0.065\,\mathrm{kg}$, $c_{\mathrm{iron}} = 444\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg}   \mathrm{K}}$, $T_{\mathrm{iron}} = 525^{\circ} C$; $m_{\mathrm{water}} = 0.635\,\mathrm{kg}$, $c_{\mathrm{water}} = 4186$, $T_{\mathrm{water}} = 15^{\circ} C$.

1. Assume an insulated system, negligible container heat capacity, and a final liquid-water state.
2. Write both signed heat terms using the same final temperature.
3. Expand the brackets and collect the terms containing Tf.

$$\begin{aligned}
0.065(444)(T_f-525)+0.635(4186)(T_f-15)&=0,\\
28.86T_f-15151.5+2658.11T_f-39871.65&=0,\\
2686.97T_f&=55023.15,\\
T_f&=20.4778^\circ\mathrm C.
\end{aligned}$$

4. Check that $15^{\circ} C < T_{f} < 525^{\circ} C$.
5. Calculate heat gained by the water and heat lost by the iron. Both magnitudes are about $14.56\,\mathrm{kJ}$.

**Why the sign works.** Tf−15 is positive; Tf−525 is negative. Both signs come from the same $\Delta T$ definition.
:::

:::example Melt $2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice already at its melting point
**Sources:** Companion p. 10; alternate companion p. 8. Use the problem'$s L_{f} = 334{,}000\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg}}$.

1. Identify a phase change at constant temperature, so use $Q = mL_{f}$.
2. Calculate $Q = 2.0 \left( 334{,}000 \right) = 668{,}000\,\mathrm{J}$.
3. Report $668\,\mathrm{kJ}$ absorbed.

**Check.** There is no $mc\Delta T$ term because the ice starts at 0°C and the question ends with liquid water at 0°C. Freezing the same mass reverses the heat sign.

:::example How much ice cools the cola to 0°C?
**Sources:** Companion p. 11; alternate companion pp. 8–9. **Given:** $0.25\,\mathrm{kg}$ of cola approximated as water at 25°C; ice starts at −20°C. Neglect the glass. Use $c_{\mathrm{water}} = 4190$, $c_{\mathrm{ice}} = 2090\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg}   \mathrm{K}}$, and $L_{f} = 334{,}000\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg}}$.

1. Calculate the energy the cola releases on cooling: $Q_{\mathrm{cola}} = 0.25 \left( 4190 \right) \left( 25 \right) = 26{,}187.5\,\mathrm{J}$.
2. Find the energy needed per kilogram of ice to warm to 0°C: $c_{\mathrm{ice}} \left( 20 \right) = 41{,}800\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg}}$.
3. Add the energy per kilogram needed to melt it: $41{,}800 + 334{,}000 = 375{,}800\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg}}$.
4. Equate the gained and lost energy magnitudes, then isolate the ice mass.

$$m_{ice}=\frac{m_{cola}c_{water}(25-0)}{c_{ice}(0-(-20))+L_f}
=\frac{26187.5}{375800}=0.0696847\ \mathrm{kg}.$$

5. Convert to grams: about 69.7 g, which rounds to the slide's 70 g.

**Boundary check.** This is the ice mass that can just warm and completely melt while the final mixture reaches 0°C. More ice can remain unmelted. Less ice produces a final temperature above 0°C under this model.
:::

### First-law cases, heating curves, and conduction calculations

:::example Separate heat from work in the source's conceptual cases
**Source:** Primary p. 43.

- Soup on an electric stove receives energy through heat transfer from the hot pan.
- Hands near a fire receive thermal radiation; warm-air convection can also contribute.
- A re-entering spacecraft transfers macroscopic mechanical energy into internal energy through interaction with the atmosphere. Compression and drag are central; a hot atmosphere can also exchange heat with the surface.

**Take note.** A temperature increase alone does not tell you whether the energy enters as heat or as work. Specify the system and the transfer mechanism.
:::

:::example Complete the two first-law numerical cases
**Primary p. 44:** $Q = + 3000\,\mathrm{J}$ and $W_{\mathrm{by}} = + 2500\,\mathrm{J}$. Therefore $\Delta U = Q - W_{\mathrm{by}} = + 500\,\mathrm{J}$.

**Primary p. 45:** The net heat is $40 - 25 = + 15\,\mathrm{J}$. The net work by the system is $10 - 4 = + 6\,\mathrm{J}$. Therefore $\Delta U = 15 - 6 = + 9\,\mathrm{J}$.

**Companion p. 8; alternate companion p. 6:** The net heat is $1500 - 400 = 1100\,\mathrm{J}$. The net work on the gas is $- 600 + 250 = - 350\,\mathrm{J}$. Therefore $\Delta U = Q + W_{\mathrm{on}} = 1100 - 350 = + 750\,\mathrm{J}$.

**Check.** The primary and companion sign conventions agree. Their work variables have opposite definitions.
:::

:::derivation Read the slope of a heating curve
During a single phase, $Q = mc\Delta T$. At fixed m and approximately constant c, $\frac{\Delta T}{\Delta Q} = \frac{1}{\left( mc \right)}$. A larger heat capacity produces a flatter temperature-versus-energy slope. During an equilibrium phase change, the added energy changes phase fraction, so the temperature plateau has slope zero.

For heating at constant power P, $Q = Pt$. Thus $\frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{P}{\left( mc \right)}$ within a phase, and a complete phase transition takes $\Delta t = \frac{\mathrm{mL}}{P}$. These relations require negligible energy losses and the stated constant-power model.
:::

:::example Apply the companion's conduction equation
**Source law:** Companion p. 11; alternate companion p. 9. The numbers below form a labeled extension.

**Given:** $k = 0.80\,\frac{\mathrm{W}}{\mathrm{m}   \mathrm{K}}$, $A = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}^{2}$, thickness $L = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}$, and face temperatures 30°C and 10°C.

1. Find $\Delta T = 20\,\mathrm{K}$.
2. Calculate the steady heat rate: $H = \frac{kA\Delta T}{L} = \frac{0.80 \left( 2.0 \right) \left( 20 \right)}{0.10} = 320\,\mathrm{W}$.
3. For $60\,\mathrm{s}$, calculate transferred energy $Q = Ht = 320 \left( 60 \right) = 19{,}200\,\mathrm{J}$.
4. Check the rate unit: $\frac{\frac{W}{\left( m \cdot K \right)} \times m^{2} \times K}{m} = W$.

**Assumptions.** The slab is uniform. The temperature profile has reached steady state. Ignore side losses and contact resistance.
:::
