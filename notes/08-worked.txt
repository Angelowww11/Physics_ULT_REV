### Magnetism, field notation, and the three right-hand rules

**Source context — primary pp. 4–11.** The historical material describes magnetite and the name Magnesia. A magnetized needle aligns with the local field. Either end of a magnet can attract an initially unmagnetized iron nail because the nail becomes magnetized. Splitting a magnet produces smaller dipoles. It does not separate an ordinary bar magnet into isolated north and south poles.

**Earth and a compass.** The geographic poles describe the rotation axis. Magnetic-pole locations and the local field direction differ from that axis. Near geographic north, Earth's field can attract a compass's north-seeking end. Keep the pole-naming convention separate from the field direction used in a force calculation.

| Question | Right-hand operation | What the result means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Field around a straight wire | Point the thumb along conventional current; curl the fingers | Finger curl gives B around the wire |
| Force on a current-carrying segment | Form $L \times B$, with L along conventional current | The cross product gives the force direction |
| Force on a moving positive charge | Form $v \times B$ | The result gives force for positive q |
| Force on a negative charge | Find $v \times B$, then reverse it | Reversal accounts for negative q |

{{figure:m8-main:18|The source's three right-hand rules answer different physical questions.}}

Use a dot ⊙ for a vector out of the page and a cross ⊗ for a vector into the page. In a right-handed coordinate system, $x \times y = z$, $y \times z = x$, and $z \times x = y$. Reversing either factor reverses the cross product.

**Units.** $1\,\mathrm{T} = 1\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{A}   \mathrm{m}} = \frac{1\,\mathrm{N} \cdot s}{\left( C \cdot m \right)} = \frac{1\,\mathrm{Wb}}{m^{2}}$. Also, $1\,\mathrm{T} = 10^{4}$ gauss. Convert $3.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ to $0.0030\,\mathrm{m}$ before using a wire-spacing formula.

:::derivation Wire-force law from the motion of charge carriers
**Source laws:** Primary pp. 12–17. Let the carrier number density be n, wire cross section A, and segment length L. Use a uniform external field.

1. Count the carriers inside the segment: $N_{\mathrm{carriers}} = nAL$.
2. Add their magnetic forces: $F_{\mathrm{total}} = nALq \left( v_{d} \times B \right)$.
3. Use the signed carrier-current relation $nqv_{d} = J$, with conventional-current direction encoded in J.
4. Replace JA by the current and direct the length vector along that current.

$$\mathbf F=I\mathbf L\times\mathbf B,
\qquad F=ILB\sin\theta.$$

This derivation removes the microscopic carrier density and drift speed. L means the length inside the field. For a curved path in a nonuniform field, integrate $I dl \times B$ instead of using one segment's product.
:::

### Solve both source magnetic-force examples in full

:::example Eastward current in a northeast field
**Source:** Primary p. 14. **Given:** $I = 5.0\,\mathrm{A}$ east, $L = 1.0\,\mathrm{m}$, $B = 1.20\,\mathrm{T}$ at 45° north of east.

1. Choose $+x$ for east, $+y$ for north, and $+z$ for up.
2. Resolve B: $Bx = B\cos 45^{\circ}$ and $By = B\sin 45^{\circ}$.
3. The x component is parallel to the current and produces no force.
4. Calculate $F = ILBy = 5.0 \left( 1.0 \right) \left( 1.20 \right) \sin 45^{\circ} = 4.24264\,\mathrm{N}$.
5. Use $+ x \times + y = + z$. The force points upward.

**Check.** The maximum possible magnitude for these I, L, and B values is $ILB = 6.0\,\mathrm{N}$. The 45° result is smaller by $\sin45^\circ$.
:::

:::example Magnetic force on the television-tube electron
**Source:** Primary p. 20. **Given:** $v = 8.0 \times 10^{6}\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}$ along +x, $B = 0.025\,\mathrm{T}$ at 60° to +x in the xy plane. Use $\left| q \right| = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$.

1. Calculate the perpendicular field component: $B_{\mathrm{perp}} = B\sin 60^{\circ} = 0.0216506\,\mathrm{T}$.
2. Calculate $F = \left| q \right|$vBperp ≈ $2.7747 \times 10^{-14}\,\mathrm{N}$.
3. If the field has a positive y component, $v \times B$ points +z.
4. Reverse that result for the electron: F points −z.

**Geometry check.** An angle of 60° to +x alone does not distinguish the +y and −y sides. A negative y component would reverse the force direction. Use the actual drawing's orientation before naming into-page or out-of-page.
:::

:::derivation Recover an unknown field or angle from a force measurement
Start with $F = \left| q \right| vB\sin \theta$ or $F = ILB\sin \theta$. If the geometry is perpendicular, $\sin \theta = 1$.

$$B=\frac{F}{|q|v\sin\theta},\qquad
v=\frac{F}{|q|B\sin\theta},\qquad
\sin\theta=\frac{F}{|q|vB}.$$

For a wire, replace |q|v by IL. A sine value larger than one signals inconsistent data or a wrong equation. In 0°–180°, both $\theta$ and $180^{\circ} - \theta$ have the same sine. Magnitude data alone cannot distinguish those directions. Parallel motion gives zero force even in a nonzero field.
:::

### Torque: derive the area factor and use the actual angle

:::derivation Why a current loop has torque $NIAB\sin \theta$
**Sources:** Primary pp. 25–28. Let a rectangular loop have side length a along one pair of opposite forces and separation b between them.

1. Each active side feels force $F = IaB$ for the perpendicular-field component in the stated geometry.
2. The perpendicular lever arm between the forces is $b\sin \theta$, where $\theta$ measures the area normal relative to B.
3. Add the two moments as a force couple: $\tau = F \left( b\sin \theta \right)$.
4. Substitute $A = ab$ and multiply by N turns.

$$\tau=NIabB\sin\theta=NIAB\sin\theta.$$

Equal and opposite side forces can cancel the net force while their torques add. Primary p. 27's side-force expression “IAB” uses area where a length is needed. Force uses ILB; torque uses NIAB.
:::

:::example Circular coil: maximum, minimum, and an intermediate angle
**Source problem:** Primary p. 28. **Given:** Diameter $0.200\,\mathrm{m}$, $N = 10$, $I = 3.00\,\mathrm{A}$, $B = 2.00\,\mathrm{T}$.

1. Halve the diameter: $r = 0.100\,\mathrm{m}$.
2. Find the one-turn area: $A = \pi r^{2} = 0.0314159\,\mathrm{m}^{2}$.
3. Find $\tau _{\mathrm{max}} = NIAB = 1.88496\,\mathrm{N} \cdot m$.
4. Set the area normal perpendicular to B for the maximum. Set it parallel or antiparallel for $\tau _{\mathrm{min}} = 0$.
5. For a labeled extension at $\theta = 30^{\circ}$ to the normal, $\tau = \tau _{\mathrm{max}}\sin 30^{\circ} = 0.942478\,\mathrm{N} \cdot m$.

**Angle check.** If 30° is measured to the plane instead, $\theta _{\mathrm{normal}} = 60^{\circ}$, giving $\tau \approx 1.63242\,\mathrm{N} \cdot m$. The reference line changes the answer.
:::

:::derivation Magnetic-loop potential energy from the torque
This is a labeled extension of the source's torque model. Keep the current, loop area, and applied field constant, so $\mu = NIA$ stays constant. The magnetic torque turns a loop toward alignment with B. Its signed angular component is $\tau _{\mathrm{mag}} = - \mu B\sin \theta$ when positive $\theta$ increases the normal-to-field angle.

For a quasistatic increase $d\theta$, an external agent supplies $\tau _{\mathrm{external}} = + \mu B\sin \theta$. Thus $dU = \tau _{\mathrm{external}}d\theta$. Integrating and choosing zero energy at $\theta = 90^{\circ}$ gives $U = - \mu B\cos \theta$. Alignment at $\theta = 0$ has minimum energy $- \mu B$. Anti-alignment at $\theta = \pi$ has maximum energy $+ \mu B$. Both have zero torque, but only the first is a stable orientation in this simple model.
:::

**Devices — primary pp. 29–31.** A galvanometer balances magnetic torque against a restoring torque. In a linear spring model, $\kappa _{\mathrm{torsion}}\phi = NIAB$ for a radial-field meter, so its deflection can indicate current. A motor uses continuing electromagnetic torque to produce rotation. A loudspeaker uses a coil's magnetic force to move its attached cone. An ohmmeter also needs an internal source and associated circuitry; the galvanometer alone does not directly measure resistance.

### Source-wire fields, parallel-wire force, and solenoids

:::example Field north of an upward $25\,\mathrm{A}$ wire
**Source:** Primary p. 33. **Given:** $I = 25\,\mathrm{A}$, perpendicular distance $r = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}$, $\mu_{0} = \frac{4 \pi \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T} \cdot m}{A}$.

1. Select the long-straight-wire model: $B = \frac{\mu_{0} I}{\left( 2 \pi r \right)}$.
2. Substitute $B = \frac{\left( 4 \pi \times 10^{-7} \right) \left( 25 \right)}{\left( 2 \pi \times 0.10 \right)} = 5.0 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T}$.
3. Point the right thumb upward along the current.
4. Read the field at the north-side point: it points west.

**Check.** This is $50\,\mathrm{\mu T}$. “Into the page” is correct only for a drawing whose page orientation makes west point inward.
:::

:::derivation Parallel-wire force and its attraction or repulsion
**Sources:** Primary pp. 34–36. Wire 1 creates $B_{1} = \frac{\mu_{0} I_{1}}{\left( 2 \pi d \right)}$ at wire 2. For parallel wires, this field is perpendicular to wire 2. Apply $F_{2} = I_{2} LB_{1}$:

$$F_2=\frac{\mu_0 I_1I_2L}{2\pi d},\qquad
\frac FL=\frac{\mu_0 I_1I_2}{2\pi d}.$$

Use the grip rule for $B_{1}$, then $L_{2} \times B_{1}$ for the second force. Matching current directions give forces toward each other. Opposite current directions give forces away from each other. Repeating the calculation on wire 1 gives the same force magnitude.
:::

:::example Appliance-cord wire force
**Source:** Primary p. 36. **Given:** $I_{1} = I_{2} = 8.0\,\mathrm{A}$, $L = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$, $d = 3.0\,\mathrm{mm} = 0.0030\,\mathrm{m}$.

1. Calculate $\frac{F}{L} = \frac{\left( 4 \pi \times 10^{-7} \right) \left( 8 \right) \left( 8 \right)}{\left( 2 \pi \times 0.0030 \right)} = 0.00426667\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{m}}$.
2. Multiply by $2.0\,\mathrm{m}$: $F = 0.00853333\,\mathrm{N}$.
3. For the supply and return wires of an ordinary DC appliance cord, currents run in opposite directions, so the force is repulsive.

**Check.** Report a force in newtons when L is supplied. Reporting $\frac{F}{L}$ alone omits the requested wire length.
:::

:::derivation Ampere's law inside a long solenoid
**Sources:** Primary pp. 37–42. Use a rectangular Amperian path with an interior segment of length x parallel to the solenoid axis.

1. The transverse path segments have B perpendicular to dl, so their dot-product contributions vanish.
2. Neglect the outside field in the ideal long-solenoid approximation.
3. The interior segment contributes Bx.
4. If $n = \frac{N}{\ell}$ is turns per unit solenoid length, the path encloses nx turns. Its enclosed current is nxI.
5. Apply Ampere's law and cancel x.

$$Bx=\mu_0(nxI)\Rightarrow B=\mu_0nI
=\mu_0\frac{N}{\ell}I.$$

The Amperian-path length x cancels. The physical solenoid length ℓ remains. It is not the total length of wire used in the winding.
:::

:::example The source's 420-turn solenoid
**Source:** Primary p. 39. **Given:** $N = 420$, $\ell = 12\,\mathrm{cm} = 0.12\,\mathrm{m}$, and $I = 2.0\,\mathrm{A}$.

1. Calculate the turn density $n = \frac{N}{\ell} = 3500\,\mathrm{m}^{-1}$.
2. Calculate $B = \left( 4 \pi \times 10^{-7} \right) \left( 3500 \right) \left( 2.0 \right) = 0.00879646\,\mathrm{T}$.
3. Report about 8.80 mT near the center under the long-solenoid model.

**Core check.** A linear material of relative permeability $\mu r$ would multiply this ideal value by $\mu r$. A real iron core can saturate, so a constant multiplier is not universal.
:::
