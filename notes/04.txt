## Electric fields: calculate the field before inserting a test charge

An electric field assigns a force-per-charge vector to each point. It is not merely an area with electricity. A source charge produces its field even when no test particle is present. Define field direction by the force on a positive test charge.

$$\mathbf E=\frac{\mathbf F}{q_0},\quad \mathbf F=q_0\mathbf E,\quad [E]=\mathrm{N/C}=\mathrm{V/m}.$$

For a negative test charge, force opposes E. The source charge sets the field; the test charge sets the force experienced in that field. The ideal test charge is small enough not to rearrange the source distribution significantly.

### Derive the point-charge field and its inverse forms

Substitute Coulomb's force into $E = \frac{F}{q_{0}}$ and cancel the positive test-charge magnitude:

$$E=\frac{k|Q|}{r^2},\quad |Q|=\frac{Er^2}{k},\quad r=\sqrt{\frac{k|Q|}{E}}.$$

E points away from positive Q and toward negative Q. The field magnitude decreases as $\frac{1}{r^{2}}$. The value at $r = 0$ is not finite in the point-charge idealization.

**Worked example — primary p. 12.** A $+ 3.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C}$ test charge experiences $6.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}$. $E = 2.0\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$. Replacing it with $+ 12 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C}$ gives $F = 2.4 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{N}$ in the same direction. Replacing it with a negative charge reverses the force, not the pre-existing field.

**Worked examples — primary pp. 29–30.** A $+ 4.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ source gives $E = 9.0\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$ at $2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ when $k = 9.0 \times 10^{9}$. Conversely, $E = 5\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$ at $1.50\,\mathrm{m}$ requires $\left| Q \right| = 1.25 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C}$. Magnitude alone does not identify the source's sign.

### Field lines, dipoles, and equipattern reasoning

The tangent to a field line gives the local field direction. Denser lines indicate stronger field. Lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges or at infinity. Line counts communicate relative magnitude; they are not physical wires. Electrostatic field lines do not cross because the field has one direction at a regular point.

A dipole has equal and opposite separated charges. Equal like charges can cancel at their midpoint. Equal opposite charges do not cancel their midpoint fields: both point from positive to negative. A curved field line is not generally a charged particle's trajectory; motion also depends on initial velocity and inertia.

### Discrete-source superposition and a midpoint example

$$\mathbf E(\mathbf r)=k\sum_j Q_j\frac{\mathbf r-\mathbf r_j}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_j|^3}.$$

**Worked example — primary p. 32.** Put $- 5.00\,\mathrm{nC}$ on the left and $- 3.00\,\mathrm{nC}$ on the right, $1.50\,\mathrm{m}$ apart. At their midpoint $r = 0.75\,\mathrm{m}$ from each. $E_{\mathrm{left}} = 80\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$ left and $E_{\mathrm{right}} = 48\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$ right. The result is $32\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$ left. Use $0.75\,\mathrm{m}$, not the full separation, for BOTH denominators.

**Derivation — the scanned companion's symmetric three-charge geometry.** Put equal charges q at (0, −a), (0, 0), and (0, +a). Evaluate at (x, 0), with $x > 0$. The upper/lower y components cancel. Each outer charge contributes $\frac{kqx}{\left( x^{2} + a^{2} \right)^{3/2}}$ horizontally. The central charge contributes $\frac{kq}{x^{2}}$. Thus $E_{x} = kq \left[ \frac{1}{x^{2}} + \frac{2 x}{\left( x^{2} + a^{2} \right)^{3/2}} \right]$, $E_{y} = 0$. The signs reverse when q is negative. This result belongs to this stated geometry only.

### Uniform-field motion: derive the trajectory

Combine Newton's law $ma = qE$ with constant-acceleration kinematics. For a constant field along y:

$$a_y=\frac{qE_y}{m},\quad x=x_0+v_{0x}t,\quad y=y_0+v_{0y}t+\frac{qE_y}{2m}t^2.$$

When $v_{0x} \ne 0$, eliminate $t = \frac{\left( x - x_{0} \right)}{v_{0}} x$. The resulting y(x) is parabolic. An electron has $q = - e$; do not substitute +e for its acceleration. A particle initially moving along E can speed up or slow down depending on q and its velocity direction.

**Worked example.** An electron enters horizontally at $2.0 \times 10^{6}\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}$ through a $0.020\,\mathrm{m}$ long uniform-field region. Let $E_{y} = + 100\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$ and $y_{0} = v_{0y} = 0$. Transit time: $1.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s}$. $a_{y} = - 1.759 \times 10^{13}\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}^{2}}$. Exit displacement: $- 8.79 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}$ and $v_{y} = - 1.759 \times 10^{5}\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}$. A plate collision invalidates the full-length transit assumption. Relativistic speeds require relativistic dynamics.

### Continuous charge distributions: a labeled extension

The primary objectives mention continuous distributions but do not develop all standard integrations. Use $dq = \lambda dl$ for a line, $\sigma dA$ for a surface, or $\rho_{q} dV$ for a volume. Here $\rho_{q}$ is charge density, not mass density.

$$\mathbf E=k\int\frac{\hat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}\,dq.$$

**Uniform finite rod.** At perpendicular distance a from the midpoint of a rod extending from −L to +L, transverse components add and longitudinal components cancel. Integrate $dE \perp = \frac{k\lambda a dx}{\left( x^{2} + a^{2} \right)^{3/2}}$ to obtain $E = \frac{2 k\lambda L}{\left[ a \sqrt{\left( L^{2} + a^{2} \right)} \right]}$. As $L \to \infty$, $E \to \frac{2 k\lambda}{a}$. The result must tend toward the point-charge field $\frac{k \left( 2 \lambda L \right)}{a^{2}}$ when a ≫ L.

The antiderivative follows from $\frac{d \left[ \frac{x}{\sqrt{\left( x^{2} + a^{2} \right)}} \right]}{dx} = \frac{a^{2}}{\left( x^{2} + a^{2} \right)^{3/2}}$. With $a > 0$ and $\lambda \ge 0$, the outward field magnitude is:

$$E_\perp=k\lambda a\left[\frac{x}{a^2\sqrt{x^2+a^2}}\right]_{-L}^{L}=\frac{2k\lambda L}{a\sqrt{L^2+a^2}}.$$

For $\lambda = \frac{2.0\,\mathrm{nC}}{m}$, $L = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}$, and $a = 0.40\,\mathrm{m}$, $E = 53.94\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$. The total rod length is $2 L = 0.60\,\mathrm{m}$. Confusing L with the total length changes both the charge and the geometry.

**Uniform ring.** At axial distance x from a ring of radius R, each element has distance $\sqrt{\left( x^{2} + R^{2} \right)}$. Radial components cancel. Integrating the axial part gives $E_{x} = \frac{kQx}{\left( x^{2} + R^{2} \right)^{3/2}}$. At $x = 0$, $E = 0$ although the potential is generally not zero.

**Disk and infinite-sheet limits.** Build a uniformly charged disk from rings with $dQ = \sigma 2 \pi r dr$. For $x > 0$, $E_{x} = \frac{\sigma}{\left( 2 \varepsilon_{0} \right)} \left[ 1 - \frac{x}{\sqrt{\left( x^{2} + R^{2} \right)}} \right]$. Let $R \to \infty$ to obtain $E = \frac{\sigma}{\left( 2 \varepsilon_{0} \right)}$ for one isolated infinite sheet. Two oppositely charged ideal capacitor sheets add to $\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_{0}}$ between them. Do not confuse the one-sheet and two-sheet factors.

The disk integration keeps the ring radius r separate from the fixed axial distance x:

$$E_x=2\pi k\sigma x\int_0^R\frac{r\,dr}{(x^2+r^2)^{3/2}}=2\pi k\sigma x\left[-\frac1{\sqrt{x^2+r^2}}\right]_0^R.$$

For $x > 0$, the lower endpoint uses $\sqrt{\left( x^{2} \right)} = x$. Substitute $k = \frac{1}{\left( 4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} \right)}$ to recover the stated disk result. On the opposite side, the normal field direction reverses for the same positive surface charge.
