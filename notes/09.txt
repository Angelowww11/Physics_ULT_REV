## Induction: change in flux, not merely the presence of a magnet

An induced emf can exist without a closed conducting circuit. A closed path is required for the ordinary induced current examples. A stationary loop in a time-varying magnetic field can have emf even when no wire moves. Conversely, motion that leaves the relevant flux unchanged need not produce a net loop emf.

### Magnetic flux and all three routes to a changing flux

$$\Phi_B=\int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf A,\quad \Phi_B=BA\cos\theta\ \text{for uniform }B.$$

The angle is between B and the AREA NORMAL. If a problem gives the angle to the plane, use the complementary angle. One weber is one $T \cdot m^{2}$. A field perpendicular to the plane produces maximum flux magnitude; a field parallel to the plane produces zero flux.

If B occupies only an overlap area, integrate over that region. Primary p. 10 has flux $BA_{2}$, not $BA_{1}$, when the field is confined to the smaller perpendicular overlap area $A_{2}$. Treat “zero outside the field region” explicitly.

**Worked example — primary p. 11.** A $0.100\,\mathrm{m}$ square loop in $1.25\,\mathrm{T}$ has $A = 0.0100\,\mathrm{m}^{2}$. Maximum magnitude: $0.0125\,\mathrm{Wb}$; minimum magnitude: $0$. Signed flux can range from −0.0125 to $+ 0.0125\,\mathrm{Wb}$ depending on the chosen normal.

### Faraday's law and the full product derivative

Faraday's law is a fundamental induction law in this course. For N turns linking the same flux, $\mathcal{E} = - \frac{N d\Phi B}{dt}$. For nonidentical linked fluxes, sum the flux linkages instead of assuming $N\Phi$. The finite average magnitude is $\frac{N \left| \Delta \Phi \right|}{\Delta t}$; it equals the instantaneous value only for a constant rate during the interval.

$$\mathcal E=-N\left(A\cos\theta\frac{dB}{dt}+B\cos\theta\frac{dA}{dt}-BA\sin\theta\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right).$$

This product-rule expression covers changing field strength, changing area, changing orientation, and simultaneous changes. Set only the quantities explicitly held constant to zero.

**Worked example — primary p. 18.** $N = 200$; square side: $0.180\,\mathrm{m}$; $R = 2.0 \Omega$; $\Delta B = 0.500\,\mathrm{T}$ in $0.80\,\mathrm{s}$, with perpendicular field. $A = 0.0324\,\mathrm{m}^{2}$. $\left| \mathcal{E} \right| = \frac{200 \left( 0.0324 \right) \left( 0.500 \right)}{0.80} = 4.05\,\mathrm{V}$. Current magnitude: $\frac{4.05}{2.0} = 2.025\,\mathrm{A}$. The source's $4.1\,\mathrm{V}$ is rounded.

### Lenz's law with four direction cases

Choose the external field direction, then determine whether its flux increases or decreases. The induced field OPPOSES THE CHANGE, not necessarily the external field. Use the right-hand grip rule to convert the required induced field into conventional current.

| External flux seen by the observer | Required induced field | Induced current as seen by that observer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Into page, increasing | Out of page | Counterclockwise |
| Into page, decreasing | Into page | Clockwise |
| Out of page, increasing | Into page | Clockwise |
| Out of page, decreasing | Out of page | Counterclockwise |

Primary p. 28 prints a decreasing inward-field example with the wrong outward induced field. For its stated inward and decreasing flux, the correction is inward induced field and clockwise current. Always check the actual loop position and arrow before applying a memorized wire-direction answer.

### Motional emf from two derivations

**Flux derivation.** A rod of length ℓ slides distance dx in a perpendicular uniform B. The loop's area change is $dA = \ell dx$, so $\left| \mathcal{E} \right| = B \ell \left( \frac{dx}{dt} \right) = B \ell v$.

**Charge-force derivation.** Charges in an open moving rod redistribute until qE balances the magnetic qvB. Thus $E = vB$, and voltage magnitude $E \ell = B \ell v$. The general expression is $\int \left( v \times B \right) \cdot dl$; the product Bℓv needs the stated perpendicular geometry.

For a sliding rod in a closed resistance R: $I = \frac{B \ell v}{R}$. Magnetic drag magnitude is $F = I \ell B = \frac{B^{2} \ell^{2} v}{R}$. Required mechanical power at constant speed is $Fv = I^{2} R$, which verifies energy conservation.

**Worked example — primary p. 35.** Speed $\frac{1000\,\mathrm{km}}{h} = 277.778\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}$. A $70\,\mathrm{m}$ wingspan moving perpendicular to a nearly vertical $5.0 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T}$ field develops about $0.9722\,\mathrm{V}$ between tips. Which tip is positive depends on the velocity and field vector directions.

### Generators, microphones, transformers, and eddy currents

For a rigid rotating N-turn coil, $\Phi = BA\cos \left( \omega t + \phi \right)$. Differentiate to derive $\mathcal{E} \left( t \right) = NBA\omega \sin \left( \omega t + \phi \right)$, with peak $NBA\omega$. A GENERATOR converts mechanical input into electrical output. Primary p. 14 reverses that conversion in its wording; it describes motor action instead. A moving-coil microphone converts mechanical vibration into an electrical signal.

An ideal transformer links both windings to the same changing core flux. Dividing their Faraday equations gives $\frac{V_{s}}{V_{p}} = \frac{N_{s}}{N_{p}}$. With ideal power conservation, $V_{p} I_{p} = V_{s} I_{s}$ and $\frac{I_{s}}{I_{p}} = \frac{N_{p}}{N_{s}}$. These are compatible AC magnitudes, usually rms values. A transformer does not directly convert AC into DC; a rectifier and other power-supply stages are required.

**Worked example — primary p. 19.** Ideal $120\,\mathrm{V}$ to $9\,\mathrm{V}$ AC, $N_{s} = 30$, $I_{s} = 0.400\,\mathrm{A}$ gives $N_{p} = 400$ turns, output power: $3.6\,\mathrm{W}$, and input current: $0.030\,\mathrm{A}$. The slide's description of $9\,\mathrm{V}$ DC includes an implicit rectifier; the transformer equations alone address the AC stage.

Eddy currents circulate in bulk conductors. Their opposing magnetic effects can slow moving metal. Energy becomes heat. A changing current in one coil can induce emf in a nearby coil even without a conducting connection between them.

### Mutual/self-inductance and stored magnetic energy

Define mutual inductance through $\mathcal{E}_{2} = - \frac{M dI_{1}}{dt}$. For a linear coil, flux linkage $N\Phi = LI$, giving $\mathcal{E} _{\mathrm{self}} = - \frac{L dI}{dt}$. L and M have units henry: $\frac{V \cdot s}{A} = \Omega \cdot s$. Constant permeability and fixed geometry are part of the simple constant-L model.

For an ideal long solenoid, $B = \frac{\mu NI}{\ell}$. Flux per turn is BA. Therefore $L = \frac{N\Phi}{I} = \frac{\mathrm{\mu N}^{2} A}{\ell}$. Derive stored energy by $P = LI \left( \frac{dI}{dt} \right)$ and integrate: $U = \int_{0} ᴵ Li di = \frac{LI^{2}}{2}$. Inductors resist a CHANGE in current; they do not simply “oppose current” in every situation.

Substitute $L = \frac{\mathrm{\mu N}^{2} A}{\ell}$ into $U = \frac{LI^{2}}{2}$ and replace $\frac{NI}{\ell}$ with $\frac{B}{\mu}$. This gives $U = \frac{B^{2} A \ell}{\left( 2 \mu \right)}$. Divide by the solenoid's internal field volume Aℓ to get magnetic energy density $u_{B} = \frac{B^{2}}{\left( 2 \mu \right)}$. Compare the electric result $u_{E} = \frac{\varepsilon E^{2}}{2}$. Both formulas assume the specified linear material model.

### RL growth, decay, and arbitrary initial current

KVL for an ideal L in series with total R gives $\mathcal{E} = L \left( \frac{dI}{dt} \right) + RI$. Separate and integrate with $I \left( 0 \right) = 0$:

$$I(t)=\frac{\mathcal E}{R}(1-e^{-tR/L}),\quad \tau_{RL}=L/R.$$

After removing the source while retaining a closed R–L path, $I = I_{0} \exp \left( - \frac{t R}{L} \right)$. More generally $I(t)=I_f+(I_0-I_f)e^{-t/\tau}$. Inductor current is continuous in this ideal finite-voltage model. At DC steady state, an ideal inductor has zero voltage; real winding resistance remains.

For fraction f of final growth, $t = - \left( \frac{L}{R} \right) \ln \left( 1 - f \right)$. For remaining decay fraction f, $t = - \left( \frac{L}{R} \right) \ln f$. RC increases its time constant with R; RL decreases its time constant with R. Do not interchange them.

**Worked example — primary p. 44.** $L = 87.5\,\mathrm{mH} = 0.0875\,\mathrm{H}$ and $R = 0.250 \Omega$. $\tau = 0.350\,\mathrm{s}$. Growth reaches $1 - e^{-1} = 63.212$% at $0.350\,\mathrm{s}$. Exactly 63% requires $- 0.350 \ln \left( 0.37 \right) \approx 0.348\,\mathrm{s}$. The printed “63%” convention is a rounding approximation.

**Show the RL integration.** Rearrange the loop equation to $\frac{dI}{\left( \mathcal{E} - RI \right)} = \frac{dt}{L}$. Integrate using $I \left( 0 \right) = 0$. The left side contributes $- \frac{\ln \left[ \frac{\left( \mathcal{E} - RI \right)}{\mathcal{E}} \right]}{R}$. Therefore:

$$-\ln\left(1-\frac{RI}{\mathcal E}\right)=\frac{Rt}{L}\Rightarrow I=\frac{\mathcal E}{R}(1-e^{-Rt/L}).$$

For $\mathcal{E} = 12\,\mathrm{V}$, $R = 6 \Omega$, and $L = 3\,\mathrm{H}$, $\tau = 0.5\,\mathrm{s}$ and $I_{\mathrm{final}} = 2\,\mathrm{A}$. At one time constant, $I \approx 1.26424\,\mathrm{A}$. The resistor drop is $7.58545\,\mathrm{V}$; the inductor drop is $\mathcal{E} e^{-1} \approx 4.41455\,\mathrm{V}$. Their sum remains $12\,\mathrm{V}$. Initial current is zero, while the initial inductor drop is the full $12\,\mathrm{V}$. At long times, the ideal inductor drop tends to zero and stored energy tends to $\tfrac{1}{2} \left( 3 \right) \left( 2^{2} \right) = 6\,\mathrm{J}$.

**Switching check.** A source-free decay needs a closed path. Opening the only path does not make a real inductor's current disappear without consequence. Its induced voltage can become large and cause a spark or flow through a protection component. The simple exponential requires a valid R–L discharge path.
