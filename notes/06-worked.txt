### Derive capacitance with the charge, geometry, and units visible

:::derivation From the plate fields to C = εA/d
**Sources:** Primary pp. 7–13; companion pp. 1–4. Use large parallel plates, a uniform linear dielectric, and negligible edge effects.

1. Write the free surface-charge density as σ = Q/A.
2. Add the two ideal plate fields. The gap field is E = σ/ε, not σ/(2ε).
3. Use the uniform-gap potential difference magnitude V = Ed.
4. Substitute σ = Q/A to obtain V = Qd/(εA).
5. Divide charge by voltage: C = Q/V = εA/d.

**Unit check.** εA/d has units (F/m)(m²)/m = F. The dielectric constant κ = ε/ε₀ has no unit. Do not confuse κ with Coulomb's constant k.
:::

{{figure:m6-main:12|The primary dielectric-constant table at 20°C, also reproduced in the companion.}}

The table gives κ = 1 for vacuum and about 1.00059 for air at 1 atm. The usual classroom approximation uses air ≈ vacuum. Listed examples include Teflon 2.1, polyethylene 2.25, glass 5–10, and water 80.4. Capacitance depends on both geometry and the dielectric, not geometry alone when materials change.

:::example Complete the primary capacitor geometry calculations
**Source:** Primary p. 14. Use ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m.

**First problem: 1.0 F with a 1.0 mm vacuum gap.**

1. Convert d = 1.0 × 10⁻³ m.
2. Rearrange C = ε₀A/d to A = Cd/ε₀.
3. Calculate A = (1.0)(1.0 × 10⁻³)/(8.85 × 10⁻¹²) = 1.12994 × 10⁸ m².

**Second problem: A = 2.0 m², d = 5.0 mm, and V = 10,000 V.**

1. Convert d = 0.0050 m.
2. Calculate C = ε₀A/d = 3.54 × 10⁻⁹ F = 3.54 nF = 0.00354 μF.
3. Calculate Q = CV = 3.54 × 10⁻⁵ C = 35.4 μC on either plate in magnitude.
4. Calculate E = V/d = 2.0 × 10⁶ V/m.
5. Check the field independently: E = Q/(ε₀A) gives the same result.

**Check.** The plate pair's algebraic net charge is zero. C = Q/V uses one plate's charge magnitude, not that zero total.
:::

:::example The omitted companion dielectric calculation
**Source:** Companion p. 4. **Given:** A = 0.020 m², d = 2.0 mm, κ = 4.0. Approximate the initial air-filled gap as vacuum.

1. Convert d = 0.0020 m.
2. Calculate C₀ = ε₀A/d = 8.85 × 10⁻¹¹ F = 88.5 pF.
3. Multiply by the dielectric constant: C = κC₀ = 4.0(88.5) = 354 pF.

**Source correction.** The printed 353 pF does not follow from 88.5 pF and κ = 4.0. These stated classroom inputs give 354 pF. The presence of a battery does not change the geometric capacitance calculation; it changes which charge or voltage stays fixed.
:::

:::derivation Plate separation changes: connected and isolated cases
These are labeled consequences of the source's parallel-plate law. Keep plate area and dielectric fixed. Change the gap from d to sd, with s > 0.

| Quantity | Ideal battery connected | Isolated plates, no leakage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cnew/Cinitial | 1/s | 1/s |
| Vnew/Vinitial | 1 | s |
| Qnew/Qinitial | 1/s | 1 |
| Enew/Einitial | 1/s | 1 |
| Unew/Uinitial | 1/s | s |

Derive each row from C ∝ 1/d, Q = CV, E = V/d, and U = Q²/(2C) or CV²/2. Moving isolated charged plates apart increases field energy because external mechanical work enters the system. Do not use the dielectric-insertion ratios for a plate-gap change.
:::

### Restore every capacitor in the source networks

:::example Two capacitors in series or parallel: full branch values
**Sources:** Primary p. 24; companion p. 6. **Given:** 6.0 μF and 3.0 μF across 18.0 V.

**Series solution.**

1. Calculate Ceq = (6×3)/(6+3) = 2.0 μF.
2. Calculate the common series charge Q = CeqV = 2.0(18.0) = 36.0 μC.
3. Find V₆ = Q/C₆ = 36/6 = 6 V and V₃ = 36/3 = 12 V.
4. Check V₆+V₃ = 18 V.

**Parallel solution.**

1. Calculate Ceq = 6+3 = 9.0 μF.
2. Set both branch voltages to 18.0 V.
3. Calculate Q₆ = 6(18) = 108 μC and Q₃ = 3(18) = 54 μC.
4. Check Qtotal = 162 μC = CeqV.

For the other primary problem, 5.0 μF and 12.0 μF in parallel across 9.0 V give Ceq = 17.0 μF. Each has 9.0 V. Their charges are 45 μC and 108 μC, totaling 153 μC.
:::

{{figure:m6-main:25|The original five-capacitor network. The 9 μF element is in series with the entire upper group.}}

:::example Five-capacitor network at 28 V: every branch solved
**Sources:** Primary p. 25; companion p. 6. The upper group has 3 μF and 11 μF branches beside a series pair of 12 μF and 6 μF. This group connects in series with 9 μF between a and b.

1. Reduce the right series pair: Cright = (12×6)/(12+6) = 4 μF.
2. Add the three parallel branches: Cupper = 3+11+4 = 18 μF.
3. Combine 18 μF in series with 9 μF: Ceq = (18×9)/(18+9) = 6 μF.
4. Apply 28 V: Qseries = CeqVab = 6(28) = 168 μC.
5. Recover group voltages: Vupper = 168/18 = 28/3 V; V₉ = 168/9 = 56/3 V.
6. Use the shared upper voltage for each upper parallel branch.
7. Use the shared right-branch charge for its 12 μF and 6 μF capacitors.

| Capacitor | Voltage magnitude, V | Charge magnitude, μC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 μF | 28/3 ≈ 9.3333 | 28 |
| 11 μF | 28/3 ≈ 9.3333 | 308/3 ≈ 102.6667 |
| 12 μF | 28/9 ≈ 3.1111 | 112/3 ≈ 37.3333 |
| 6 μF | 56/9 ≈ 6.2222 | 112/3 ≈ 37.3333 |
| 9 μF | 56/3 ≈ 18.6667 | 168 |

**Three checks.** The right-pair voltages sum to 28/3 V. The upper branch charges sum to 168 μC. The upper-group and 9 μF voltages sum to 28 V. The full network stores U = ½CeqV² = 2352 μJ.

**Common error.** The 12 μF and 6 μF elements each carry the right branch's charge, not the full network's 168 μC.
:::

### Energy, dielectric insertion, and capacitor applications

:::example Evaluate the source energy and charge questions
**Source:** Primary p. 16.

1. Convert C = 5.80 μF to 5.80 × 10⁻⁶ F and use V = 400 V.
2. Calculate U = ½CV² = ½(5.80 × 10⁻⁶)(400²) = 0.464 J.
3. For the separate 5.0 μF, 1.5 V problem, use Q = CV = 7.5 μC.

**Check.** Squaring the voltage squares its factor of ten. The first problem's plate separation is not needed once C and V are already supplied.
:::

:::derivation Battery work and stored energy during dielectric insertion
This is a labeled extension. Initially C₀ and V₀ describe the capacitor. Insert a dielectric of constant κ quasistatically into the entire fixed gap, while an ideal battery stays connected.

1. Find ΔQ = (κ−1)C₀V₀ from Q = CV.
2. Calculate battery energy supplied: Wbattery = V₀ΔQ = (κ−1)C₀V₀².
3. Calculate the increase in stored field energy: ΔU = ½(κ−1)C₀V₀².
4. Under the ideal loss-free quasistatic model, the remaining half leaves as mechanical work by the field on the dielectric.

$$W_{field,mech}=W_{battery}-\Delta U
=\frac12(\kappa-1)C_0V_0^2.$$

For an isolated capacitor, the battery supplies no energy. Its field energy decreases from Q₀²/(2C₀) to Q₀²/(2κC₀). The decrease can become mechanical work. State the electrical connection before applying energy conservation.
:::

**Applications — primary p. 26.** A camera flash stores energy over a longer charging interval and releases it over a shorter interval. The same energy released in less time gives greater average power, Pavg = ΔU/Δt. Capacitors also smooth changing supply voltages and provide brief charge pulses. Their circuit role depends on the connection, not merely the presence of a capacitor symbol.
