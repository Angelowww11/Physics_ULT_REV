### Derive capacitance with the charge, geometry, and units visible

:::derivation From the plate fields to $C = \frac{\varepsilon A}{d}$
**Sources:** Primary pp. 7–13; companion pp. 1–4. Use large parallel plates, a uniform linear dielectric, and negligible edge effects.

1. Write the free surface-charge density as $\sigma = \frac{Q}{A}$.
2. Add the two ideal plate fields. The gap field is $E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}$, not $\frac{\sigma}{\left( 2 \varepsilon \right)}$.
3. Use the uniform-gap potential difference magnitude $V = Ed$.
4. Substitute $\sigma = \frac{Q}{A}$ to obtain $V = \frac{Qd}{\left( \varepsilon A \right)}$.
5. Divide charge by voltage: $C = \frac{Q}{V} = \frac{\varepsilon A}{d}$.

**Unit check.** $\frac{\varepsilon A}{d}$ has units $\frac{\left( \frac{F}{m} \right) \left( m^{2} \right)}{m} = F$. The dielectric constant $\kappa = \frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_{0}}$ has no unit. Do not confuse $\kappa$ with Coulomb's constant k.
:::

{{figure:m6-main:12|The primary dielectric-constant table at 20°C, also reproduced in the companion.}}

The table gives $\kappa = 1$ for vacuum and about 1.00059 for air at $1\,\mathrm{atm}$. The usual classroom approximation uses air ≈ vacuum. Listed examples include Teflon 2.1, polyethylene 2.25, glass 5–10, and water 80.4. Capacitance depends on both geometry and the dielectric, not geometry alone when materials change.

:::example Complete the primary capacitor geometry calculations
**Source:** Primary p. 14. Use $\varepsilon_{0} = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\,\frac{\mathrm{F}}{\mathrm{m}}$.

**First problem: $1.0\,\mathrm{F}$ with a $1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ vacuum gap.**

1. Convert $d = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}$.
2. Rearrange $C = \frac{\varepsilon_{0} A}{d}$ to $A = \frac{Cd}{\varepsilon_{0}}$.
3. Calculate $A = \frac{\left( 1.0 \right) \left( 1.0 \times 10^{-3} \right)}{\left( 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \right)} = 1.12994 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m}^{2}$.

**Second problem: $A = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}^{2}$, $d = 5.0\,\mathrm{mm}$, and $V = 10{,}000\,\mathrm{V}$.**

1. Convert $d = 0.0050\,\mathrm{m}$.
2. Calculate $C = \frac{\varepsilon_{0} A}{d} = 3.54 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{F} = 3.54\,\mathrm{nF} = 0.00354\,\mathrm{\mu F}$.
3. Calculate $Q = CV = 3.54 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{C} = 35.4\,\mathrm{\mu C}$ on either plate in magnitude.
4. Calculate $E = \frac{V}{d} = 2.0 \times 10^{6}\,\frac{\mathrm{V}}{\mathrm{m}}$.
5. Check the field independently: $E = \frac{Q}{\left( \varepsilon_{0} A \right)}$ gives the same result.

**Check.** The plate pair's algebraic net charge is zero. $C = \frac{Q}{V}$ uses one plate's charge magnitude, not that zero total.
:::

:::example The omitted companion dielectric calculation
**Source:** Companion p. 4. **Given:** $A = 0.020\,\mathrm{m}^{2}$, $d = 2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$, $\kappa = 4.0$. Approximate the initial air-filled gap as vacuum.

1. Convert $d = 0.0020\,\mathrm{m}$.
2. Calculate $C_{0} = \frac{\varepsilon_{0} A}{d} = 8.85 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{F} = 88.5\,\mathrm{pF}$.
3. Multiply by the dielectric constant: $C = \kappa C_{0} = 4.0 \left( 88.5 \right) = 354\,\mathrm{pF}$.

**Source correction.** The printed $353\,\mathrm{pF}$ does not follow from $88.5\,\mathrm{pF}$ and $\kappa = 4.0$. These stated classroom inputs give $354\,\mathrm{pF}$. The presence of a battery does not change the geometric capacitance calculation; it changes which charge or voltage stays fixed.
:::

:::derivation Plate separation changes: connected and isolated cases
These are labeled consequences of the source's parallel-plate law. Keep plate area and dielectric fixed. Change the gap from d to sd, with $s > 0$.

| Quantity | Ideal battery connected | Isolated plates, no leakage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\frac{C_{\mathrm{new}}}{C_{\mathrm{initial}}}$ | $\frac{1}{s}$ | $\frac{1}{s}$ |
| $\frac{V_{\mathrm{new}}}{V_{\mathrm{initial}}}$ | 1 | s |
| $\frac{Q_{\mathrm{new}}}{Q_{\mathrm{initial}}}$ | $\frac{1}{s}$ | 1 |
| $\frac{E_{\mathrm{new}}}{E_{\mathrm{initial}}}$ | $\frac{1}{s}$ | 1 |
| $\frac{U_{\mathrm{new}}}{U_{\mathrm{initial}}}$ | $\frac{1}{s}$ | s |

Derive each row from $C \propto \frac{1}{d}$, $Q = CV$, $E = \frac{V}{d}$, and $U = \frac{Q^{2}}{\left( 2\,\mathrm{C} \right)}$ or $\frac{CV^{2}}{2}$. Moving isolated charged plates apart increases field energy because external mechanical work enters the system. Do not use the dielectric-insertion ratios for a plate-gap change.
:::

### Restore every capacitor in the source networks

:::example Two capacitors in series or parallel: full branch values
**Sources:** Primary p. 24; companion p. 6. **Given:** $6.0\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ and $3.0\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ across $18.0\,\mathrm{V}$.

**Series solution.**

1. Calculate $C_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{\left( 6 \times 3 \right)}{\left( 6 + 3 \right)} = 2.0\,\mathrm{\mu F}$.
2. Calculate the common series charge $Q = C_{\mathrm{eq}}V = 2.0 \left( 18.0 \right) = 36.0\,\mathrm{\mu C}$.
3. Find $V_{6} = \frac{Q}{C_{6}} = \frac{36}{6} = 6\,\mathrm{V}$ and $V_{3} = \frac{36}{3} = 12\,\mathrm{V}$.
4. Check $V_{6} + V_{3} = 18\,\mathrm{V}$.

**Parallel solution.**

1. Calculate $C_{\mathrm{eq}} = 6 + 3 = 9.0\,\mathrm{\mu F}$.
2. Set both branch voltages to $18.0\,\mathrm{V}$.
3. Calculate $Q_{6} = 6 \left( 18 \right) = 108\,\mathrm{\mu C}$ and $Q_{3} = 3 \left( 18 \right) = 54\,\mathrm{\mu C}$.
4. Check $Q_{\mathrm{total}} = 162\,\mathrm{\mu C} = C_{\mathrm{eq}}V$.

For the other primary problem, $5.0\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ and $12.0\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ in parallel across $9.0\,\mathrm{V}$ give $C_{\mathrm{eq}} = 17.0\,\mathrm{\mu F}$. Each has $9.0\,\mathrm{V}$. Their charges are $45\,\mathrm{\mu C}$ and $108\,\mathrm{\mu C}$, totaling $153\,\mathrm{\mu C}$.
:::

{{figure:m6-main:25|The original five-capacitor network. The 9 μF element is in series with the entire upper group.}}

:::example Five-capacitor network at $28\,\mathrm{V}$: every branch solved
**Sources:** Primary p. 25; companion p. 6. The upper group has $3\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ and $11\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ branches beside a series pair of $12\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ and $6\,\mathrm{\mu F}$. This group connects in series with $9\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ between a and b.

1. Reduce the right series pair: $C_{\mathrm{right}} = \frac{\left( 12 \times 6 \right)}{\left( 12 + 6 \right)} = 4\,\mathrm{\mu F}$.
2. Add the three parallel branches: $C_{\mathrm{upper}} = 3 + 11 + 4 = 18\,\mathrm{\mu F}$.
3. Combine $18\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ in series with $9\,\mathrm{\mu F}$: $C_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{\left( 18 \times 9 \right)}{\left( 18 + 9 \right)} = 6\,\mathrm{\mu F}$.
4. Apply $28\,\mathrm{V}$: $Q_{\mathrm{series}} = C_{\mathrm{eq}}V_{ab} = 6 \left( 28 \right) = 168\,\mathrm{\mu C}$.
5. Recover group voltages: $V_{\mathrm{upper}} = \frac{168}{18} = \frac{28}{3} V$; $V_{9} = \frac{168}{9} = \frac{56}{3} V$.
6. Use the shared upper voltage for each upper parallel branch.
7. Use the shared right-branch charge for its $12\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ and $6\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ capacitors.

| Capacitor | Voltage magnitude, V | Charge magnitude, $\mathrm{\mu C}$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $3\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ | $\frac{28}{3} \approx 9.3333$ | 28 |
| $11\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ | $\frac{28}{3} \approx 9.3333$ | $\frac{308}{3} \approx 102.6667$ |
| $12\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ | $\frac{28}{9} \approx 3.1111$ | $\frac{112}{3} \approx 37.3333$ |
| $6\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ | $\frac{56}{9} \approx 6.2222$ | $\frac{112}{3} \approx 37.3333$ |
| $9\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ | $\frac{56}{3} \approx 18.6667$ | 168 |

**Three checks.** The right-pair voltages sum to $\frac{28}{3} V$. The upper branch charges sum to $168\,\mathrm{\mu C}$. The upper-group and $9\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ voltages sum to $28\,\mathrm{V}$. The full network stores $U = \tfrac{1}{2} C_{\mathrm{eq}}V^{2} = 2352\,\mathrm{\mu J}$.

**Common error.** The $12\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ and $6\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ elements each carry the right branch's charge, not the full network's $168\,\mathrm{\mu C}$.
:::

### Energy, dielectric insertion, and capacitor applications

:::example Evaluate the source energy and charge questions
**Source:** Primary p. 16.

1. Convert $C = 5.80\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ to $5.80 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{F}$ and use $V = 400\,\mathrm{V}$.
2. Calculate $U = \tfrac{1}{2} CV^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2} \left( 5.80 \times 10^{-6} \right) \left( 400^{2} \right) = 0.464\,\mathrm{J}$.
3. For the separate $5.0\,\mathrm{\mu F}$, $1.5\,\mathrm{V}$ problem, use $Q = CV = 7.5\,\mathrm{\mu C}$.

**Check.** Squaring the voltage squares its factor of ten. The first problem's plate separation is not needed once C and V are already supplied.
:::

:::derivation Battery work and stored energy during dielectric insertion
This is a labeled extension. Initially $C_{0}$ and $V_{0}$ describe the capacitor. Insert a dielectric of constant $\kappa$ quasistatically into the entire fixed gap, while an ideal battery stays connected.

1. Find $\Delta Q = \left( \kappa - 1 \right) C_{0} V_{0}$ from $Q = CV$.
2. Calculate battery energy supplied: $W_{\mathrm{battery}} = V_{0} \Delta Q = \left( \kappa - 1 \right) C_{0} V_{0}^{2}$.
3. Calculate the increase in stored field energy: $\Delta U = \tfrac{1}{2} \left( \kappa - 1 \right) C_{0} V_{0}^{2}$.
4. Under the ideal loss-free quasistatic model, the remaining half leaves as mechanical work by the field on the dielectric.

$$W_{field,mech}=W_{battery}-\Delta U
=\frac12(\kappa-1)C_0V_0^2.$$

For an isolated capacitor, the battery supplies no energy. Its field energy decreases from $\frac{Q_{0}^{2}}{\left( 2 C_{0} \right)}$ to $\frac{Q_{0}^{2}}{\left( 2 \kappa C_{0} \right)}$. The decrease can become mechanical work. State the electrical connection before applying energy conservation.
:::

**Applications — primary p. 26.** A camera flash stores energy over a longer charging interval and releases it over a shorter interval. The same energy released in less time gives greater average power, $P_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{\Delta U}{\Delta t}$. Capacitors also smooth changing supply voltages and provide brief charge pulses. Their circuit role depends on the connection, not merely the presence of a capacitor symbol.
