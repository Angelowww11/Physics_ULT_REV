### Situational problems: choose the law, then solve

These added practice problems apply the module concepts. They are not copied examination questions. Keep the stated assumptions when you use each result.

#### Situation 1: Field cancellation is not source absence

Two equal positive charges lie on opposite sides of a midpoint. The electric field at the midpoint is zero. What follows for a test particle placed exactly there?

1. **Add the source field vectors.**

   $$\vec E_{\mathrm{net}}=\vec E_1+\vec E_2=0$$

2. **Apply the field to a test charge.**

   $$\vec F=q\vec E_{\mathrm{net}}=0$$

3. **Separate three questions.** Net field, individual contributions, and stability after displacement are distinct.

4. **Avoid a potential shortcut.** Field cancellation does not imply zero scalar potential.

**Answer:** The electric force there is zero, but that alone does not establish stable equilibrium.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **No source charges can exist nearby.** — A zero vector sum does not require each contribution to vanish.
- **Each source exerts zero force individually.** — The nonzero contributions cancel.
- **The point must have zero electric potential under every reference.** — Zero field at one point does not determine potential or its reference.

#### Situation 2: Negative charge and a stated velocity

A negative particle is moving east in a uniform electric field directed west. Ignore other forces. What initially happens to its speed?

1. **Choose east as positive.** The field component is negative and the charge is negative.

2. **Determine force sign.**

   $$F_x=qE_x>0$$

3. **Determine initial power sign.**

   $$P=\vec F\cdot\vec v>0$$

4. **Interpret the energy change.** Positive electric work increases kinetic energy under the stated model.

**Answer:** It increases.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **It decreases because every charge moves with the electric field.** — Negative charge feels force opposite the field.
- **It stays fixed because the force is electric.** — An electric force can do work.
- **It instantly moves west without passing through zero velocity.** — A finite force changes velocity continuously.

#### Situation 3: A trajectory is not a field line

A charged particle enters a uniform vertical electric field with a horizontal velocity. Why does it not immediately follow a vertical field line?

1. **Resolve the equations of motion.**

   $$a_x=0,\qquad a_y=qE_y/m$$

2. **Keep the initial horizontal motion.**

   $$x=v_{0x}t$$

3. **Add the vertical motion.**

   $$y=v_{0y}t+\tfrac12a_yt^2$$

4. **Combine the motions.** For zero initial vertical velocity, eliminating time gives a parabolic trajectory in the uniform-field region.

**Answer:** Its horizontal velocity persists while the vertical electric force changes its vertical velocity.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **The field is too weak to exert any force.** — A nonzero charge in a nonzero field has electric force.
- **The particle must move in a circle at constant speed.** — A uniform electric force differs from a perpendicular magnetic force.
- **Electric field lines are physical tracks that block sideways motion.** — Field lines describe the field; they are not mechanical guides.

#### Situation 4: Reading a nonuniform field drawing

Two points in a correctly drawn electric-field-line diagram have different line densities. What does the denser region indicate?

1. **Identify what the diagram encodes.** Line direction represents the force direction for a positive test charge.

2. **Compare density consistently.** Closer lines represent larger magnitude when the same line-count convention is used.

3. **Separate field from a particle’s response.**

   $$\vec F=q\vec E,\qquad \vec a=q\vec E/m$$

4. **Do not infer an unstated speed.** The field alone is insufficient to determine the particle’s velocity.

**Answer:** A larger field magnitude, under the drawing’s consistent line convention.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **Every test particle must move faster there regardless of its history.** — Speed also depends on charge, mass, initial conditions, and the energy change.
- **A larger test-charge magnitude is built into the field.** — The source arrangement determines the field.
- **Field lines can cross there because the field is strong.** — A field vector has one direction at a regular point; crossing lines would conflict.

#### Situation 5: A dipole far from its sources

A neutral dipole’s two charges stay fixed while an observation distance much larger than their separation is doubled. How does its leading far-field magnitude scale along a fixed direction?

1. **Identify the cancellation.** Equal opposite charges cancel the leading monopole contribution.

2. **Use the dipole leading order.**

   $$E_{\mathrm{dipole}}\propto\frac p{r^3}$$

3. **Apply the distance change.**

   $$\frac{E(2r)}{E(r)}\approx\frac1{2^3}=\frac18$$

4. **Keep the condition.** Near the charges, use the full superposition expression rather than this far-field approximation.

**Answer:** It becomes approximately $1/8$ as large.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **It becomes $1/4$ as large.** — That is the monopole distance scaling, whose leading contribution cancels for a neutral dipole.
- **It becomes $1/2$ as large.** — That is not the dipole far-field law.
- **It stays the same because total charge is zero.** — A neutral source distribution can still produce a nonzero field.

#### Situation 6: Two sources above their midpoint

Equal +2.0 nC charges are at (−0.030 m,0) and (+0.030 m,0). Find the net electric-field magnitude at (0,+0.040 m). Use $k=8.99\times10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}$.

1. **Find each source distance.**

   $$r=\sqrt{0.030^2+0.040^2}=0.050\,\mathrm m$$

2. **Use symmetry.** The horizontal components cancel, and both vertical components point upward.

3. **Project the two fields.**

   $$E_y=2\left(\frac{kq}{r^2}\right)\frac{0.040}{r}$$

4. **Combine before evaluating.** The net field is upward. The distance projection is essential.

   $$E=\frac{2kq(0.040)}{r^3}$$

5. **State the result.**

   $$11507.2\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$$

**Answer:** $11507.2\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$14384\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$** — This adds both field magnitudes without their directional components.
- **$0\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$** — Only the horizontal components cancel; the vertical components add.
- **$5753.6\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$** — Both equal vertical components must be included.

#### Situation 7: Electron deflection in a field region

An electron enters horizontally at $2.0\times10^{7}$ m/s through a 0.030 m-long region of uniform upward electric field 5000 N/C. Its initial vertical speed is zero. Ignore gravity, fringing, and relativistic corrections. Find the vertical displacement magnitude at exit. Use $|e|=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$ and $m_e=9.11\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$.

1. **Use the unchanged horizontal speed for transit time.**

   $$t=\frac L{v_x}=\frac{0.030}{2.0\times10^7}=1.5\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm s$$

2. **Find vertical acceleration magnitude.**

   $$|a_y|=\frac{eE}{m_e}$$

3. **Integrate the vertical motion.**

   $$|y|=\frac12|a_y|t^2=\frac{eEL^2}{2m_ev_x^2}$$

4. **Determine the direction separately.** The electron has negative charge, so the displacement is downward, opposite the upward field.

5. **State the result.**

   $$9.8916\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm m$$

**Answer:** $9.8916\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm m$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$0.00197832\,\mathrm m$** — This omits the one-half factor for constant acceleration from zero vertical speed.
- **$659440\,\mathrm m$** — The time must be squared in displacement.
- **$0.98916\,\mathrm m$** — This numerical value is the millimetre result mislabeled as metres.

#### Situation 8: Stopping against an electric force

A hypothetical +4.0 μC particle of mass $2.0\times10^{-6}$ kg moves at 10 m/s opposite a uniform 50 N/C electric field. Ignore all other forces. How far does it travel before it first stops?

1. **Choose the initial velocity direction as positive.** The electric force opposes that direction, so the acceleration is negative.

2. **Use work and energy.**

   $$0-\frac12mv_0^2=-qEd$$

3. **Rearrange for a positive stopping distance.**

   $$d=\frac{mv_0^2}{2qE}$$

4. **Substitute.** Stopping is instantaneous here; if the field remains, the particle then accelerates back in the field direction.

   $$d=\frac{(2.0\times10^{-6})(10)^2}{2(4.0\times10^{-6})(50)}$$

5. **State the result.**

   $$0.5\,\mathrm m$$

**Answer:** $0.5\,\mathrm m$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$1\,\mathrm m$** — The stopping-energy equation contains one half in the initial kinetic energy.
- **$0.25\,\mathrm m$** — This inserts an extra factor of two.
- **$5\,\mathrm m$** — Use the electric force qE rather than the charge alone.

#### Situation 9: Signed force components

A −2.0 μC particle is placed in a field with $E_x=+300\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$ and $E_y=-400\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$. Take east as +x and north as +y. Find the y-component of electric force.

1. **Convert the signed charge.**

   $$q=-2.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm C$$

2. **Apply the vector relation component by component.**

   $$F_y=qE_y=(-2.0\times10^{-6})(-400)$$

3. **Interpret the sign.** The positive result points north.

4. **Check with the other component.** The full force points northwest; component and magnitude questions are different.

   $$F_x=(-2.0\times10^{-6})(300)=-6.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm N$$

5. **State the result.**

   $$8\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm N$$

**Answer:** $8\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm N$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$-8\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm N$** — The negative charge reverses the negative y field component.
- **$0.001\,\mathrm N$** — This is the total force magnitude, not its y-component.
- **$-6\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm N$** — This uses the x component instead of the y component.

#### Situation 10: Exact axial dipole field

A −3.0 nC charge is at $x=-0.020\,\mathrm m$ and a +3.0 nC charge at $x=+0.020\,\mathrm m$. Find the field magnitude at $x=+0.100\,\mathrm m$ using exact superposition, not the far-field approximation. Use $k=8.99\times10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}$.

1. **Use the actual source distances.**

   $$r_+=0.100-0.020=0.080\,\mathrm m,\quad r_-=0.100+0.020=0.120\,\mathrm m$$

2. **Assign directions.** The closer positive charge gives an eastward field; the negative charge gives a westward field.

3. **Subtract the collinear magnitudes.**

   $$E_x=k(3.0\times10^{-9})\left(\frac1{0.080^2}-\frac1{0.120^2}\right)$$

4. **Check the approximation condition.** The far-field expression is useful only when distance greatly exceeds the source separation; it is not an exact replacement here.

5. **State the result.**

   $$2341.15\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$$

**Answer:** $2341.15\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$6086.98\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$** — The two fields at this exterior point are oppositely directed.
- **$2157.6\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$** — This is the far-field estimate; the question asks for the exact finite-separation result.
- **$0\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$** — Neutral total charge does not imply zero field outside a dipole.
