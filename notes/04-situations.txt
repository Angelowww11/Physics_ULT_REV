### Worked applications: models, calculations, and interpretation

These worked examples apply the module concepts. Each example states its conditions, explains the model, and shows the complete working.

<section class="worked-application">

<h4 id="worked-m4h1">Worked application 1: Field cancellation is not source absence</h4>

Two equal positive charges lie on opposite sides of a midpoint. The electric field at the midpoint is zero.

The zero net field determines the instantaneous electric force at the midpoint; stability needs a separate displacement analysis.

1. **Add the source field vectors.**

   $$\vec E_{\mathrm{net}}=\vec E_1+\vec E_2=0$$

2. **Apply the field to a test charge.**

   $$\vec F=q\vec E_{\mathrm{net}}=0$$

3. **Separate three questions.** Net field, individual contributions, and stability after displacement are distinct.

4. **Avoid a potential shortcut.** Field cancellation does not imply zero scalar potential.

**Result:** The electric force there is zero, but that alone does not establish stable equilibrium.

</section>

<section class="worked-application">

<h4 id="worked-m4h2">Worked application 2: Negative charge and a stated velocity</h4>

A negative particle is moving east in a uniform electric field directed west. Ignore other forces.

The charge sign and electric-field direction determine the initial change in the particle’s speed.

1. **Choose east as positive.** The field component is negative and the charge is negative.

2. **Determine force sign.**

   $$F_x=qE_x>0$$

3. **Determine initial power sign.**

   $$P=\vec F\cdot\vec v>0$$

4. **Interpret the energy change.** Positive electric work increases kinetic energy under the stated model.

**Result:** It increases.

</section>

<section class="worked-application">

<h4 id="worked-m4h3">Worked application 3: A trajectory is not a field line</h4>

A charged particle enters a uniform vertical electric field with a horizontal velocity.

The particle retains horizontal motion while the vertical electric force changes its vertical velocity.

1. **Resolve the equations of motion.**

   $$a_x=0,\qquad a_y=qE_y/m$$

2. **Keep the initial horizontal motion.**

   $$x=v_{0x}t$$

3. **Add the vertical motion.**

   $$y=v_{0y}t+\tfrac12a_yt^2$$

4. **Combine the motions.** For zero initial vertical velocity, eliminating time gives a parabolic trajectory in the uniform-field region.

**Result:** Its horizontal velocity persists while the vertical electric force changes its vertical velocity.

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m4h4">Worked application 4: Reading a nonuniform field drawing</h4>

Two points in a correctly drawn electric-field-line diagram have different line densities.

The line density represents the relative electric-field magnitude.

1. **Identify what the diagram encodes.** Line direction represents the force direction for a positive test charge.

2. **Compare density consistently.** Closer lines represent larger magnitude when the same line-count convention is used.

3. **Separate field from a particle’s response.**

   $$\vec F=q\vec E,\qquad \vec a=q\vec E/m$$

4. **Do not infer an unstated speed.** The field alone is insufficient to determine the particle’s velocity.

**Result:** A larger field magnitude, under the drawing’s consistent line convention.

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m4h5">Worked application 5: A dipole far from its sources</h4>

A neutral dipole’s two charges stay fixed while an observation distance much larger than their separation is doubled.

The far-field dipole law determines the magnitude change when the observation distance doubles along a fixed direction.

1. **Identify the cancellation.** Equal opposite charges cancel the leading monopole contribution.

2. **Use the dipole leading order.**

   $$E_{\mathrm{dipole}}\propto\frac p{r^3}$$

3. **Apply the distance change.**

   $$\frac{E(2r)}{E(r)}\approx\frac1{2^3}=\frac18$$

4. **Keep the condition.** Near the charges, use the full superposition expression rather than this far-field approximation.

**Result:** It becomes approximately $1/8$ as large.

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m4h6">Worked application 6: Two sources above their midpoint</h4>

Equal +2.0 nC charges are at (−0.030 m,0) and (+0.030 m,0). The calculation determines the net electric-field magnitude at (0,+0.040 m). Use $k=8.99\times10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}$.

1. **Find each source distance.**

   $$r=\sqrt{0.030^2+0.040^2}=0.050\,\mathrm m$$

2. **Use symmetry.** The horizontal components cancel, and both vertical components point upward.

3. **Project the two fields.**

   $$E_y=2\left(\frac{kq}{r^2}\right)\frac{0.040}{r}$$

4. **Combine before evaluating.** The net field is upward. The distance projection is essential.

   $$E=\frac{2kq(0.040)}{r^3}$$

5. **State the result.**

   $$11507.2\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$$

**Result:** $11507.2\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m4h7">Worked application 7: Electron deflection in a field region</h4>

An electron enters horizontally at $2.0\times10^{7}$ m/s through a 0.030 m-long region of uniform upward electric field 5000 N/C. Its initial vertical speed is zero. Ignore gravity, fringing, and relativistic corrections. The calculation determines the vertical displacement magnitude at exit. Use $|e|=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$ and $m_e=9.11\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$.

1. **Use the unchanged horizontal speed for transit time.**

   $$t=\frac L{v_x}=\frac{0.030}{2.0\times10^7}=1.5\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm s$$

2. **Find vertical acceleration magnitude.**

   $$|a_y|=\frac{eE}{m_e}$$

3. **Integrate the vertical motion.**

   $$|y|=\frac12|a_y|t^2=\frac{eEL^2}{2m_ev_x^2}$$

4. **Determine the direction separately.** The electron has negative charge, so the displacement is downward, opposite the upward field.

5. **State the result.**

   $$9.8916\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm m$$

**Result:** $9.8916\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm m$

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m4h8">Worked application 8: Stopping against an electric force</h4>

A hypothetical +4.0 μC particle of mass $2.0\times10^{-6}$ kg moves at 10 m/s opposite a uniform 50 N/C electric field. Ignore all other forces. The calculation determines the travel distance before the particle first stops.

1. **Choose the initial velocity direction as positive.** The electric force opposes that direction, so the acceleration is negative.

2. **Use work and energy.**

   $$0-\frac12mv_0^2=-qEd$$

3. **Rearrange for a positive stopping distance.**

   $$d=\frac{mv_0^2}{2qE}$$

4. **Substitute.** Stopping is instantaneous here; if the field remains, the particle then accelerates back in the field direction.

   $$d=\frac{(2.0\times10^{-6})(10)^2}{2(4.0\times10^{-6})(50)}$$

5. **State the result.**

   $$0.5\,\mathrm m$$

**Result:** $0.5\,\mathrm m$

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m4h9">Worked application 9: Signed force components</h4>

A −2.0 μC particle is placed in a field with $E_x=+300\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$ and $E_y=-400\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$. Take east as +x and north as +y. The calculation determines the y-component of electric force.

1. **Convert the signed charge.**

   $$q=-2.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm C$$

2. **Apply the vector relation component by component.**

   $$F_y=qE_y=(-2.0\times10^{-6})(-400)$$

3. **Interpret the sign.** The positive result points north.

4. **Check with the other component.** The full force points northwest; component and magnitude questions are different.

   $$F_x=(-2.0\times10^{-6})(300)=-6.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm N$$

5. **State the result.**

   $$8\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm N$$

**Result:** $8\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm N$

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m4h10">Worked application 10: Exact axial dipole field</h4>

A −3.0 nC charge is at $x=-0.020\,\mathrm m$ and a +3.0 nC charge at $x=+0.020\,\mathrm m$. The calculation determines the field magnitude at $x=+0.100\,\mathrm m$ using exact superposition, not the far-field approximation. Use $k=8.99\times10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}$.

1. **Use the actual source distances.**

   $$r_+=0.100-0.020=0.080\,\mathrm m,\quad r_-=0.100+0.020=0.120\,\mathrm m$$

2. **Assign directions.** The closer positive charge gives an eastward field; the negative charge gives a westward field.

3. **Subtract the collinear magnitudes.**

   $$E_x=k(3.0\times10^{-9})\left(\frac1{0.080^2}-\frac1{0.120^2}\right)$$

4. **Check the approximation condition.** The far-field expression is useful only when distance greatly exceeds the source separation; it is not an exact replacement here.

5. **State the result.**

   $$2341.15\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$$

**Result:** $2341.15\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$

</section>

<h4 id="solution-m4h11">Worked application 11: Two perpendicular source fields · the net electric-field magnitude at the origin</h4>

A fixed $+Q$ source lies at $(-a,0)$ and a fixed $-2Q$ source lies at $(0,+b)$. Use $Q=2\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm C$, $a=0.04\,\mathrm m$, $b=0.06\,\mathrm m$, and $k=8.99\times10^9$ in SI units. A separate test particle at the origin has charge $-2.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ and mass $1.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{kg}$. Its presence does not move the sources. Ignore other forces. The calculation determines the net electric-field magnitude at the origin.

1. **Identify the model and the conditions.** The left positive source gives a rightward field; the upper negative source gives an upward field. Multiply the resulting field by the signed test charge only after superposition.

2. **Calculate the field components.**

   $$E_x=kQ/a^2=11237.5,\quad E_y=2kQ/b^2=9988.89\quad(\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}})$$

3. **Combine the components.**

   $$E=\sqrt{E_x^2+E_y^2}=15035.3\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$$

4. **Solve the requested quantity.**

   $$E=\sqrt{(11237.5)^2+(9988.89)^2}$$

5. **Check the physical result.** Field magnitude is independent of the chosen test-particle charge.

6. **State the result.**

   $$15035.3\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$$

**Result:** $15035.3\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$

</section>

<h4 id="solution-m4h21">Worked application 21: An electron first rises, then falls · the signed exit vertical displacement</h4>

An electron enters a region of uniform upward electric field $E=1000\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}$. The region extends 0.04 m horizontally. Initially $v_x=2.00\times10^7\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $v_y=+1.00\times10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Take its entry point as y zero. Use $e=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$ and $m_e=9.11\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$. Ignore gravity, fringing, and relativistic corrections. No plate intercepts the trajectory. The calculation determines the signed exit vertical displacement.

1. **Identify the model and the conditions.** The electron accelerates downward. Its horizontal velocity stays constant. Initial upward motion does not imply upward acceleration.

2. **Find the signed acceleration and flight time.**

   $$a_y=-eE/m_e=-1.75851\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}},\quad t=L/v_x=2\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm s$$

3. **Keep the initial vertical velocity term.**

   $$y=v_{y0}t+\tfrac12a_yt^2,\quad v_y=v_{y0}+a_yt$$

4. **Check that the turning point occurs inside the region.**

   $$t_{\rm turn}=-v_{y0}/a_y=5.68664\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm s<t$$

5. **Solve the requested quantity.**

   $$y=(10^5)(2\times10^{-9})+\tfrac12(-1.75851\times10^{14})(2\times10^{-9})^2=-1.51701\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm m$$

6. **Check the physical result.** The electron can finish below entry height even though it initially moved upward.

7. **State the result.**

   $$-1.51701\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm m$$

**Result:** $-1.51701\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm m$

</section>
