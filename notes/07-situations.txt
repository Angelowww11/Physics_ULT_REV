### Worked applications: models, calculations, and interpretation

These worked examples apply the module concepts. Each example states its conditions, explains the model, and shows the complete working.

<section class="worked-application">

<h4 id="worked-m7h1">Worked application 1: Stretching a wire</h4>

A uniform wire is stretched to twice its length. Its volume and resistivity remain constant. The same ideal voltage is applied before and after stretching.

The length and area changes determine the current after stretching at fixed volume, resistivity, and applied voltage.

1. **Conserve volume.**

   $$A_f(2L)=A_iL\ \Rightarrow\ A_f=A_i/2$$

2. **Compare resistances.**

   $$R_f=\rho\frac{2L}{A_i/2}=4R_i$$

3. **Use fixed voltage.**

   $$I_f=V/R_f=I_i/4$$

4. **Check the assumption.** The result assumes that temperature and resistivity do not change.

**Result:** It becomes one quarter of its original value.

</section>

<section class="worked-application">

<h4 id="worked-m7h2">Worked application 2: Loaded battery voltage</h4>

A battery has fixed emf and positive internal resistance. Its external load resistance increases. Use the steady DC model.

The battery model determines the delivered current and terminal voltage as the external resistance increases.

1. **Write the loop current.**

   $$I=\mathcal E/(R+r)$$

2. **Relate terminal voltage to internal drop.**

   $$V_{\mathrm{term}}=\mathcal E-Ir$$

3. **Increase the load resistance.** The denominator increases, so current and internal drop decrease.

4. **Check the open-circuit limit.** As load resistance approaches infinity, current approaches zero and terminal voltage approaches the emf.

**Result:** Current decreases; terminal voltage increases toward the emf.

</section>

<section class="worked-application">

<h4 id="worked-m7h3">Worked application 3: A capacitor at a switching instant</h4>

An ideal capacitor initially has a nonzero voltage. A switch connects it through a finite resistor to a different constant voltage source.

Capacitor-voltage continuity determines the electrical state immediately after switching.

1. **Start from the capacitor relation.**

   $$i=C\,dV_C/dt$$

2. **Apply finite-current behavior.** Finite current changes the charge continuously.

3. **Apply the loop equation at the instant.**

   $$i(0^+)=\frac{V_s-V_C(0^-)}{R}$$

4. **Separate the two quantities.** Continuity of capacitor voltage does not require continuity of resistor current.

**Result:** Capacitor voltage stays continuous; resistor current can change immediately.

</section>

<section class="worked-application">

<h4 id="worked-m7h4">Worked application 4: A negative Kirchhoff result</h4>

You choose clockwise current for one branch and solve consistent Kirchhoff equations. The branch current is negative.

A negative calculated branch current indicates motion opposite the chosen positive direction.

1. **Keep the original arrows.** Use the same current arrows throughout the equations.

2. **Read the solved sign.** A negative value reverses the physical direction relative to its reference arrow.

3. **Check resistor power.**

   $$P_R=I^2R\ge0\quad(R>0)$$

4. **Verify the equations.** Substitute the signed result into the original node and loop equations.

**Result:** The actual current flows opposite to the chosen arrow.

</section>

<section class="worked-application">

<h4 id="worked-m7h5">Worked application 5: Removing one parallel branch</h4>

Two resistors connect in parallel across an ideal constant-voltage source. One branch is removed.

The parallel-circuit model distinguishes the unchanged remaining-branch current from the reduced total source current.

1. **Use the maintained voltage.**

   $$I_{\mathrm{remaining}}=V/R$$

2. **Add the original branch currents.**

   $$I_{\mathrm{total,i}}=V/R_1+V/R_2$$

3. **Remove the disconnected branch.**

   $$I_{\mathrm{total,f}}=V/R_1$$

4. **Check the source assumption.** A real battery with internal resistance can also change its terminal voltage.

**Result:** Remaining-branch current stays the same; total current decreases.

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m7h6">Worked application 6: Load power with internal resistance</h4>

A 12 V battery with 1.0 Ω internal resistance supplies a 5.0 Ω load. The calculation determines the steady power delivered to the load.

1. **Include both series resistances.**

   $$R_{\mathrm{total}}=5+1=6\,\Omega$$

2. **Find the current.**

   $$I=12/6=2\,\mathrm A$$

3. **Find the load voltage.**

   $$V_L=IR_L=2(5)=10\,\mathrm V$$

4. **Calculate only the load power.**

   $$P_L=I^2R_L=2^2(5)=20\,\mathrm W$$

5. **Check energy per second.**

   $$\mathcal EI=24\,\mathrm W=P_L+I^2r=20+4$$

6. **State the result.**

   $$20\,\mathrm W$$

**Result:** $20\,\mathrm W$

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m7h7">Worked application 7: Stretching changes wire power</h4>

A uniform 3.0 Ω wire is stretched to twice its length at constant volume and resistivity. It is then connected across an ideal 12 V source. The calculation determines its new electrical power.

1. **Conserve material volume.**

   $$A_f=A_i/2$$

2. **Use both geometric changes.**

   $$R_f=\rho\frac{2L}{A_i/2}=4(3)=12\,\Omega$$

3. **Use fixed-voltage power.**

   $$P_f=V^2/R_f=12^2/12=12\,\mathrm W$$

4. **Check the trend.** The larger resistance reduces power at fixed voltage. Fixed-current operation would give a different trend.

5. **State the result.**

   $$12\,\mathrm W$$

**Result:** $12\,\mathrm W$

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m7h8">Worked application 8: a charging time from voltage</h4>

An initially uncharged 100 μF capacitor charges through 2.0 kΩ from an ideal 9.0 V source. The calculation determines the time when capacitor voltage first reaches 6.0 V.

1. **Convert and calculate the time constant.**

   $$\tau=RC=(2000)(100\times10^{-6})=0.200\,\mathrm s$$

2. **Choose charging from zero.**

   $$V_C=V_s(1-e^{-t/\tau})$$

3. **Isolate the exponential.**

   $$e^{-t/\tau}=1-6/9=1/3$$

4. **Take logarithms.**

   $$t=-\tau\ln(1/3)=0.200\ln3$$

5. **Check the current at this instant.**

   $$I=(9-6)/2000=1.50\,\mathrm{mA}$$

6. **State the result.**

   $$0.219722\,\mathrm s$$

**Result:** $0.219722\,\mathrm s$

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m7h9">Worked application 9: Power in a mixed network</h4>

A 4.0 Ω resistor is in series with a parallel pair of 6.0 Ω and 3.0 Ω resistors. An ideal 12 V source supplies the network. The calculation determines power in the 3.0 Ω resistor.

1. **Reduce the parallel pair.**

   $$R_p=(1/6+1/3)^{-1}=2\,\Omega$$

2. **Find source current.**

   $$I=12/(4+2)=2\,\mathrm A$$

3. **Find voltage across the pair.**

   $$V_p=IR_p=4\,\mathrm V$$

4. **Use the requested branch voltage.**

   $$P_3=V_p^2/3=16/3\,\mathrm W$$

5. **Check branch currents.**

   $$I_3=4/3\,\mathrm A,\quad I_6=4/6\,\mathrm A,\quad I_3+I_6=2\,\mathrm A$$

6. **State the result.**

   $$5.33333\,\mathrm W$$

**Result:** $5.33333\,\mathrm W$

</section>

<h4 id="worked-m7h10">Worked application 10: A source receives current</h4>

Three branches connect the same upper node to ground. Branch 1 has a 12 V ideal source in series with 3 Ω. Branch 2 has a 3 V ideal source in series with 6 Ω. Both source positive terminals point toward the upper node. Branch 3 is a 2 Ω load. The calculation determines the signed current delivered by the 3 V source toward the upper node.

1. **Define the reference.** Let V be upper-node voltage. Positive source current flows toward that node.

2. **Apply node conservation.**

   $$\frac{12-V}{3}+\frac{3-V}{6}=\frac V2$$

3. **Solve the node voltage.**

   $$2(12-V)+(3-V)=3V\ \Rightarrow\ V=4.5\,\mathrm V$$

4. **Calculate the requested signed branch current.**

   $$I_2=(3-4.5)/6=-0.250\,\mathrm A$$

5. **Interpret the sign.** Current enters the positive terminal of the 3 V source. The ideal source absorbs electrical energy in this model.

6. **State the result.**

   $$-0.25\,\mathrm A$$

**Result:** $-0.25\,\mathrm A$

</section>

<h4 id="solution-m7h11">Worked application 11: A voltage divider with a load · the equivalent resistance seen by the source</h4>

An ideal 12 V source supplies a divider with upper resistor $R_1=100\,\Omega$ and lower resistor $R_2=200\,\Omega$. A load $R_L=300\,\Omega$ connects across R2. Use steady DC conditions and ideal wires. The calculation determines the equivalent resistance seen by the source.

1. **Identify the model and the conditions.** The load is parallel to the lower resistor. Calculate the loaded equivalent resistance before applying the voltage-divider relation.

2. **Reduce the loaded lower branch.**

   $$R_p=R_2R_L/(R_2+R_L)=120\,\Omega$$

3. **Find source current and load voltage.**

   $$I=V/(R_1+R_p)=0.0545455\,\mathrm A,\quad V_L=IR_p=6.54545\,\mathrm V$$

4. **Solve the requested quantity.**

   $$R_{\rm eq}=R_1+R_p=220\,\Omega$$

5. **Check the physical result.** Adding the load reduces the lower-branch resistance and the whole divider resistance.

6. **State the result.**

   $$220\,\Omega$$

**Result:** $220\,\Omega$

</section>

<h4 id="solution-m7h15">Worked application 15: A voltage divider with a load · model and interpretation</h4>

**Result:** The load changes the equivalent resistance of the lower branch.

<h4 id="solution-m7h21">Worked application 21: A warm resistor on a real battery · the warm wire resistance</h4>

A wire has resistance 6 Ω at 20°C and linear temperature coefficient $0.0040\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$. It is held at 70°C while connected to a battery of emf 12 V and internal resistance 1.0 Ω. Treat temperature as externally fixed and the coefficient as constant over this range. The calculation determines the warm wire resistance.

1. **Identify the model and the conditions.** First update the wire resistance using the temperature difference. Then include internal resistance in the complete circuit.

2. **Find the wire resistance at the stated temperature.**

   $$R=R_0[1+\alpha(T-T_0)]=7.2\,\Omega$$

3. **Include battery internal resistance.**

   $$I=\mathcal E/(R+r)=1.46341\,\mathrm A$$

4. **Find terminal voltage.**

   $$V_t=\mathcal E-Ir=10.5366\,\mathrm V$$

5. **Solve the requested quantity.**

   $$R=6[1+0.0040(70-20)]$$

6. **Check the physical result.** The positive temperature coefficient makes the warm resistance exceed the reference resistance.

7. **State the result.**

   $$7.2\,\Omega$$

**Result:** $7.2\,\Omega$

</section>
