### Situational problems: choose the law, then solve

These added practice problems apply the module concepts. They are not copied examination questions. Keep the stated assumptions when you use each result.

#### Situation 1: Stretching a wire

A uniform wire is stretched to twice its length. Its volume and resistivity remain constant. The same ideal voltage is applied before and after stretching. How does the current change?

1. **Conserve volume.**

   $$A_f(2L)=A_iL\ \Rightarrow\ A_f=A_i/2$$

2. **Compare resistances.**

   $$R_f=\rho\frac{2L}{A_i/2}=4R_i$$

3. **Use fixed voltage.**

   $$I_f=V/R_f=I_i/4$$

4. **Check the assumption.** The result assumes that temperature and resistivity do not change.

**Answer:** It becomes one quarter of its original value.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **It becomes one half.** — This ignores the reduction in cross-sectional area.
- **It stays constant.** — The resistance changes even though the material volume stays constant.
- **It doubles.** — At fixed voltage, increasing resistance decreases current.

#### Situation 2: Loaded battery voltage

A battery has fixed emf and positive internal resistance. Its external load resistance increases. Use the steady DC model. What happens to its delivered current and terminal voltage?

1. **Write the loop current.**

   $$I=\mathcal E/(R+r)$$

2. **Relate terminal voltage to internal drop.**

   $$V_{\mathrm{term}}=\mathcal E-Ir$$

3. **Increase the load resistance.** The denominator increases, so current and internal drop decrease.

4. **Check the open-circuit limit.** As load resistance approaches infinity, current approaches zero and terminal voltage approaches the emf.

**Answer:** Current decreases; terminal voltage increases toward the emf.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **Both decrease.** — The internal voltage drop decreases as current decreases.
- **Current increases; terminal voltage decreases.** — Increasing total series resistance decreases the current.
- **Both remain constant.** — Internal resistance makes terminal voltage depend on load current.

#### Situation 3: A capacitor at a switching instant

An ideal capacitor initially has a nonzero voltage. A switch connects it through a finite resistor to a different constant voltage source. Which statement applies immediately after switching?

1. **Start from the capacitor relation.**

   $$i=C\,dV_C/dt$$

2. **Apply finite-current behavior.** Finite current changes the charge continuously.

3. **Apply the loop equation at the instant.**

   $$i(0^+)=\frac{V_s-V_C(0^-)}{R}$$

4. **Separate the two quantities.** Continuity of capacitor voltage does not require continuity of resistor current.

**Answer:** Capacitor voltage stays continuous; resistor current can change immediately.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **Capacitor voltage instantly equals the source voltage.** — The finite resistor limits current and prevents an instantaneous change in stored charge.
- **Capacitor current must be zero because the initial voltage is fixed.** — The voltage is continuous at the instant, but its time derivative can be nonzero.
- **Capacitor voltage and resistor current must both be continuous.** — The resistor has no energy-storage requirement that forces current continuity.

#### Situation 4: A negative Kirchhoff result

You choose clockwise current for one branch and solve consistent Kirchhoff equations. The branch current is negative. What does that result mean?

1. **Keep the original arrows.** Use the same current arrows throughout the equations.

2. **Read the solved sign.** A negative value reverses the physical direction relative to its reference arrow.

3. **Check resistor power.**

   $$P_R=I^2R\ge0\quad(R>0)$$

4. **Verify the equations.** Substitute the signed result into the original node and loop equations.

**Answer:** The actual current flows opposite to the chosen arrow.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **The circuit violates charge conservation.** — A negative signed current can satisfy charge conservation.
- **The resistor has negative resistance.** — A negative current alone does not imply negative resistance.
- **Replace the current with its magnitude in every equation without changing signs.** — The signed solution must remain consistent with the original reference directions.

#### Situation 5: Removing one parallel branch

Two resistors connect in parallel across an ideal constant-voltage source. One branch is removed. What happens to current in the remaining resistor and total source current?

1. **Use the maintained voltage.**

   $$I_{\mathrm{remaining}}=V/R$$

2. **Add the original branch currents.**

   $$I_{\mathrm{total,i}}=V/R_1+V/R_2$$

3. **Remove the disconnected branch.**

   $$I_{\mathrm{total,f}}=V/R_1$$

4. **Check the source assumption.** A real battery with internal resistance can also change its terminal voltage.

**Answer:** Remaining-branch current stays the same; total current decreases.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **Both currents increase.** — Removing a parallel path increases equivalent resistance.
- **Remaining-branch current doubles because it receives the removed current.** — An ideal voltage source does not maintain fixed total current.
- **Both become zero.** — The remaining branch still forms a closed circuit.

#### Situation 6: Load power with internal resistance

A 12 V battery with 1.0 Ω internal resistance supplies a 5.0 Ω load. Find the steady power delivered to the load.

1. **Include both series resistances.**

   $$R_{\mathrm{total}}=5+1=6\,\Omega$$

2. **Find the current.**

   $$I=12/6=2\,\mathrm A$$

3. **Find the load voltage.**

   $$V_L=IR_L=2(5)=10\,\mathrm V$$

4. **Calculate only the load power.**

   $$P_L=I^2R_L=2^2(5)=20\,\mathrm W$$

5. **Check energy per second.**

   $$\mathcal EI=24\,\mathrm W=P_L+I^2r=20+4$$

6. **State the result.**

   $$20\,\mathrm W$$

**Answer:** $20\,\mathrm W$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$24\,\mathrm W$** — This is total source power, including internal heating.
- **$28.8\,\mathrm W$** — This treats the full emf as the load voltage.
- **$4\,\mathrm W$** — This is power dissipated in the internal resistance.

#### Situation 7: Stretching changes wire power

A uniform 3.0 Ω wire is stretched to twice its length at constant volume and resistivity. It is then connected across an ideal 12 V source. Find its new electrical power.

1. **Conserve material volume.**

   $$A_f=A_i/2$$

2. **Use both geometric changes.**

   $$R_f=\rho\frac{2L}{A_i/2}=4(3)=12\,\Omega$$

3. **Use fixed-voltage power.**

   $$P_f=V^2/R_f=12^2/12=12\,\mathrm W$$

4. **Check the trend.** The larger resistance reduces power at fixed voltage. Fixed-current operation would give a different trend.

5. **State the result.**

   $$12\,\mathrm W$$

**Answer:** $12\,\mathrm W$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$24\,\mathrm W$** — This accounts for length but misses the area change.
- **$48\,\mathrm W$** — This uses the original resistance.
- **$192\,\mathrm W$** — This reverses the resistance ratio at fixed voltage.

#### Situation 8: Find a charging time from voltage

An initially uncharged 100 μF capacitor charges through 2.0 kΩ from an ideal 9.0 V source. Find the time when capacitor voltage first reaches 6.0 V.

1. **Convert and calculate the time constant.**

   $$\tau=RC=(2000)(100\times10^{-6})=0.200\,\mathrm s$$

2. **Choose charging from zero.**

   $$V_C=V_s(1-e^{-t/\tau})$$

3. **Isolate the exponential.**

   $$e^{-t/\tau}=1-6/9=1/3$$

4. **Take logarithms.**

   $$t=-\tau\ln(1/3)=0.200\ln3$$

5. **Check the current at this instant.**

   $$I=(9-6)/2000=1.50\,\mathrm{mA}$$

6. **State the result.**

   $$0.219722\,\mathrm s$$

**Answer:** $0.219722\,\mathrm s$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$0.2\,\mathrm s$** — One time constant gives about 63.2% of the final voltage, not 6/9.
- **$0.081093\,\mathrm s$** — This uses the wrong logarithm argument.
- **$0.6\,\mathrm s$** — The voltage does not rise linearly with time.

#### Situation 9: Power in a mixed network

A 4.0 Ω resistor is in series with a parallel pair of 6.0 Ω and 3.0 Ω resistors. An ideal 12 V source supplies the network. Find power in the 3.0 Ω resistor.

1. **Reduce the parallel pair.**

   $$R_p=(1/6+1/3)^{-1}=2\,\Omega$$

2. **Find source current.**

   $$I=12/(4+2)=2\,\mathrm A$$

3. **Find voltage across the pair.**

   $$V_p=IR_p=4\,\mathrm V$$

4. **Use the requested branch voltage.**

   $$P_3=V_p^2/3=16/3\,\mathrm W$$

5. **Check branch currents.**

   $$I_3=4/3\,\mathrm A,\quad I_6=4/6\,\mathrm A,\quad I_3+I_6=2\,\mathrm A$$

6. **State the result.**

   $$5.33333\,\mathrm W$$

**Answer:** $5.33333\,\mathrm W$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$48\,\mathrm W$** — This applies the full source voltage to the parallel branch.
- **$12\,\mathrm W$** — This uses the total current as the 3 Ω branch current.
- **$1.33333\,\mathrm W$** — This uses the 6 Ω branch current in the 3 Ω resistor.

#### Situation 10: A source receives current

Three branches connect the same upper node to ground. Branch 1 has a 12 V ideal source in series with 3 Ω. Branch 2 has a 3 V ideal source in series with 6 Ω. Both source positive terminals point toward the upper node. Branch 3 is a 2 Ω load. Find the signed current delivered by the 3 V source toward the upper node.

1. **Define the reference.** Let V be upper-node voltage. Positive source current flows toward that node.

2. **Apply node conservation.**

   $$\frac{12-V}{3}+\frac{3-V}{6}=\frac V2$$

3. **Solve the node voltage.**

   $$2(12-V)+(3-V)=3V\ \Rightarrow\ V=4.5\,\mathrm V$$

4. **Calculate the requested signed branch current.**

   $$I_2=(3-4.5)/6=-0.250\,\mathrm A$$

5. **Interpret the sign.** Current enters the positive terminal of the 3 V source. The ideal source absorbs electrical energy in this model.

6. **State the result.**

   $$-0.25\,\mathrm A$$

**Answer:** $-0.25\,\mathrm A$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$0.25\,\mathrm A$** — This removes the sign that identifies current entering the source.
- **$0.5\,\mathrm A$** — This ignores the shared node voltage and the other source.
- **$1.5\,\mathrm A$** — This is the current delivered by the 12 V branch, not the requested branch.

An ideal 12 V source supplies a divider with upper resistor $R_1=100\,\Omega$ and lower resistor $R_2=200\,\Omega$. A load $R_L=300\,\Omega$ connects across R2. Use steady DC conditions and ideal wires. Find the equivalent resistance seen by the source.

1. **Identify the model and the conditions.** The load is parallel to the lower resistor. Calculate the loaded equivalent resistance before applying the voltage-divider relation.

2. **Reduce the loaded lower branch.**

   $$R_p=R_2R_L/(R_2+R_L)=120\,\Omega$$

3. **Find source current and load voltage.**

   $$I=V/(R_1+R_p)=0.0545455\,\mathrm A,\quad V_L=IR_p=6.54545\,\mathrm V$$

4. **Solve the requested quantity.**

   $$R_{\rm eq}=R_1+R_p=220\,\Omega$$

5. **Check the physical result.** Adding the load reduces the lower-branch resistance and the whole divider resistance.

6. **State the result.**

   $$220\,\Omega$$

**Answer:** $220\,\Omega$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$600\,\Omega$** — The load and R2 are parallel, not series.
- **$300\,\Omega$** — This ignores the connected load.
- **$54.5455\,\Omega$** — R1 is not parallel to the lower branch.

<h4 id="solution-m7h15">Situation 15: A voltage divider with a load · model check</h4>

An ideal 12 V source supplies a divider with upper resistor $R_1=100\,\Omega$ and lower resistor $R_2=200\,\Omega$. A load $R_L=300\,\Omega$ connects across R2. Use steady DC conditions and ideal wires. Why does connecting the load change the output voltage of this divider?

1. **Identify what stays fixed.** The load is parallel to the lower resistor. Calculate the loaded equivalent resistance before applying the voltage-divider relation.

2. **Reduce the loaded lower branch.**

   $$R_p=R_2R_L/(R_2+R_L)=120\,\Omega$$

3. **Find source current and load voltage.**

   $$I=V/(R_1+R_p)=0.0545455\,\mathrm A,\quad V_L=IR_p=6.54545\,\mathrm V$$

4. **Apply the conclusion.** The source voltage is fixed, but its division between the two series branches changes.

**Answer:** The load changes the equivalent resistance of the lower branch.

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **An ideal source must reduce its specified voltage whenever current rises.** — That behavior belongs to a source with internal resistance.
- **The upper resistor automatically changes resistance.** — Its specified resistance stays fixed.
- **Parallel components always have equal current.** — They have equal voltage; their currents depend on resistance.

<h4 id="solution-m7h21">Situation 21: A warm resistor on a real battery · Find the warm wire resistance</h4>

A wire has resistance 6 Ω at 20°C and linear temperature coefficient $0.0040\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$. It is held at 70°C while connected to a battery of emf 12 V and internal resistance 1.0 Ω. Treat temperature as externally fixed and the coefficient as constant over this range. Find the warm wire resistance.

1. **Identify the model and the conditions.** First update the wire resistance using the temperature difference. Then include internal resistance in the complete circuit.

2. **Find the wire resistance at the stated temperature.**

   $$R=R_0[1+\alpha(T-T_0)]=7.2\,\Omega$$

3. **Include battery internal resistance.**

   $$I=\mathcal E/(R+r)=1.46341\,\mathrm A$$

4. **Find terminal voltage.**

   $$V_t=\mathcal E-Ir=10.5366\,\mathrm V$$

5. **Solve the requested quantity.**

   $$R=6[1+0.0040(70-20)]$$

6. **Check the physical result.** The positive temperature coefficient makes the warm resistance exceed the reference resistance.

7. **State the result.**

   $$7.2\,\Omega$$

**Answer:** $7.2\,\Omega$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$7.68\,\Omega$** — This uses the final Celsius reading instead of a temperature change.
- **$6\,\Omega$** — This ignores the temperature increase.
- **$1.2\,\Omega$** — This gives the resistance increase, not final resistance.

<h4 id="solution-m7h51">Situation 51: Maximum power from a resistive source · Find the load resistance for maximum delivered load power</h4>

A battery has fixed emf 12 V and fixed internal resistance 2 Ω. A variable positive load resistance R connects to it. Assume steady DC behavior. Find the operating point that maximizes power delivered to the load, rather than total source power or efficiency. Find the load resistance for maximum delivered load power.

1. **Identify the model and the conditions.** Load power depends on both load resistance and the current it allows. Differentiate the full expression rather than treating current as fixed.

2. **Write load power as a function of the adjustable load.**

   $$P_L(R)=\left(\frac{\mathcal E}{R+r}\right)^2R$$

3. **Differentiate and use the positive-resistance domain.**

   $$\frac{dP_L}{dR}=\frac{\mathcal E^2(r-R)}{(R+r)^3}=0\ \Rightarrow\ R=r$$

4. **Check the two limiting cases.**

   $$P_L\to0\text{ as }R\to0\text{ or }R\to\infty$$

5. **Solve the requested quantity.**

   $$R_{\rm opt}=r=2\,\Omega$$

6. **Check the physical result.** The stationary point is a maximum because power falls to zero at both resistance limits.

7. **State the result.**

   $$2\,\Omega$$

**Answer:** $2\,\Omega$

**Why the other answers do not work:**

- **$0\,\Omega$** — A short circuit has zero load voltage and zero load power.
- **$4\,\Omega$** — The derivative vanishes at $R=r$, not 2r.
- **$1\,\Omega$** — This gives a smaller load than the maximum-power condition.
