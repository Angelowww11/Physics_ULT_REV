### Read field diagrams and solve the primary examples completely

**Source and field points.** A source point identifies the charge producing the field. A field point identifies where E is evaluated. The field equation uses their separation, not the source's distance from an arbitrary coordinate origin.

**Field-line diagnosis — primary p. 20.** Reject crossing electrostatic lines. Check that lines leave positive charges and enter negative charges. Check that line counts reflect relative charge magnitudes. Check that stronger fields have closer line spacing. Do not treat the number of lines as an independently measurable number of physical threads.

{{figure:m4-main:20|The field-line concept check. Use direction, crossing, line-count, and spacing tests.}}

:::example Field from a measured test-charge force
**Source:** Primary p. 12. **Given:** $q_{0} = + 3.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C}$ and $F_{0} = 6.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}$.

1. Divide force by the positive test charge: $E = \frac{F_{0}}{q_{0}} = 2.0\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$.
2. Preserve the force direction as the field direction because $q_{0}$ is positive.
3. Replace the charge with $+ 12 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C}$ while keeping the source distribution fixed.
4. Calculate $F_{\mathrm{new}} = q_{\mathrm{new}}E = \left( 12 \times 10^{-8} \right) \left( 2.0 \right) = 2.4 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{N}$.

**Check.** The test charge increases fourfold, so its force increases fourfold. E does not change in the ideal non-disturbing-test-charge model.
:::

:::example Find the source charge from the field
**Source:** Primary p. 30. **Given:** $E = 5\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$ at $r = 150\,\mathrm{cm}$. Use $k = \frac{9.0 \times 10^{9}\,\mathrm{N} \cdot m^{2}}{C^{2}}$.

1. Convert $150\,\mathrm{cm}$ to $1.50\,\mathrm{m}$.
2. Start with $E = \frac{k \left| Q \right|}{r^{2}}$.
3. Multiply by $r^{2}$ and divide by k: $\left| Q \right| = \frac{Er^{2}}{k}$.
4. Calculate $\left| Q \right| = \frac{5 \left( 1.50 \right)^{2}}{\left( 9.0 \times 10^{9} \right)} = 1.25 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C} = 1.25\,\mathrm{nC}$.

**Check.** The problem gives a magnitude. It does not give enough information to choose a positive or negative source charge.
:::

:::example Electric field halfway between two negative charges
**Source:** Primary p. 32. Put $- 5.00\,\mathrm{nC}$ at $x = 0$ and $- 3.00\,\mathrm{nC}$ at $x = 1.50\,\mathrm{m}$. Evaluate at $x = 0.75\,\mathrm{m}$. Use $k = 9.0 \times 10^{9}$.

1. Use the midpoint distance $0.75\,\mathrm{m}$ for both contributions.
2. Calculate $E_{\mathrm{left}} = \frac{9.0 \times 10^{9} \left( 5.00 \times 10^{-9} \right)}{\left( 0.75 \right)^{2}} = 80.0\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$.
3. Point that contribution left, toward the left negative source.
4. Calculate $E_{\mathrm{right}} = \frac{9.0 \times 10^{9} \left( 3.00 \times 10^{-9} \right)}{\left( 0.75 \right)^{2}} = 48.0\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$, directed right.
5. Add x components: $E_{x} = - 80.0 + 48.0 = - 32.0\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$.

**Check.** The stronger contribution points toward the larger-magnitude negative charge. Do not multiply the result by a test charge unless the question asks for a force.
:::

### Derive the companion's three-charge field and the dipole limits

{{figure:m4-dyn:2|The companion's three-charge geometry and its field-line diagrams.}}

:::derivation Three equal charges on the y-axis
**Source:** Companion p. 2. Put equal charges q at (0,d), (0,0), and (0,−d). Evaluate at (x,0), with $x > 0$.

1. Find the outer distances $r = \sqrt{\left( x^{2} + d^{2} \right)}$. The central distance is x.
2. Use Coulomb's field magnitude $\frac{k \left| q \right|}{r^{2}}$ for either outer source.
3. Project horizontally with $\cos \theta = \frac{x}{r}$. Each outer x component is $\frac{kqx}{r^{3}}$.
4. Cancel the equal, opposite y components.
5. Add the central x component $\frac{kq}{x^{2}}$ and both outer x components.

$$\begin{aligned}
E_x&=\frac{kq}{x^2}+2\frac{kq}{x^2+d^2}\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+d^2}}\\
&=kq\left[\frac1{x^2}+\frac{2x}{(x^2+d^2)^{3/2}}\right],
\qquad E_y=0.
\end{aligned}$$

**Limit check.** Far away, x ≫ d, the result tends to $\frac{3 kq}{x^{2}}$. The collection then resembles one point charge of total charge 3q. At x → 0, the central point-charge term diverges; do not substitute $x = 0$ into the formula.
:::

:::derivation Electric dipole: axial and perpendicular-bisector fields, labeled extensions
Put −q at $x = - a$ and +q at $x = + a$. The dipole moment has magnitude $p = 2 aq$ and points from negative to positive.

For an axial point $x > a$, the two fields oppose. Subtract their signed x components:

$$E_{axis}=kq\left[\frac1{(x-a)^2}-\frac1{(x+a)^2}\right]
=\frac{4kqax}{(x^2-a^2)^2}
\longrightarrow\frac{2kp}{x^3}\quad(x\gg a).$$

For a point (0,y) on the perpendicular bisector, both source distances equal $\sqrt{\left( a^{2} + y^{2} \right)}$. The y components cancel. The x components both point toward −x:

$$E_x=-\frac{2kqa}{(a^2+y^2)^{3/2}}
=-\frac{kp}{(a^2+y^2)^{3/2}},\qquad E_y=0.$$

Far away on that bisector, the magnitude tends to $\frac{kp}{\left| y \right|^{3}}$. Compare the dipole's $\frac{1}{r^{3}}$ far field with a nonzero net point charge's $\frac{1}{r^{2}}$ field. These extensions explain the dipole patterns shown in both decks.
:::

### Motion problems: initial conditions, stopping, and deflection

:::derivation Derive the stopping distance in a uniform electric field
This is a labeled extension of primary pp. 24–25. Let a positive charge enter along +x while E points along −x. Neglect other forces.

1. Write $a_{x} = - \frac{qE}{m}$, where E denotes the positive field magnitude.
2. Use $v_{x} = v_{0}$+at. Setting $v_{x} = 0$ gives $t_{\mathrm{stop}} = \frac{mv_{0}}{\left( qE \right)}$.
3. Use $v_{x}^{2} = v_{0}^{2} + 2 a\Delta x$. Set $v_{x} = 0$ and isolate the stopping distance.

$$d_{stop}=\frac{mv_0^2}{2qE}.$$

The particle reaches a turning point only if the uniform-field region extends that far. After stopping, the force still acts, so the particle reverses direction. Zero instantaneous velocity does not mean zero acceleration.
:::

:::example Electron deflection: retain the negative charge sign
**Extension of the uniform-field diagrams.** Let an electron enter horizontally at $2.0 \times 10^{6}\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}$. The field region is $0.020\,\mathrm{m}$ long, with $E_{y} = + 100\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{C}}$. Take $y_{0} = v_{0y} = 0$, $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$, and $m_{e} = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$.

1. Calculate transit time $t = \frac{L}{v_{0}} x = \frac{0.020}{\left( 2.0 \times 10^{6} \right)} = 1.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s}$.
2. Calculate $a_{y} = \frac{qE_{y}}{m_{e}} = - 1.7585 \times 10^{13}\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}^{2}}$.
3. Find exit displacement $y = \tfrac{1}{2} a_{y} t^{2} \approx - 8.793 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}$, or $- 0.8793\,\mathrm{mm}$.
4. Find exit vertical speed $v_{y} = a_{y} t \approx - 1.7585 \times 10^{5}\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}$.
5. Check the available plate gap. A plate collision ends this free-flight solution before the nominal exit.

**Why the path curves downward.** The field points upward, but the electron is negative. Its horizontal speed stays constant only because this model has no horizontal force.
:::
