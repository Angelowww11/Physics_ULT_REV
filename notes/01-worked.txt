### Source tables, pressure units, and the two air examples

The PDFs use different air temperatures and slightly different material values. Read the conditions before choosing a density.

| Material or condition | Density in the source, $\frac{\mathrm{kg}}{m^{3}}$ | Where it appears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Air at 20°C, $1\,\mathrm{atm}$ | 1.20 | Primary pp. 8–10 |
| Air at 0°C, $1\,\mathrm{atm}$ | 1.29 | Companion p. 2 |
| Freshwater | 1000 | Both decks |
| Seawater | 1030 | Both decks |
| Ice | 920; companion iceberg uses 917 | Primary p. 8; companion p. 11 |
| Brass | 8600 in the table; 8700 in the relative-density example | Primary pp. 8–9 |
| Steel | 7800 | Primary pp. 8–9 |
| Wood | 500 in the relative-density example; 700 in the tied-block problem | Primary p. 9; companion p. 10 |

These are the source's classroom values. Temperature and composition affect real densities. The brass example below uses the explicit $8700\,\frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{m}^{3}}$ value from p. 9.

**Why a fluid has pressure — companion pp. 3–4.** Molecular motion and interactions transfer momentum to container walls. Gravity creates a vertical pressure gradient, but it is not the only cause of pressure. Gas molecules still collide with walls in weightlessness. Over a small laboratory height, a gas's gravitational pressure change can be small; atmospheric pressure changes substantially over larger heights. Internal and external pressure partly balance around the body, so atmospheric pressure does not act as an unopposed inward force everywhere.

**Relative-density check.** Steel: $SG = \frac{7800}{1000} = 7.80$. Brass: $SG = \frac{8700}{1000} = 8.70$. Wood: $SG = \frac{500}{1000} = 0.500$. Relative density has no $\frac{\mathrm{kg}}{m^{3}}$ unit because the density units cancel.

| Pressure unit | Relation |
| --- | --- |
| Pascal | $1\,\mathrm{Pa} = 1\,\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{m}^{2}}$ |
| Kilopascal | $1\,\mathrm{kPa} = 1000\,\mathrm{Pa}$ |
| Bar | $1\,\mathrm{bar} = 100{,}000\,\mathrm{Pa}$ |
| Meter of water column | $1\,\mathrm{mH}_{2} O = 9806.65\,\mathrm{Pa}$ under the conventional reference values |
| Meter of mercury column | The source uses $1\,\mathrm{mHg} \approx \left( \frac{1}{0.76} \right) \mathrm{atm}$ |
| Standard atmosphere | $1\,\mathrm{atm} = 101{,}325\,\mathrm{Pa}$ exactly; the slides round to $101{,}300\,\mathrm{Pa}$ |
| Torr | $760\,\mathrm{torr} = 1\,\mathrm{atm}$ |
| Millimeter of mercury | $1\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ is about $133.322\,\mathrm{Pa}$; the slides use $760\,\mathrm{mmHg} \approx 1\,\mathrm{atm}$ |
| Pound-force per square inch | 1 psi ≈ $6894.76\,\mathrm{Pa}$ |

Use one atmospheric-pressure convention throughout a calculation. Do not combine 101,300 and 101,325 as though they were different physical pressures in the same problem.

:::example Air in a room: distinguish the primary and companion problems
**Sources:** Primary p. 10; companion p. 2. **Find:** The air mass, then its weight.

1. Use the primary dimensions $4.0 \times 5.0 \times 3.0\,\mathrm{m}$. Their product is $60\,\mathrm{m}^{3}$.
2. Use its 20°C air density: $m = \rho V = 1.20 \left( 60 \right) = 72\,\mathrm{kg}$.
3. Calculate weight separately: $W = mg = 72 \left( 9.81 \right) = 706.32\,\mathrm{N}$.
4. Use the companion dimensions $4.0 \times 6.0 \times 2.5\,\mathrm{m}$. Their product also equals $60\,\mathrm{m}^{3}$.
5. Use the companion's 0°C table: $m = 1.29 \left( 60 \right) = 77.4\,\mathrm{kg}$, which rounds to $77\,\mathrm{kg}$.

**Check.** The volumes agree, but the selected air densities differ. The two mass answers therefore do not contradict each other.
:::

### Missing companion pressure and buoyancy problems

:::example Pressure on the submarine
**Source:** Companion p. 5. **Given:** Seawater density $1030\,\frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{m}^{3}}$, depth $300\,\mathrm{m}$, surface pressure $101{,}300\,\mathrm{Pa}$. Use $g = 9.8\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}^{2}}$ to match the slide.

1. Calculate the water contribution: $\Delta P = 1030 \left( 9.8 \right) \left( 300 \right) = 3{,}028{,}200\,\mathrm{Pa}$.
2. Add the surface pressure: $P_{\mathrm{abs}} = 101{,}300 + 3{,}028{,}200 = 3{,}129{,}500\,\mathrm{Pa}$.
3. Convert to atmospheres: $\frac{P_{\mathrm{abs}}}{101{,}300} = 30.893\,\mathrm{atm}$.
4. Report about $3.13 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{Pa}$ absolute, or $30.9\,\mathrm{atm}$.

**Check.** The gauge pressure is $3.0282 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{Pa}$. It is one atmosphere smaller than the absolute pressure.
:::

:::example Pressure at the closed end of the water tube
**Source:** Companion p. 6. **Given:** Open surface height $1.00\,\mathrm{m}$; closed-end height $0.40\,\mathrm{m}$ above the same reference. Surface pressure is $1\,\mathrm{atm}$.

1. Find the depth below the open surface: $h = 1.00 - 0.40 = 0.60\,\mathrm{m}$.
2. Move down through water, so add the hydrostatic term.
3. Calculate $P_{\mathrm{closed}} = 101{,}300 + 1000 \left( 9.81 \right) \left( 0.60 \right) = 107{,}186\,\mathrm{Pa}$.
4. Divide by 101,300 to obtain $1.0581\,\mathrm{atm}$, about $1.06\,\mathrm{atm}$.
5. Change the surface pressure to $1.50\,\mathrm{atm}$ for the follow-up question. The water contribution stays $0.0581\,\mathrm{atm}$.
6. Obtain a new closed-end pressure of $1.5581\,\mathrm{atm}$, about $1.56\,\mathrm{atm}$.

**Check.** Pascal's principle transfers the added $0.50\,\mathrm{atm}$ without changing the depth difference. The word “closed” does not make the pressure zero.
:::

:::derivation Find the force and displacement in a hydraulic lift
**Sources:** Primary pp. 21–23; companion p. 7. Assume an incompressible fluid, equal piston elevations, and negligible friction.

1. Write the common gauge pressure: $\Delta P = \frac{F_{1}}{A_{1}} = \frac{F_{2}}{A_{2}}$.
2. Isolate the output force: $F_{2} = \frac{F_{1} A_{2}}{A_{1}}$.
3. Replace circular areas by $\frac{\pi D^{2}}{4}$. The $\frac{\pi}{4}$ factors cancel.
4. Conserve displaced volume: $A_{1} d_{1} = A_{2} d_{2}$.
5. Isolate $d_{2} = \frac{d_{1} A_{1}}{A_{2}}$. The larger piston moves a smaller distance.

$$\frac{F_2}{F_1}=\left(\frac{D_2}{D_1}\right)^2,
\qquad \frac{d_2}{d_1}=\left(\frac{D_1}{D_2}\right)^2,
\qquad F_1d_1=F_2d_2.$$

For $5\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $25\,\mathrm{cm}$ diameters, $F_{2} = 100 \left( \frac{25}{5} \right)^{2} = 2500\,\mathrm{N}$. If the small piston moves $10\,\mathrm{cm}$, the large piston moves $\frac{10}{25} = 0.40\,\mathrm{cm}$. Multiplying the forces by their respective distances gives $10\,\mathrm{J}$ on either side.
:::

:::example A fully submerged block: buoyancy, apparent weight, and sinking
**Source:** Companion p. 9. **Given:** Displaced volume $0.020\,\mathrm{m}^{3}$; water density $1000\,\frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{m}^{3}}$; block weight $250\,\mathrm{N}$. Use $g = 9.8\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}^{2}}$.

1. Calculate $F_{B} = \rho_{f} gV = 1000 \left( 9.8 \right) \left( 0.020 \right) = 196\,\mathrm{N}$ upward.
2. Compare forces: the weight exceeds buoyancy by $250 - 196 = 54\,\mathrm{N}$.
3. If released without support, the block accelerates downward initially. It sinks.
4. If an upper string holds the block at rest, the string tension is $54\,\mathrm{N}$.

**Check.** The block still has buoyancy while sinking. “It sinks” does not mean $F_{B} = 0$.
:::

:::example Complete the $2\,\mathrm{kg}$ brass-block solution
**Source:** Primary p. 32, using the $8700\,\frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{m}^{3}}$ brass value on p. 9. Use $g = 9.81\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}^{2}}$.

1. Find the external volume: $V = \frac{m}{\rho _{\mathrm{brass}}} = \frac{2}{8700} = 2.29885 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}^{3}$.
2. Use that volume as the displaced-water volume because the block is fully submerged.
3. Calculate $F_{B} = 1000 \left( 9.81 \right) V = 2.25517\,\mathrm{N}$ upward.
4. Calculate the true weight: $mg = 2 \left( 9.81 \right) = 19.62\,\mathrm{N}$ downward.
5. Write vertical equilibrium: $T + F_{B} - mg = 0$.
6. Solve $T = 19.62 - 2.25517 = 17.3648\,\mathrm{N}$ upward.

**Value check.** If a question instead supplies $8600\,\frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{m}^{3}}$ from p. 8, $F_{B} \approx 2.2814\,\mathrm{N}$ and $T \approx 17.3386\,\mathrm{N}$. State which density you use.
:::

:::example Why an iceberg is mostly underwater
**Source:** Companion p. 11. **Given:** Ice density $917\,\frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{m}^{3}}$; seawater density $1030\,\frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{m}^{3}}$. Assume free floating equilibrium.

1. Set $F_{B} = mg$: $\rho _{\mathrm{sea}} gV_{\mathrm{sub}} = \rho _{\mathrm{ice}} gV_{\mathrm{total}}$.
2. Cancel g and divide by $\rho _{\mathrm{sea}}V_{\mathrm{total}}$.
3. Calculate $\frac{V_{\mathrm{sub}}}{V_{\mathrm{total}}} = \frac{917}{1030} = 0.890291$.
4. Express the submerged fraction as 89.0% and the exposed fraction as 11.0%.

**Check.** The fraction is below one because ice is less dense than seawater. Do not apply this equilibrium fraction to the tied-down wood block.
:::

> **Choose the correct force balance.** Free floating: $F_{B} = mg$. Upper support: $T + F_{B} = mg$. Bottom tether: $F_{B} = mg + T$. Start with the diagram before selecting an equation.

:::example The bottom-tethered wood block, step by step
**Source:** Companion p. 10. **Given:** A $10\,\mathrm{cm}$ cube, wood density $700\,\frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{m}^{3}}$, and water density $1000\,\frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{m}^{3}}$. Use $g = 9.81\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}^{2}}$.

1. Convert the side to $0.10\,\mathrm{m}$ and calculate $V = \left( 0.10 \right)^{3} = 0.001\,\mathrm{m}^{3}$.
2. Find the block's mass: $m = 700 \left( 0.001 \right) = 0.700\,\mathrm{kg}$.
3. Find its weight: $W = 0.700 \left( 9.81 \right) = 6.867\,\mathrm{N}$ downward.
4. Find buoyancy when fully submerged: $F_{B} = 1000 \left( 9.81 \right) \left( 0.001 \right) = 9.810\,\mathrm{N}$ upward.
5. Draw the string pulling downward toward its bottom attachment.
6. Set $F_{B} - W - T = 0$, giving $T = 9.810 - 6.867 = 2.943\,\mathrm{N}$.

**Check.** Releasing the string leaves a net upward force initially. The block rises until a smaller displaced volume permits floating equilibrium.
:::
