### Source tables, pressure units, and the two air examples

The PDFs use different air temperatures and slightly different material values. Read the conditions before choosing a density.

| Material or condition | Density in the source, kg/m³ | Where it appears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Air at 20°C, 1 atm | 1.20 | Primary pp. 8–10 |
| Air at 0°C, 1 atm | 1.29 | Companion p. 2 |
| Freshwater | 1000 | Both decks |
| Seawater | 1030 | Both decks |
| Ice | 920; companion iceberg uses 917 | Primary p. 8; companion p. 11 |
| Brass | 8600 in the table; 8700 in the relative-density example | Primary pp. 8–9 |
| Steel | 7800 | Primary pp. 8–9 |
| Wood | 500 in the relative-density example; 700 in the tied-block problem | Primary p. 9; companion p. 10 |

These are the source's classroom values. Temperature and composition affect real densities. The brass example below uses the explicit 8700 kg/m³ value from p. 9.

**Why a fluid has pressure — companion pp. 3–4.** Molecular motion and interactions transfer momentum to container walls. Gravity creates a vertical pressure gradient, but it is not the only cause of pressure. Gas molecules still collide with walls in weightlessness. Over a small laboratory height, a gas's gravitational pressure change can be small; atmospheric pressure changes substantially over larger heights. Internal and external pressure partly balance around the body, so atmospheric pressure does not act as an unopposed inward force everywhere.

**Relative-density check.** Steel: SG = 7800/1000 = 7.80. Brass: SG = 8700/1000 = 8.70. Wood: SG = 500/1000 = 0.500. Relative density has no kg/m³ unit because the density units cancel.

| Pressure unit | Relation |
| --- | --- |
| Pascal | 1 Pa = 1 N/m² |
| Kilopascal | 1 kPa = 1000 Pa |
| Bar | 1 bar = 100,000 Pa |
| Meter of water column | 1 mH₂O = 9806.65 Pa under the conventional reference values |
| Meter of mercury column | The source uses 1 mHg ≈ (1/0.76) atm |
| Standard atmosphere | 1 atm = 101,325 Pa exactly; the slides round to 101,300 Pa |
| Torr | 760 torr = 1 atm |
| Millimeter of mercury | 1 mmHg is about 133.322 Pa; the slides use 760 mmHg ≈ 1 atm |
| Pound-force per square inch | 1 psi ≈ 6894.76 Pa |

Use one atmospheric-pressure convention throughout a calculation. Do not combine 101,300 and 101,325 as though they were different physical pressures in the same problem.

:::example Air in a room: distinguish the primary and companion problems
**Sources:** Primary p. 10; companion p. 2. **Find:** The air mass, then its weight.

1. Use the primary dimensions 4.0 × 5.0 × 3.0 m. Their product is 60 m³.
2. Use its 20°C air density: m = ρV = 1.20(60) = 72 kg.
3. Calculate weight separately: W = mg = 72(9.81) = 706.32 N.
4. Use the companion dimensions 4.0 × 6.0 × 2.5 m. Their product also equals 60 m³.
5. Use the companion's 0°C table: m = 1.29(60) = 77.4 kg, which rounds to 77 kg.

**Check.** The volumes agree, but the selected air densities differ. The two mass answers therefore do not contradict each other.
:::

### Missing companion pressure and buoyancy problems

:::example Pressure on the submarine
**Source:** Companion p. 5. **Given:** Seawater density 1030 kg/m³, depth 300 m, surface pressure 101,300 Pa. Use g = 9.8 m/s² to match the slide.

1. Calculate the water contribution: ΔP = 1030(9.8)(300) = 3,028,200 Pa.
2. Add the surface pressure: Pabs = 101,300 + 3,028,200 = 3,129,500 Pa.
3. Convert to atmospheres: Pabs/101,300 = 30.893 atm.
4. Report about 3.13 × 10⁶ Pa absolute, or 30.9 atm.

**Check.** The gauge pressure is 3.0282 × 10⁶ Pa. It is one atmosphere smaller than the absolute pressure.
:::

:::example Pressure at the closed end of the water tube
**Source:** Companion p. 6. **Given:** Open surface height 1.00 m; closed-end height 0.40 m above the same reference. Surface pressure is 1 atm.

1. Find the depth below the open surface: h = 1.00 − 0.40 = 0.60 m.
2. Move down through water, so add the hydrostatic term.
3. Calculate Pclosed = 101,300 + 1000(9.81)(0.60) = 107,186 Pa.
4. Divide by 101,300 to obtain 1.0581 atm, about 1.06 atm.
5. Change the surface pressure to 1.50 atm for the follow-up question. The water contribution stays 0.0581 atm.
6. Obtain a new closed-end pressure of 1.5581 atm, about 1.56 atm.

**Check.** Pascal's principle transfers the added 0.50 atm without changing the depth difference. The word “closed” does not make the pressure zero.
:::

:::derivation Find the force and displacement in a hydraulic lift
**Sources:** Primary pp. 21–23; companion p. 7. Assume an incompressible fluid, equal piston elevations, and negligible friction.

1. Write the common gauge pressure: ΔP = F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂.
2. Isolate the output force: F₂ = F₁A₂/A₁.
3. Replace circular areas by πD²/4. The π/4 factors cancel.
4. Conserve displaced volume: A₁d₁ = A₂d₂.
5. Isolate d₂ = d₁A₁/A₂. The larger piston moves a smaller distance.

$$\frac{F_2}{F_1}=\left(\frac{D_2}{D_1}\right)^2,
\qquad \frac{d_2}{d_1}=\left(\frac{D_1}{D_2}\right)^2,
\qquad F_1d_1=F_2d_2.$$

For 5 cm and 25 cm diameters, F₂ = 100(25/5)² = 2500 N. If the small piston moves 10 cm, the large piston moves 10/25 = 0.40 cm. Multiplying the forces by their respective distances gives 10 J on either side.
:::

:::example A fully submerged block: buoyancy, apparent weight, and sinking
**Source:** Companion p. 9. **Given:** Displaced volume 0.020 m³; water density 1000 kg/m³; block weight 250 N. Use g = 9.8 m/s².

1. Calculate FB = ρfgV = 1000(9.8)(0.020) = 196 N upward.
2. Compare forces: the weight exceeds buoyancy by 250 − 196 = 54 N.
3. If released without support, the block accelerates downward initially. It sinks.
4. If an upper string holds the block at rest, the string tension is 54 N.

**Check.** The block still has buoyancy while sinking. “It sinks” does not mean FB = 0.
:::

:::example Complete the 2 kg brass-block solution
**Source:** Primary p. 32, using the 8700 kg/m³ brass value on p. 9. Use g = 9.81 m/s².

1. Find the external volume: V = m/ρbrass = 2/8700 = 2.29885 × 10⁻⁴ m³.
2. Use that volume as the displaced-water volume because the block is fully submerged.
3. Calculate FB = 1000(9.81)V = 2.25517 N upward.
4. Calculate the true weight: mg = 2(9.81) = 19.62 N downward.
5. Write vertical equilibrium: T + FB − mg = 0.
6. Solve T = 19.62 − 2.25517 = 17.3648 N upward.

**Value check.** If a question instead supplies 8600 kg/m³ from p. 8, FB ≈ 2.2814 N and T ≈ 17.3386 N. State which density you use.
:::

:::example Why an iceberg is mostly underwater
**Source:** Companion p. 11. **Given:** Ice density 917 kg/m³; seawater density 1030 kg/m³. Assume free floating equilibrium.

1. Set FB = mg: ρsea gVsub = ρice gVtotal.
2. Cancel g and divide by ρseaVtotal.
3. Calculate Vsub/Vtotal = 917/1030 = 0.890291.
4. Express the submerged fraction as 89.0% and the exposed fraction as 11.0%.

**Check.** The fraction is below one because ice is less dense than seawater. Do not apply this equilibrium fraction to the tied-down wood block.
:::

> **Choose the correct force balance.** Free floating: FB = mg. Upper support: T + FB = mg. Bottom tether: FB = mg + T. Start with the diagram before selecting an equation.

:::example The bottom-tethered wood block, step by step
**Source:** Companion p. 10. **Given:** A 10 cm cube, wood density 700 kg/m³, and water density 1000 kg/m³. Use g = 9.81 m/s².

1. Convert the side to 0.10 m and calculate V = (0.10)³ = 0.001 m³.
2. Find the block's mass: m = 700(0.001) = 0.700 kg.
3. Find its weight: W = 0.700(9.81) = 6.867 N downward.
4. Find buoyancy when fully submerged: FB = 1000(9.81)(0.001) = 9.810 N upward.
5. Draw the string pulling downward toward its bottom attachment.
6. Set FB−W−T = 0, giving T = 9.810−6.867 = 2.943 N.

**Check.** Releasing the string leaves a net upward force initially. The block rises until a smaller displaced volume permits floating equilibrium.
:::
