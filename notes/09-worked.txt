### Flux signs, partial overlap, and the induction experiment

**Starting and stopping the primary-coil current — primary pp. 5–7.** A steady primary current produces a steady field. A stationary secondary loop then has no transformer emf from that constant flux. Starting or stopping the primary current changes the shared flux and produces a secondary emf. The two coils need no conducting connection for this energy transfer.

**Flux sign convention.** Choose a surface normal first. A field component along that normal gives positive flux; the opposite component gives negative flux. Couple the positive loop direction to the normal by the right-hand grip rule. The Faraday minus sign then has a precise directional meaning.

:::example Partial magnetic-field overlap
**Source:** Primary p. 10. The loop encloses area $A_{1}$, but a perpendicular field exists only over a smaller enclosed area $A_{2}$.

1. Split the flux integral into the field region and the zero-field region.
2. Calculate $\Phi = BA_{2} + 0 \left( A_{1} - A_{2} \right) = BA_{2}$ for the aligned positive normal.
3. Reverse the surface normal to reverse the signed flux.

**Check.** $BA_{1}$ would incorrectly assign a nonzero field to the whole loop interior. The relevant area is the area where field actually passes through the chosen surface.
:::

:::example Maximum and minimum flux through the square loop
**Source:** Primary p. 11. **Given:** Side $10.0\,\mathrm{cm} = 0.100\,\mathrm{m}$ and $B = 1.25\,\mathrm{T}$.

1. Find $A = \left( 0.100 \right)^{2} = 0.0100\,\mathrm{m}^{2}$.
2. Use $\Phi = BA\cos \theta$, where $\theta$ is measured from the area normal.
3. Find the maximum magnitude at $\theta = 0^{\circ}$ or 180°: $|\Phi|_{\max}=0.0125\,\mathrm{Wb}$.
4. Find zero flux at $\theta = 90^{\circ}$.

**Wording check.** The minimum magnitude is zero. The minimum signed flux over all orientations is $- 0.0125\,\mathrm{Wb}$. State which meaning of “minimum” you use.
:::

### Full Faraday and transformer solutions

:::derivation The AC generator waveform and rotation units
**Source starting point:** Primary p. 14. Let a rigid N-turn coil of area A rotate at constant angular speed $\omega$ in a uniform field. Let $\theta$ be the angle between the area normal and B.

1. Write $\theta \left( t \right) = \omega t + \phi$, with angles in radians.
2. Write the flux per turn as $\Phi = BA\cos \left( \omega t + \phi \right)$.
3. Differentiate the cosine: $\frac{d\Phi}{dt} = - BA\omega \sin \left( \omega t + \phi \right)$.
4. Apply Faraday's minus sign and multiply by N.

$$\mathcal E(t)=NBA\omega\sin(\omega t+\phi),
\qquad \mathcal E_{peak}=NBA\omega,
\qquad \omega=2\pi f.$$

At maximum flux, the instantaneous flux slope and emf are zero. When the flux passes through zero during this rotation, the emf magnitude reaches its peak. Mechanical rotation supplies the electrical output energy. Do not substitute rotation frequency f in hertz directly for $\omega$ in radians per second.
:::

:::example The 200-turn square coil: emf and current
**Source:** Primary p. 18. **Given:** $N = 200$, side: $0.180\,\mathrm{m}$, $R_{\mathrm{total}} = 2.0 \Omega$, and perpendicular B rising from 0 to $0.500\,\mathrm{T}$ in $0.80\,\mathrm{s}$.

1. Find $A = 0.180^{2} = 0.0324\,\mathrm{m}^{2}$ for one turn.
2. Find the rate $\frac{\Delta B}{\Delta t} = \frac{0.500}{0.80} = 0.625\,\frac{\mathrm{T}}{\mathrm{s}}$.
3. Use $\left| \mathcal{E} \right| = NA \left| \frac{\Delta B}{\Delta t} \right| = 200 \left( 0.0324 \right) \left( 0.625 \right) = 4.05\,\mathrm{V}$.
4. Find the induced-current magnitude $I = \frac{\left| \mathcal{E} \right|}{R_{\mathrm{total}}} = \frac{4.05}{2.0} = 2.025\,\mathrm{A}$.
5. Find the direction separately with Lenz's law, using the actual external field direction.

**Check.** The coil's 2.0 Ω is already its total resistance. Do not multiply that resistance by the number of turns again.
:::

:::derivation Transformer turns and current ratios
**Sources:** Primary pp. 16–19. Assume ideal coupling, a common changing core flux per turn, and negligible losses.

1. Write the induced-voltage magnitudes $V_{p} = N_{p} \left| \frac{d\Phi}{dt} \right|$ and $V_{s} = N_{s} \left| \frac{d\Phi}{dt} \right|$.
2. Divide to cancel the common flux-change rate.
3. Use ideal average-power conservation for matching AC quantities.

$$\frac{V_s}{V_p}=\frac{N_s}{N_p},\qquad
V_pI_p=V_sI_s,\qquad
\frac{I_s}{I_p}=\frac{N_p}{N_s}.$$

A step-up transformer increases voltage and decreases current for the same ideal transferred power. A step-down transformer does the reverse. Current does not travel across the insulation from one winding into the other. The changing magnetic field couples the circuits.
:::

:::example Radio power supply: identify the ideal AC stage
**Source:** Primary p. 19. Use $V_{p} = 120\,\mathrm{V}$ AC, $V_{s} = 9.0\,\mathrm{V}$ AC for the transformer calculation, $N_{s} = 30$, and $I_{s} = 0.400\,\mathrm{A}$.

1. Rearrange the turns ratio: $N_{p} = \frac{N_{s} V_{p}}{V_{s}} = \frac{30 \left( 120 \right)}{9.0} = 400$ turns.
2. Find secondary power: $P_{s} = V_{s} I_{s} = 9.0 \left( 0.400 \right) = 3.6\,\mathrm{W}$.
3. Use ideal input power $P_{p} = 3.6\,\mathrm{W}$.
4. Find $I_{p} = \frac{P_{p}}{V_{p}} = \frac{3.6}{120} = 0.030\,\mathrm{A}$.

**Source correction.** The question describes a $9\,\mathrm{V}$ DC radio supply. A transformer alone supplies AC. Rectification and filtering are additional stages; the ideal turns-ratio calculation describes the AC transformer stage.
:::

**Source geometry:** Primary pp. 31–33. The following numerical values form a labeled extension: $B = 0.50\,\mathrm{T}$ out of the page, vertical bar length $\ell = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}$, rightward speed $v = 3.0\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}$, and loop resistance $R = 2.0 \Omega$.

2. Calculate the emf magnitude: $\left| \mathcal{E} \right| = B \ell v = 0.50 \left( 0.20 \right) \left( 3.0 \right) = 0.300\,\mathrm{V}$.
3. Calculate $I = \frac{\left| \mathcal{E} \right|}{R} = 0.150\,\mathrm{A}$. The bar carries current downward, so the loop current is clockwise.
4. Calculate the magnetic force on the bar: $F_{\mathrm{mag}} = I \ell B = 0.0150\,\mathrm{N}$ left.
5. Apply $0.0150\,\mathrm{N}$ right to maintain the constant speed.
6. Calculate mechanical input power: $Fv = 0.0450\,\mathrm{W}$.
7. Check resistive heating: $I^{2} R = 0.150^{2} \left( 2.0 \right) = 0.0450\,\mathrm{W}$.

**Check.** Increasing outward flux induces an inward field, consistent with clockwise current. The force opposes the motion, not the existence of the bar itself.

### The missing distinction: induced electric fields are not electrostatic fields

**Sources:** Primary objectives on p. 23 and discussion on p. 34. A changing magnetic flux creates a circulating electric field. It can act on a charge initially at rest. A conducting wire is not required for the electric field to exist.

For electrostatics, a single-valued scalar potential gives $E = - \nabla V$ and $\oint E \cdot dl = 0$. In induction, the closed-loop integral can be nonzero:

$$\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}.$$

The $E = vB$ inside a moving open rod instead describes charge separation that balances $qv \times B$ in that rod. It is not a universal formula for the circulating field around every changing magnetic field.

:::derivation Induced field inside and outside a circular changing-field region
This labeled extension develops the source objective. Let uniform B(t) point along +z inside radius a and be zero outside. Choose counterclockwise circulation as positive.

1. By cylindrical symmetry, E is tangent to a centered circle and has constant magnitude along it.
2. The circulation is $E\phi \left( 2 \pi r \right)$.
3. For $r \le a$, the enclosed magnetic flux is $\pi r^{2} B$.
4. For $r \ge a$, the enclosed magnetic flux is $\pi a^{2} B$.
5. Apply Faraday's law and solve each region separately.

$$E_\phi(r)=\begin{cases}
-\dfrac r2\dfrac{dB}{dt},&r\le a,\\[6pt]
-\dfrac{a^2}{2r}\dfrac{dB}{dt},&r\ge a.
\end{cases}$$

**Checks.** Both expressions agree at $r = a$. E tends to zero at the center. Outside, E can remain nonzero even where the local magnetic field is zero, because the loop encloses changing flux. For increasing +z flux, $E\phi$ is negative, so the circulation is clockwise.
:::

### Mutual inductance, self-inductance, and complete RL decay

:::derivation Derive self-inductance from flux linkage
**Sources:** Primary pp. 37–41. For a linear coil, the total linkage is $\Lambda = N\Phi = LI$. If L is constant, differentiation gives $\frac{d \Lambda}{dt} = L \left( \frac{dI}{dt} \right)$. Faraday's law therefore gives $\mathcal{E} _{\mathrm{self}} = - L \left( \frac{dI}{dt} \right)$.

For a long solenoid, substitute $B = \frac{\mu NI}{\ell}$ into $\Phi = BA$:

$$L=\frac{N\Phi}{I}
=\frac{N(\mu NI/\ell)A}{I}
=\frac{\mu N^2A}{\ell}.$$

For mutual inductance, use $\Lambda_{2} = N_{2} \Phi_{21} = MI_{1}$, so $\mathcal{E}_{2} = - M \left( \frac{dI_{1}}{dt} \right)$ for constant geometry and a linear material. A steady current gives no induced emf from these constant-linkage terms.
:::

:::example Interpret a mutual- or self-induced emf measurement
**Labeled extension of primary pp. 38–39.** Let $M = 0.040\,\mathrm{H}$ and let primary current rise by $2.0\,\mathrm{A}$ in $0.010\,\mathrm{s}$.

1. Find the average current-change rate: $\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t} = 200\,\frac{\mathrm{A}}{\mathrm{s}}$.
2. Calculate the secondary emf magnitude: $\left| \mathcal{E}_{2} \right| = M \left| \frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t} \right| = 8.0\,\mathrm{V}$.
3. Set its polarity with the chosen winding directions and Lenz's law.

For a self-inductor $L = 0.20\,\mathrm{H}$ whose current rises by $3.0\,\mathrm{A}$ in $0.050\,\mathrm{s}$, the average opposing-emf magnitude is $12\,\mathrm{V}$. The negative sign describes opposition to the current change. It does not mean the inductance is negative.
:::

:::derivation RL decay and the fraction-of-current time
**Sources:** Primary pp. 42–44. Remove the source while retaining a closed series R–L path. Choose the continuing current as positive.

1. Write the loop equation $L \left( \frac{dI}{dt} \right) + RI = 0$.
2. Separate $\frac{dI}{I} = - \left( \frac{R}{L} \right) dt$.
3. Integrate from $I_{0}$ at time zero to I(t).
4. Exponentiate, then isolate time for a specified remaining fraction.

$$\ln\frac{I}{I_0}=-\frac{R}{L}t
\Rightarrow I=I_0e^{-tR/L},
\qquad t=\frac LR\ln\frac{I_0}{I}.$$

The initial magnetic energy, $\tfrac{1}{2} LI_{0}^{2}$, becomes resistor heating during an ideal source-free decay. The current remains continuous through switching when the circuit supplies a finite-voltage discharge path.
:::

:::example The source's $87.5\,\mathrm{mH}$ coil
**Source:** Primary p. 44. **Given:** $L = 87.5\,\mathrm{mH} = 0.0875\,\mathrm{H}$ and total series $R = 0.250 \Omega$.

1. Calculate $\tau = \frac{L}{R} = \frac{0.0875}{0.250} = 0.350\,\mathrm{s}$.
2. For growth from zero, use $\frac{I}{I_{\mathrm{final}}} = 1 - \exp \left( - \frac{t}{\tau} \right)$.
3. Set the exact requested fraction 0.63: $0.37 = \exp \left( - \frac{t}{\tau} \right)$.
4. Calculate $t = - 0.350 \ln \left( 0.37 \right) = 0.347989\,\mathrm{s}$, about $0.348\,\mathrm{s}$.
5. If “63%” means the usual rounded one-time-constant result, $t \approx 0.350\,\mathrm{s}$. At that time the exact fraction is 63.2121%.

**Check.** The battery voltage cancels because the problem asks for a fraction of the final current. Include winding resistance in the total R.
:::

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Series RC | $V_{C} = 0$; current starts at $\frac{\mathcal{E}}{R}$ | $V_{C} \to \mathcal{E}$; current → 0 | RC |
| Series RL | $I = 0$; inductor initially has the source voltage | $I \to \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R}$; ideal inductor voltage → 0 | $\frac{L}{R}$ |

Increasing R makes RC charging slower for fixed C. Increasing R makes the RL normalized transient faster for fixed L, while also lowering its final current. Compare fractions and final values separately.
