Fluids: start with a force diagram

A fluid can flow. Liquids and gases are fluids. Fluid statics studies equilibrium; fluid dynamics studies motion. In the molecular model, gas particles are widely separated and compressible. A liquid has a definite volume but takes its container's shape. The hydrostatic formulas below require a stationary fluid, not an accelerating flow.

Density, relative density, and unit conversions

Density measures mass per volume. Relative density compares two densities and has no unit. Use the fluid density in buoyancy calculations, not the object's density.

$$\rho=\frac mV,\quad m=\rho V,\quad V=\frac m\rho,\quad w=mg=\rho Vg,\quad SG=\frac{\rho}{\rho_{water}}.$$

For a composite body, divide the total mass by the total external volume. For separate parts with additive volumes, use ρavg = Σm/ΣV. A hollow object's average density includes its empty interior. Water is about 1000 kg/m³ in the classroom model. Convert 1 g/cm³ to 1000 kg/m³. Also use 1 L = 10⁻³ m³, 1 mL = 1 cm³, and 1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³. Squaring and cubing conversion factors is essential.

Worked example — primary p. 10. A room measures 4.0 × 5.0 × 3.0 m. With ρair = 1.20 kg/m³, V = 60 m³. Therefore m = 1.20 × 60 = 72 kg. Its weight is 72 × 9.81 = 706.32 N. Mass and weight have different units.

Pressure and the hydrostatic derivation

Pressure is normal force per area: P = F⊥/A. One pascal equals one N/m². Rearrange to F⊥ = PA or A = F⊥/P. Pressure is a scalar; the force on each wall points perpendicular to that wall. A static fluid cannot sustain a shear stress.

Derivation. Isolate a vertical fluid column of area A and depth h. The downward forces are P₀A and its weight ρAhg. The upward force is PbottomA. Equilibrium gives:

$$P_bA-P_0A-\rho Ahg=0\Rightarrow P_b=P_0+\rho gh.$$

The area cancels. This explains why pressure at a given depth does not depend on container shape. Equal pressure at equal depth requires the same connected, stationary fluid under the same surface conditions.

$$\Delta P=\rho gh,\quad h=\frac{\Delta P}{\rho g},\quad \rho=\frac{\Delta P}{gh},\quad F=(P_0+\rho gh)A.$$

Pressure differences across a wall use the pressure on BOTH sides. Do not multiply an absolute pressure by area when the question asks the net force against outside air. For nonconstant density, the general vertical relation is dP/dz = −ρ(z)g; integrate instead of assuming ρgh.

Worked example — diver, primary p. 17. At 20 m in freshwater, Pgauge = 1000 × 9.8 × 20 = 196,000 Pa. Add 101,300 Pa for an absolute pressure of 297,300 Pa. The statement “pressure due to water” means the gauge contribution here.

Worked example — two liquids, primary p. 19. At 3.00 m and g = 9.81 m/s², freshwater gives 130,730 Pa absolute. Seawater with ρ = 1030 gives 131,612.9 Pa. Their difference is 882.9 Pa, because the same atmospheric term cancels.

Atmospheric, gauge, absolute, and connected-column cases

$$P_{abs}=P_{atm}+P_g,\quad P_g=P_{abs}-P_{atm}.$$

The slides use 1 atm ≈ 101.3 kPa = 760 torr ≈ 760 mmHg. Gauge pressure can be negative; absolute pressure cannot be negative. Vacuum devices do not create a pulling pressure. A pressure difference lets the higher-pressure surroundings push inward.

For a manometer, start at a known pressure. Add ρgΔh when moving DOWN through a fluid. Subtract it when moving UP. Keep each fluid's density separate. Set pressures equal at the same level only within the same connected fluid. For multiple stationary layers, Pbottom = Ptop + Σρᵢghᵢ.

Worked example — companion p. 6 type. A closed water column's gas interface is 0.60 m below an open water surface. Pgas = 101,300 + 1000 × 9.81 × 0.60 = 107,186 Pa, about 1.058 atm. A different interface height reverses the sign. The drawing, not the word “closed,” decides the height contribution.

Pascal's principle and hydraulic displacement

An applied pressure change travels through a confined fluid. At equal piston elevations and neglecting friction, ΔP₁ = ΔP₂:

$$\frac{F_1}{A_1}=\frac{F_2}{A_2},\quad F_2=F_1\frac{A_2}{A_1}=F_1\left(\frac{r_2}{r_1}\right)^2.$$

Derive the distance relation. Conservation of incompressible fluid volume gives A₁d₁ = A₂d₂. Thus d₂/d₁ = A₁/A₂. Multiplying by the force ratio gives F₁d₁ = F₂d₂: force multiplication is not energy creation. For unequal piston heights, include the hydrostatic pressure difference before applying the force balance.

Worked example — primary p. 23. A 10,000 N load sits on a piston of radius 1.00 m. The smaller radius is 0.050 m. Required input force = 10,000(0.050/1.00)² = 25 N. A diameter ratio gives the same squared ratio if both inputs are diameters.

Worked example — companion p. 7. Diameters 5 cm and 25 cm give an area ratio of 25. An input of 100 N produces 2500 N ideally. The large piston moves only 1/25 of the smaller piston's distance.

Archimedes' principle: derive it before choosing a volume

Pressure at a submerged block's bottom exceeds the pressure at its top. For constant area A, the net upward fluid force is (Pbottom − Ptop)A = ρfg(hbottom − htop)A. Since AΔh = Vdisplaced:

$$F_B=\rho_f gV_{disp}.$$

This relation uses the volume of displaced fluid. For a fully submerged impermeable body, it equals the object's external volume. For partial immersion, it does not equal the whole object volume. A fluid's pressure gradient produces buoyancy even without a free surface immediately beside the object.

For a hanging object in equilibrium, T + FB − mg = 0, so T = mg − FB. Apparent weight equals that tension. If a light object is tied DOWN, FB − mg − T = 0, so T = FB − mg. Choose the sign from the diagram.

Worked example — primary p. 32 type. A 2.00 kg brass block has density 8700 kg/m³ and is fully submerged in water. V = 2/8700 = 2.299 × 10⁻⁴ m³. FB = 2.255 N. Its upward-string tension is 19.62 − 2.255 = 17.365 N. The density is the chosen brass-table value; a different supplied value changes the result.

Worked example — companion pp. 9–10. A 0.10 m cube of wood has density 700 kg/m³. V = 0.001 m³, mg = 6.867 N, and FB = 9.81 N. A bottom string must pull downward with 2.943 N. A negative “upward tension” would indicate the wrong assumed string direction.

Floating, sinking, and identifying unknown density

For free floating equilibrium, FB = mg. With m = ρobjectVtotal:

$$\frac{V_{sub}}{V_{total}}=\frac{\rho_{object}}{\rho_f},\quad \rho_f=\rho_{object}\frac{V_{total}}{V_{sub}}.$$

This fraction must lie between zero and one. If the average object density exceeds the fluid density, a freely floating equilibrium with partial immersion is impossible. A sinking object still has an upward buoyant force; that force is simply insufficient to balance its weight. Neutral buoyancy means equal average densities, not zero buoyancy.

Worked example — primary p. 34. One-third of a person's volume is above the surface, so two-thirds is submerged. With average density 970 kg/m³, fluid density = 970/(2/3) = 1455 kg/m³. The printed 1460 is a rounded value. The assumed 3 m³ is only a convenient volume ratio, not a realistic measured body volume.

Take note. Primary p. 12 labels density with Pa; its unit must be kg/m³. Floating formulas require equilibrium without an extra vertical support force. Do not use them for a block held underwater by a string.

Derivation extension — weighing an unknown solid. Measure the weight Wair in air, then the apparent weight Wsub while fully submerged. Neglect air buoyancy and string mass. The difference is the fluid buoyancy: Wair − Wsub = ρf gV. Meanwhile Wair = ρobject gV. Divide the equations to remove both g and V:

$$\rho_{object}=\rho_f\frac{W_{air}}{W_{air}-W_{sub}}.$$

For Wair = 12.0 N and Wsub = 8.0 N in water, density = 1000(12)/(12−8) = 3000 kg/m³. A smaller apparent weight means a larger buoyant force. This formula does not apply unchanged to an object resting on the bottom.

Worked extension — a layered column. An open tank holds 0.20 m of oil, density 800 kg/m³, above 0.30 m of water. Gauge pressure at the bottom is 800(9.81)(0.20) + 1000(9.81)(0.30) = 4512.6 Pa. A small horizontal bottom hatch of area 0.010 m², exposed to air underneath, feels a net downward force of 45.126 N. Atmospheric pressure cancels across the hatch. For a tall vertical hatch, pressure varies over its area; use F = ∫Pdepth dA, not the pressure at its lowest point everywhere.

Thermal physics: separate heat, temperature, and internal energy

Temperature describes thermal state. Heat Q is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. Internal energy U belongs to the system. Heat is not a stored substance or another name for U. Work is a different mode of energy transfer. The source sometimes uses these terms interchangeably; the notes keep them separate.

Temperature scales and differences

$$T_K=T_C+273.15,\quad T_F=\frac95T_C+32,\quad T_C=\frac59(T_F-32).$$

Derivation. Water's standard freezing/boiling interval spans 100 Celsius degrees and 180 Fahrenheit degrees. The slope is 180/100 = 9/5. The freezing-point offset supplies 32. Kelvin uses the same interval size as Celsius but a different zero.

$$\Delta T_K=\Delta T_C,\quad \Delta T_F=\frac95\Delta T_C.$$

Do not add 273.15 to a temperature CHANGE. A 20°C rise is a 20 K rise, not 293.15 K. Gas-law absolute-temperature ratios require Kelvin. Absolute zero does not mean every quantum-mechanical contribution to energy vanishes.

Thermometers measure temperature through a calibrated property, such as a liquid's volume, gas pressure, or electrical resistance. Mercury's illustrated −39°C to 357°C liquid range explains one thermometer limitation; it is not a universal temperature scale limit.

Linear, area, and volume expansion: derivations and inverses

For moderate changes with approximately constant coefficient, ΔL = αL₀ΔT and L = L₀(1 + αΔT). The coefficient has units K⁻¹ or °C⁻¹.

$$\alpha=\frac{L-L_0}{L_0\Delta T},\quad \Delta T=\frac{L-L_0}{\alpha L_0},\quad L_0=\frac{L}{1+\alpha\Delta T}.$$

Derive area expansion. Expand both dimensions by the same factor: A/A₀ = (1 + αΔT)² = 1 + 2αΔT + α²ΔT². Neglect the second-order term only when |αΔT| ≪ 1. Then ΔA ≈ 2αA₀ΔT. The slides call the area coefficient γ; γ ≈ 2α.

Derive volume expansion. Three dimensions give V/V₀ = (1 + αΔT)³. Its first-order term is 1 + 3αΔT. Thus β ≈ 3α for an isotropic solid, and ΔV ≈ βV₀ΔT. Liquids have their own volume coefficient; do not assign them a solid's α without justification. The finite-power expressions follow the linear dimensional model; a temperature-dependent instantaneous coefficient requires integration.

A hole expands as the surrounding material would expand if the hole were filled with the same material. Heating both a plug and hole made from the same material does not automatically create clearance. Cooling only the plug or heating only the plate changes their relative fit.

Worked example — primary p. 11. A 50 m steel tape at 20°C has α = 11 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹. At 35°C, ΔL = 0.00825 m and L = 50.00825 m. The printed 50.008 m is rounded. A hot tape has wider-spaced markings; distinguish the indicated reading from the actual length.

Containers, overflow, water's anomaly, and phase diagrams

For a full container and a small common temperature rise:

$$\Delta V_{overflow}=V_0(\beta_{liquid}-\beta_{container})\Delta T.$$

Derivation. Subtract the expanded container capacity V₀(1 + βcontainerΔT) from the expanded liquid volume V₀(1 + βliquidΔT). The original V₀ terms cancel. A negative result means no positive overflow in this model; the liquid leaves space instead.

Worked example — primary p. 18. V₀ = 200 cm³; ΔT = 80 K; βHg = 18 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹; αglass = 0.40 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹. Therefore βglass = 1.20 × 10⁻⁵. Overflow = 200(18 − 1.2) × 10⁻⁵ × 80 = 2.688 cm³. Keep volume units consistent; conversion to m³ is not necessary when every volume uses cm³.

Companion extension. Water contracts when heated from about 0°C to 4°C at ordinary pressure. Its density reaches a maximum near 4°C. “All matter expands when heated” is not a universal law. A phase diagram plots pressure against temperature. Boundaries separate phases; the triple point joins three phases. A liquid–gas boundary ends at the critical point. Do not confuse a phase diagram with a heating curve.

Heat transfer, specific heat, and thermal equilibrium

Conduction transfers energy through microscopic interactions; metals also have mobile-electron contributions. Convection carries energy with bulk fluid motion. Radiation transfers electromagnetic energy and needs no material medium. The source's explanation that solid molecules are generally far apart is incorrect.

The zeroth law states: if A and B separately share thermal equilibrium with C, A and B share thermal equilibrium. Equality of temperature, not equal mass or equal internal energy, characterizes that equilibrium.

$$Q=mc(T_f-T_i),\quad c=\frac{Q}{m\Delta T},\quad m=\frac{Q}{c\Delta T},\quad T_f=T_i+\frac{Q}{mc}.$$

The relation assumes no phase change and approximately constant c. Heat capacity C = mc has units J/K. Specific heat c has units J/(kg·K). With variable c, Q = m∫c(T)dT. The PDFs use 1 cal = 4.186 J and cwater = 4186 J/(kg·K). Use the supplied convention consistently. One kcal is 1000 cal; the source uses 1 Btu ≈ 252 cal.

Worked example — primary p. 32. Water of mass 0.625 kg initially at 75°C loses 7.96 × 10⁴ J. Use Q = −79,600 J. Tf = 75 − 79,600/(0.625 × 4186) ≈ 44.575°C, about 44.6°C. A positive loss magnitude must acquire a negative sign in the system energy equation.

Mixing: derive the common final temperature

For an insulated mixture without phase changes, Σmc(Tf − Ti) = 0. Expand the sum and collect Tf:

$$T_f=\frac{\sum_i m_ic_iT_i}{\sum_i m_ic_i}.$$

Include a calorimeter as another heat-capacity term if its energy is not negligible. Without phase change or external heating, Tf lies between the initial temperatures. That is a useful sign check.

Worked example — primary p. 33. Iron: 0.065 kg at 525°C, c = 444 J/(kg·K). Water: 0.635 kg at 15°C, c = 4186. The weighted formula gives Tf ≈ 20.478°C, about 20.5°C. Neither the simple arithmetic average nor the mass-weighted average alone is correct when c differs.

Latent heat and every stage of an ice-to-steam problem

During an equilibrium phase transition at the specified pressure, Q = ±mL. Melting and vaporization absorb heat; freezing and condensation release heat. Lf belongs to solid–liquid transitions. Lv belongs to liquid–gas transitions. Primary p. 38 incorrectly repeats melting/freezing labels for vaporization.

Decision method. Bring material to the phase boundary with mcΔT. Use mL for the transition. Then use the new phase's specific heat. Never apply a single water c across ice, liquid, and steam.

Worked example — primary p. 41. Convert 0.100 kg of ice at −40°C to steam at 110°C. Use cice = 2090, cwater = 4186, csteam = 2110 J/(kg·K), Lf = 3.35 × 10⁵ and Lv = 2.26 × 10⁶ J/kg.

$$Q=0.1(2090)(40)+0.1(335000)+0.1(4186)(100)+0.1(2260000)+0.1(2110)(10)=311830\ \mathrm J.$$

The five contributions are 8360, 33,500, 41,860, 226,000, and 2110 J. The result rounds to 3.12 × 10⁵ J. The latent-heat stage dominates here.

For ice mixed with warm water, compare the available cooling energy with mLf before assuming all ice melts. If the energy is insufficient, the final mixture can stay at 0°C with some ice remaining. If all ice melts, subtract the melting energy before solving the later temperature rise. The companion's milk-and-ice case uses this energy-budget logic.

Worked extension — test the final phase first. Mix 0.050 kg of ice at 0°C with 0.200 kg of water at 30°C. Use cwater = 4186 and Lf = 335,000 in SI units. The water can release 0.200(4186)(30) = 25,116 J before reaching 0°C. Melting all the ice needs 0.050(335,000) = 16,750 J. All the ice therefore melts. The remaining 8366 J warms 0.250 kg of liquid water, giving Tf = 8366/(0.250×4186) ≈ 7.994°C.

Change only the ice mass to 0.100 kg. Melting it all needs 33,500 J, more than 25,116 J. The final temperature stays 0°C. Melted mass = 25,116/335,000 ≈ 0.07497 kg; about 0.02503 kg of ice remains. Do not report a negative final liquid temperature from an equation that incorrectly assumes complete melting.

First law and two sign conventions

The primary uses ΔU = Q − Wby. The companion uses ΔU = Q + Won. They agree because Won = −Wby. Q is positive into the system. A closed system exchanges energy but not matter; an isolated system exchanges neither.

Worked example — primary p. 45. Qnet = 40 − 25 = 15 J. Wby,net = 10 − 4 = 6 J. ΔU = 15 − 6 = 9 J. Adding the 4 J of compression to Wby would reverse its meaning.

Companion example. Heat input 1500 J, heat loss 400 J, expansion work 600 J, and paddle-wheel work ON the gas 250 J give ΔU = 1500 − 400 − 600 + 250 = 750 J.

Extension — steady conduction. Fourier's one-dimensional law gives heat rate H = kA(Th − Tc)/L for a uniform slab. Derive Q = Ht; rearrange k = HL/(AΔT). For stacked slabs, thermal resistance is L/(kA), so H = ΔT/ΣRthermal. This is the conduction relation shown in the scanned companion, not an electrical resistance formula.

Electric charge and force: signs before substitution

Charge is a property of matter, not a synonym for “electron.” Electrons carry −e, protons +e, and neutrons zero net charge. Use e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C in course calculations. A positive net charge usually indicates an electron deficit. A negative net charge indicates excess electrons. Ordinary rubbing transfers electrons rather than creating charge.

Quantization, conservation, and charging mechanisms

$$q=(N_p-N_e)e,\quad N=\frac{|q|}{e},\quad \Delta m=N m_e.$$

For ordinary isolated charged bodies, q is an integer multiple of e. An isolated system conserves total signed charge: Σqbefore = Σqafter. Neutral means balanced net charge, not absence of charged particles. Charge is frame invariant in the classical situations covered here.

Worked example — primary p. 8. A charge of +0.32 C represents 0.32/(1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 1.998 × 10¹⁸ removed electrons. Its mass decreases by about 1.82 × 10⁻¹² kg if only electron transfer changes its mass. Neutrino oscillations show that neutrinos are not all massless. The source's blanket zero-rest-mass statement is inaccurate and unnecessary for this charge calculation.

Conduction requires contact and permits charge transfer. Friction transfers charge during rubbing. Induction separates charge without contact with the inducing body. To leave a single conductor with net charge by the standard grounding method, approach the charged rod, connect the ground, remove the ground, then remove the rod. Changing that order can remove the intended final charge. Polarization separates positive and negative charge slightly within a neutral object; attraction does not prove the object has acquired a net charge.

Conductors have mobile charges. Insulators restrict bulk charge motion. Semiconductors have conductivity between these broad categories and strongly depend on material conditions. In electrostatic equilibrium, excess conductor charge lies on its surface; the field inside conducting material is zero. These statements concern equilibrium, not every current-carrying wire.

Coulomb's law: empirical starting law and algebraic forms

For stationary point charges in vacuum, use k ≈ 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C². Coulomb's inverse-square law is an empirical starting law here; it cannot be independently proved just by rearranging its definition.

$$F=k\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2},\quad r=\sqrt{\frac{k|q_1q_2|}{F}},\quad |q_1|=\frac{Fr^2}{k|q_2|}.$$

Use magnitudes in this scalar formula. Determine attraction or repulsion separately. Like signs repel; opposite signs attract. Doubling r divides F by four. Tripling one charge triples F. In a uniform linear medium with relative permittivity κ, replace ε₀ by κε₀ if the problem justifies that model.

Worked example — primary p. 22. q₁ = +25 nC, q₂ = −75 nC, r = 3.0 cm = 0.030 m. F = (8.99 × 10⁹)(25 × 10⁻⁹)(75 × 10⁻⁹)/(0.030)² = 0.01873 N. Both forces have this magnitude. Their directions are opposite and attractive. A larger charge does not experience the larger force in this two-body pair.

Superposition in one, two, and three dimensions

Define the displacement from source j to the target i as ri − rj. This definition prevents ambiguous subscript conventions:

$$\mathbf F_i=\sum_{j\ne i} kq_iq_j\frac{\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j}{|\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j|^3}.$$

Derive the component form. A unit displacement vector is Δr/r. Multiplying it by kqiqj/r² gives the denominator r³. Therefore Fx = ΣkqiqjΔx/r³, with analogous y and z terms. Combine components before computing magnitude:

$$F=\sqrt{F_x^2+F_y^2+F_z^2},\quad \theta=\operatorname{atan2}(F_y,F_x).$$

Ordinary arctan(Fy/Fx) needs a quadrant check. Forces on different targets cannot be added to answer “net force on q₁.” Exclude the target's own field.

Worked example — primary pp. 24–25. A +5 nC target is at x = 0. A +1 nC source at +0.020 m repels it left with 112.375 μN. A −3 nC source at +0.040 m attracts it right with 84.281 μN. Fx = −112.375 + 84.281 = −28.094 μN. Thus the net force is about 28 μN left. Adding the magnitudes would be wrong.

Rectangles, diagonals, and force cancellation

For the rectangular arrangement in primary pp. 26–28, draw all forces on the SAME corner charge. A 3–4–5 rectangle has diagonal r = 5 units. A diagonal force's component factors are 4/5 and 3/5 when the horizontal side is 4 units. Use the directions in the drawing; a magnitude of 23 μN does not decide its component signs.

The source's working mixes a downward −90° direction with a positive j term. Recompute from the arrows instead of copying that sign. If a diagonal force points upper-left, its components are (−4F/5, +3F/5). Add the horizontal and vertical forces algebraically after resolving the diagonal.

For two like-signed sources on a line, a cancellation point lies between them. For opposite signs, their fields reinforce between them; a possible zero-field point lies outside on the smaller-magnitude charge's side. Solve in each physically allowed region. Squaring distance equations can produce an invalid location.

Derive the cancellation location. Put positive Q₁ at x = 0 and positive Q₂ at x = d. A nonzero test charge between them has opposing forces. Cancel its magnitude and k: Q₁/x² = Q₂/(d−x)². Both distances are positive in this region. Taking positive square roots and collecting x gives:

$$\sqrt{Q_1}(d-x)=\sqrt{Q_2}x\Rightarrow x=\frac{d\sqrt{Q_1}}{\sqrt{Q_1}+\sqrt{Q_2}}.$$

For +4 nC and +9 nC separated by 1.00 m, x = 2/(2+3) = 0.400 m from +4 nC. The fields there have equal magnitudes and opposite directions. The point is closer to the smaller charge because its weaker source needs a smaller distance to match the other field.

Opposite-sign extension. Put +4 nC at x = 0 and −9 nC at x = 1.00 m. Between them, both fields point right. Test x < 0 instead: 4/x² = 9/(1−x)². Positive distances give 2(1−x) = 3(−x), so x = −2.00 m. Substitution gives equal field magnitudes 8.99 N/C, with opposite directions. Equal opposite charges have no finite zero-field point on their joining line.

Force comparison — primary p. 21. For a proton and electron separated by 5.3 × 10⁻¹¹ m, Fe ≈ 8.21 × 10⁻⁸ N and Fg ≈ 3.62 × 10⁻⁴⁷ N. Their ratio is ke²/(Gmemp), independent of r. Both forces attract, but they are not comparable in size. Keep the exponents; the PDF's text extraction can obscure minus signs.

Electric fields: calculate the field before inserting a test charge

An electric field assigns a force-per-charge vector to each point. It is not merely an area with electricity. A source charge produces its field even when no test particle is present. Define field direction by the force on a positive test charge.

$$\mathbf E=\frac{\mathbf F}{q_0},\quad \mathbf F=q_0\mathbf E,\quad [E]=\mathrm{N/C}=\mathrm{V/m}.$$

For a negative test charge, force opposes E. The source charge sets the field; the test charge sets the force experienced in that field. The ideal test charge is small enough not to rearrange the source distribution significantly.

Derive the point-charge field and its inverse forms

Substitute Coulomb's force into E = F/q₀ and cancel the positive test-charge magnitude:

$$E=\frac{k|Q|}{r^2},\quad |Q|=\frac{Er^2}{k},\quad r=\sqrt{\frac{k|Q|}{E}}.$$

E points away from positive Q and toward negative Q. The field magnitude decreases as 1/r². The value at r = 0 is not finite in the point-charge idealization.

Worked example — primary p. 12. A +3.0 × 10⁻⁸ C test charge experiences 6.0 × 10⁻⁸ N. E = 2.0 N/C. Replacing it with +12 × 10⁻⁸ C gives F = 2.4 × 10⁻⁷ N in the same direction. Replacing it with a negative charge reverses the force, not the pre-existing field.

Worked examples — primary pp. 29–30. A +4.0 nC source gives E = 9.0 N/C at 2.0 m when k = 9.0 × 10⁹. Conversely, E = 5 N/C at 1.50 m requires |Q| = 1.25 × 10⁻⁹ C. Magnitude alone does not identify the source's sign.

Field lines, dipoles, and equipattern reasoning

The tangent to a field line gives the local field direction. Denser lines indicate stronger field. Lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges or at infinity. Line counts communicate relative magnitude; they are not physical wires. Electrostatic field lines do not cross because the field has one direction at a regular point.

A dipole has equal and opposite separated charges. Equal like charges can cancel at their midpoint. Equal opposite charges do not cancel their midpoint fields: both point from positive to negative. A curved field line is not generally a charged particle's trajectory; motion also depends on initial velocity and inertia.

Discrete-source superposition and a midpoint example

$$\mathbf E(\mathbf r)=k\sum_j Q_j\frac{\mathbf r-\mathbf r_j}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_j|^3}.$$

Worked example — primary p. 32. Put −5.00 nC on the left and −3.00 nC on the right, 1.50 m apart. At their midpoint r = 0.75 m from each. Eleft = 80 N/C left and Eright = 48 N/C right. The result is 32 N/C left. Use 0.75 m, not the full separation, for BOTH denominators.

Derivation — the scanned companion's symmetric three-charge geometry. Put equal charges q at (0, −a), (0, 0), and (0, +a). Evaluate at (x, 0), with x > 0. The upper/lower y components cancel. Each outer charge contributes kqx/(x²+a²)³ᐟ² horizontally. The central charge contributes kq/x². Thus Ex = kq[1/x² + 2x/(x²+a²)³ᐟ²], Ey = 0. The signs reverse when q is negative. This result belongs to this stated geometry only.

Uniform-field motion: derive the trajectory

Combine Newton's law ma = qE with constant-acceleration kinematics. For a constant field along y:

$$a_y=\frac{qE_y}{m},\quad x=x_0+v_{0x}t,\quad y=y_0+v_{0y}t+\frac{qE_y}{2m}t^2.$$

When v₀x ≠ 0, eliminate t = (x − x₀)/v₀x. The resulting y(x) is parabolic. An electron has q = −e; do not substitute +e for its acceleration. A particle initially moving along E can speed up or slow down depending on q and its velocity direction.

Worked example. An electron enters horizontally at 2.0 × 10⁶ m/s through a 0.020 m long uniform-field region. Let Ey = +100 N/C and y₀ = v₀y = 0. Transit time = 1.0 × 10⁻⁸ s. ay = −1.759 × 10¹³ m/s². Exit displacement = −8.79 × 10⁻⁴ m and vy = −1.759 × 10⁵ m/s. A plate collision invalidates the full-length transit assumption. Relativistic speeds require relativistic dynamics.

Continuous charge distributions: a labeled extension

The primary objectives mention continuous distributions but do not develop all standard integrations. Use dq = λdl for a line, σdA for a surface, or ρqdV for a volume. Here ρq is charge density, not mass density.

$$\mathbf E=k\int\frac{\hat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}\,dq.$$

Uniform finite rod. At perpendicular distance a from the midpoint of a rod extending from −L to +L, transverse components add and longitudinal components cancel. Integrate dE⊥ = kλa dx/(x²+a²)³ᐟ² to obtain E = 2kλL/[a√(L²+a²)]. As L → ∞, E → 2kλ/a. The result must tend toward the point-charge field k(2λL)/a² when a ≫ L.

The antiderivative follows from d[x/√(x²+a²)]/dx = a²/(x²+a²)³ᐟ². With a > 0 and λ ≥ 0, the outward field magnitude is:

$$E_\perp=k\lambda a\left[\frac{x}{a^2\sqrt{x^2+a^2}}\right]_{-L}^{L}=\frac{2k\lambda L}{a\sqrt{L^2+a^2}}.$$

For λ = 2.0 nC/m, L = 0.30 m, and a = 0.40 m, E = 53.94 N/C. The total rod length is 2L = 0.60 m. Confusing L with the total length changes both the charge and the geometry.

Uniform ring. At axial distance x from a ring of radius R, each element has distance √(x²+R²). Radial components cancel. Integrating the axial part gives Ex = kQx/(x²+R²)³ᐟ². At x = 0, E = 0 although the potential is generally not zero.

Disk and infinite-sheet limits. Build a uniformly charged disk from rings with dQ = σ2πr dr. For x > 0, Ex = σ/(2ε₀)[1 − x/√(x²+R²)]. Let R → ∞ to obtain E = σ/(2ε₀) for one isolated infinite sheet. Two oppositely charged ideal capacitor sheets add to σ/ε₀ between them. Do not confuse the one-sheet and two-sheet factors.

The disk integration keeps the ring radius r separate from the fixed axial distance x:

$$E_x=2\pi k\sigma x\int_0^R\frac{r\,dr}{(x^2+r^2)^{3/2}}=2\pi k\sigma x\left[-\frac1{\sqrt{x^2+r^2}}\right]_0^R.$$

For x > 0, the lower endpoint uses √(x²) = x. Substitute k = 1/(4πε₀) to recover the stated disk result. On the opposite side, the normal field direction reverses for the same positive surface charge.

Electric potential: scalar sums, signed energy

Electric potential V is energy per charge. Potential energy U belongs to a charge configuration. One volt is one joule per coulomb. A point can have nonzero V and zero E; a zero potential does not imply zero field.

Work, energy, and consistent endpoint labels

For a constant force, W = Fd cos θ. More generally W = ∫F·dl. The net-work theorem is Wnet = ΔK. For the electrostatic force, Wfield = −ΔU. A slow external mover with no kinetic-energy change does Wexternal = +ΔU. The external-work equality requires those conditions; it is not true for arbitrary accelerated motion.

$$\Delta U=q(V_b-V_a),\quad W_{field}=q(V_a-V_b),\quad \Delta K=-q\Delta V.$$

The ΔK expression assumes that the electrostatic force supplies all the net work. Include other forces separately when they do work. Some slides define Vab = Va − Vb. These notes define ΔV = Vb − Va when discussing a move a → b. Write the endpoint order before substituting. The primary's statement that work equals voltage omits division by charge: work PER charge equals an appropriate potential difference.

Worked example. A +2 μC charge moves from 100 V to 40 V. ΔV = −60 V, ΔU = −120 μJ, and Wfield = +120 μJ. For −2 μC over the same endpoints, the energy and work signs reverse. The field direction still points toward decreasing potential.

Derive point-charge potential from field

Choose V(∞) = 0 for an isolated source. Integrate the radial electric field:

$$V(r)-V(\infty)=-\int_\infty^r\frac{kQ}{s^2}\,ds=\frac{kQ}{r}.$$

Keep the sign of Q. Rearrange to Q = Vr/k or r = kQ/V when the signs produce a positive r. A zero-V denominator cannot locate a finite nonzero-charge equipotential. For multiple fixed sources, V = kΣQᵢ/rᵢ. Add scalar signed contributions, not vector components.

Worked example — primary p. 34. At 0.50 m from ±20 μC, V = ±3.6 × 10⁵ V with k = 9 × 10⁹. The distance is positive in both cases. The source charge determines the sign.

Worked example — primary p. 35. q₁ = 2 nC at (0,0); q₂ = 6 nC at (0.06,0) m. At A = (0,0.07) m, r₁ = 0.07 m and r₂ = √(0.06²+0.07²) = 0.092195 m. VA = 9 × 10⁹[2 × 10⁻⁹/0.07 + 6 × 10⁻⁹/0.092195] ≈ 842.86 V. Early rounding r₂ to 0.092 m gives about 844.10 V, explaining the source's nearby rounded answer.

Pair energy and assembling several charges

Multiply source potential by the added test charge: U = qV = kQq/r. Positive pair energy occurs for like charges with the infinity reference. Negative pair energy occurs for opposite charges. Negative energy is not a negative distance or an impossible state.

Derivation — assembly. Bring q₁ from infinity first: no interaction work. Bring q₂: add kq₁q₂/r₁₂. Bring q₃: add kq₃q₁/r₁₃ + kq₃q₂/r₂₃. Therefore:

$$U_{system}=k\sum_{i<j}\frac{q_iq_j}{r_{ij}}=\frac12\sum_i q_i V_{others}(\mathbf r_i).$$

The pair restriction prevents counting twice and excludes self-interaction. The factor 1/2 is necessary only in the second expression, not in a direct list of unique pairs.

Worked example — primary p. 26. q₁ = +4 nC at 0, q₂ = −3 nC at 0.20 m, q₃ = +2 nC at 0.10 m. Distances are 0.20, 0.10, 0.10 m. U = 9 × 10⁹[(−12 × 10⁻¹⁸)/0.20 +(8 × 10⁻¹⁸)/0.10 +(−6 × 10⁻¹⁸)/0.10] = −3.60 × 10⁻⁷ J. Charge order on the line does not replace the need to calculate all pair distances.

For bringing a new charge q into an existing fixed-source arrangement, Wexternal = qVexisting(final) − qVexisting(initial), provided the move is quasistatic. Do not re-add energy already stored among the fixed source charges.

Field–potential relation and equipotentials

$$\Delta V=-\int_a^b\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l,\quad \mathbf E=-\nabla V.$$

For uniform E, ΔV = −Ed cos θ where θ is the angle between E and displacement. If motion is parallel to an equipotential, ΔV = 0, so electrostatic work is zero. Field lines are perpendicular to equipotential surfaces where E is nonzero. At a field-zero point, there is no unique local field direction to draw.

Worked example — primary pp. 39–40. For q = 4 nC and k = 9 × 10⁹, kq = 36 V·m. The 10, 20, and 30 V surfaces have radii 3.6, 1.8, and 1.2 m. They are spheres; a plane through the charge shows circles. Equal voltage intervals do not give equal radial intervals.

Speed from a potential change

Combine conservation of energy with K = mv²/2:

$$v_f=\sqrt{v_i^2-\frac{2q\Delta V}{m}}.$$

The expression inside the root must be nonnegative. A negative value means the particle cannot reach that endpoint with the stated initial energy. From rest, an electron accelerates through a positive potential increase; a positive particle accelerates toward lower potential.

Companion example. A proton moves from rest 0.20 m along E = 1.0 × 10⁵ N/C. Wfield = eEd = 3.204 × 10⁻¹⁵ J. With mp = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, v = √(2W/mp) ≈ 1.96 × 10⁶ m/s. The equivalent potential change is −20,000 V.

Extensions: uniform rings and finite rods

Potential often simplifies a symmetric field calculation. On a ring's axis, every element has the same distance, giving V = kQ/√(x²+R²). Differentiating −dV/dx gives the ring field in M4. At the ring center, V = kQ/R while E = 0.

For a uniform rod from x = a to b and a field point on the same axis outside the rod, integrate dV = kλ dx/distance. The integral contains a logarithm, not the inverse-square antiderivative used for a point field. Set positive distances and the reference explicitly. These integrations are extensions of superposition, not replacements for the source's point-charge examples.

Complete the rod integration. Put the field point at x = s > b, with λ constant and V(∞) = 0. Each element's distance is s−x. The integration gives:

$$V(s)=k\lambda\int_a^b\frac{dx}{s-x}=k\lambda\ln\frac{s-a}{s-b},\qquad E_x=-\frac{dV}{ds}=k\lambda\left(\frac1{s-b}-\frac1{s-a}\right).$$

For a = 0, b = 0.20 m, s = 0.50 m, and λ = 2.0 nC/m, V ≈ 9.18465 V and Ex ≈ 23.9733 N/C. The logarithm uses a dimensionless ratio. The positive field points away from the positive rod.

Capacitors: first decide what stays fixed

A capacitor stores energy in an electric field between conductors. Its plates carry +Q and −Q. In C = Q/V, Q is the magnitude on ONE plate, not the algebraic total charge of the pair. One farad is one coulomb per volt. Use μF = 10⁻⁶ F, nF = 10⁻⁹ F, and pF = 10⁻¹² F.

$$C=Q/V,\quad Q=CV,\quad V=Q/C.$$

For a linear ideal capacitor, geometry and dielectric determine C. A larger Q does not automatically mean a larger C: the applied voltage can increase in the same proportion. Capacitive keyboard keys exploit smaller separation increasing capacitance.

Derive parallel-plate capacitance

For large parallel plates with edge effects neglected, σ = Q/A and E = σ/ε. The approximately uniform gap field gives V = Ed. Substitute:

$$V=\frac{Qd}{\epsilon A}\Rightarrow C=\frac QV=\frac{\epsilon A}{d}=\frac{\kappa\epsilon_0 A}{d}.$$

Here ε₀ ≈ 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m and κ is a dimensionless dielectric constant. Rearrangements are A = Cd/(κε₀), d = κε₀A/C, κ = Cd/(ε₀A). The parallel-plate model needs plate dimensions much larger than gap d. Do not use the same equation for arbitrary conductor shapes.

Worked example — primary p. 14. C = 1.0 F, d = 1.0 mm in vacuum gives A = 1 × 0.001/(8.85 × 10⁻¹²) = 1.13 × 10⁸ m². A missing milli prefix changes the answer by 1000.

Worked example — primary p. 14. A = 2.0 m², d = 5.0 mm, and V = 10,000 V give C = 3.54 × 10⁻⁹ F = 0.00354 μF, Q = 35.4 μC, and E = 2.0 × 10⁶ V/m. Check Q = CV and V = Ed independently.

Dielectrics: connected versus disconnected battery

A dielectric polarizes and increases capacitance when it fills the gap: Cnew = κCold. It can mechanically separate plates and permit greater voltage before breakdown, depending on the material and conditions.

Quantity Battery stays connected Battery is disconnected, ideal isolated capacitor
Voltage Fixed by ideal battery Divides by κ
Free charge magnitude Multiplies by κ Fixed
Capacitance Multiplies by κ Multiplies by κ
Field in fully filled fixed gap Fixed Divides by κ
Stored energy Multiplies by κ Divides by κ

Derive these changes. Start with Q = CV. Fix V in the connected case or Q in the isolated case. Then use U = CV²/2 or Q²/(2C). The connected capacitor can gain stored energy because the battery exchanges energy and charge. The isolated capacitor's energy decrease can become mechanical work during dielectric insertion.

Extensions — partial dielectric geometry. Two materials occupying side-by-side area fractions share the voltage, so C = ε₀(κ₁A₁ + κ₂A₂)/d. Layers stacked across the gap share the series charge, so 1/C = d₁/(κ₁ε₀A) + d₂/(κ₂ε₀A). Use the geometry to choose the connection model.

Derive all three energy expressions

The voltage rises while charging. An additional dq requires work dU = V(q)dq = (q/C)dq. Integrate from zero to Q:

$$U=\int_0^Q\frac qC\,dq=\frac{Q^2}{2C}=\frac12QV=\frac12CV^2.$$

Multiplying the FINAL voltage by the entire charge gives QV, twice the stored energy. A linear voltage rise has average charging voltage V/2. Rearranging gives V = √(2U/C), C = 2U/V², or Q = √(2CU), with magnitudes and physically positive C.

Worked example — primary p. 16. C = 5.80 μF and V = 400 V give U = 0.5(5.80 × 10⁻⁶)(400²) = 0.464 J. The answer fixes the exponent obscured in some PDF text extraction. A 5.0 μF capacitor at 1.5 V has Q = 7.5 μC.

Substitute C = εA/d and V = Ed: U = εE²Ad/2. Divide by gap volume Ad to derive field-energy density u = εE²/2 for a linear material.

Networks: derive series and parallel rules

Parallel branches share the SAME two nodes. Their voltages are equal and terminal charges add: Qtot = ΣCiV. Thus Ceq = ΣCi. Series capacitors in an initially uncharged isolated-intermediate-node chain carry equal charge magnitude. Their voltage drops add: Vtot = QΣ(1/Ci), so 1/Ceq = Σ1/Ci.

For two series capacitors, Ceq = C₁C₂/(C₁+C₂). Solving for an unknown gives C₂ = CeqC₁/(C₁−Ceq), requiring C₁ > Ceq > 0. In parallel, C₂ = Ceq − C₁ > 0. Series equivalent capacitance is smaller than the smallest element; parallel equivalent capacitance exceeds each positive element.

Worked example — primary p. 24. Connect 6 μF and 3 μF to 18 V. Series: Ceq = 2 μF, Qeach = 36 μC, V₆ = 6 V, V₃ = 12 V. Parallel: Ceq = 9 μF, Veach = 18 V, Q₆ = 108 μC, Q₃ = 54 μC. The smaller SERIES capacitor has the larger voltage because its charge magnitude is the same.

For a mixed network, label nodes before reducing it. Two nearby symbols are not necessarily parallel. Reduce a valid group, record its equivalent value, solve the terminal circuit, and then work backward. Use the original group’s shared Q or shared V to recover each element. Check ΣVi for series groups and ΣQi for parallel groups.

Worked extension — reduce, then restore the branches. Put 2 μF and 4 μF in parallel. Connect that group in series with 3 μF across 12 V. The parallel group is 6 μF. Its series equivalent with 3 μF is (6×3)/(6+3) = 2 μF. The source transfers Q = 2×12 = 24 μC to each series unit.

The 6 μF group has voltage 24/6 = 4 V. The 3 μF capacitor has voltage 24/3 = 8 V. Both parallel capacitors share 4 V, so their charges are 8 μC and 16 μC. Their sum is the group's 24 μC. Check energy: ½(2 μF)(12²) = 144 μJ. The individual energies are 16, 32, and 96 μJ, which also sum to 144 μJ. A single equivalent capacitance alone does not reveal every branch voltage.

Charge-sharing extension. Disconnect two capacitors from their supplies and join their positive plates together. With no leakage, Vf = (C₁V₁ + C₂V₂)/(C₁+C₂). Reverse one capacitor's polarity by using a negative signed initial voltage. Final energy need not equal the sum of initial capacitor energies; the connection can dissipate energy. Charge conservation does not imply capacitor-energy conservation.

For C₁ = 2 μF at 12 V and C₂ = 4 μF at 0 V, Vf = 4 V. Initial stored energy is 144 μJ; final energy is 48 μJ. The difference, 96 μJ, leaves the capacitor fields during the transient. Real connections transfer it through resistance, radiation, or other losses. An ideal zero-resistance instantaneous connection is not a complete transient model.

Current and DC circuits: label nodes, currents, and polarity

Current is signed charge flow per time: I = dQ/dt, or Q/Δt for a constant average flow. One ampere is one C/s. Conventional current points as positive charge would move. Electron drift in a metal points the opposite way. Random microscopic motion alone gives no net current. Current through a branch is a signed scalar; current density J is a vector.

Derive drift current and macroscopic resistance

In time Δt, carriers drift through volume AvdΔt. With number density n and charge magnitude |q|, charge crossing the area is nAvdΔt|q|. Divide by Δt:

$$I=n|q|Av_d,\quad J=I/A=n|q|v_d,\quad \mathbf J=nq\mathbf v_d.$$

The vector expression keeps the signed charge. Carrier concentration has units m⁻³, not C/m³. Current from several carrier populations adds. J parallel to E is the simple isotropic ohmic model; it is not a universal statement for every medium and magnetic-field condition.

Worked examples — primary pp. 16–17. 29.3 C in 7.84 s gives 3.737 A. A 0.010 A calculator for 60 s transfers 0.600 C. J = 50 A/m², q = −0.10 C, and vd = −2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m/s give n = J/(qvd) = 2.5 × 10⁶ m⁻³. The unusually large individual charge is the source's hypothetical value, not an electron charge.

For an ohmic isotropic material, E = ρJ and conductivity σ = 1/ρ. Substitute E = V/L and J = I/A for a uniform wire:

$$\frac VL=\rho\frac IA\Rightarrow V=IR,\quad R=\frac{\rho L}{A},\quad \rho=\frac{RA}{L}.$$

Resistivity ρ has units Ω·m; resistance R has units Ω. For a circular wire A = πr² = πd²/4. Doubling LENGTH at fixed area doubles R. Doubling DIAMETER at fixed length divides R by four. For the same wire stretched without volume change, A = Vwire/L, so R = ρL²/Vwire if resistivity stays constant.

Temperature, ohmic behavior, and resistor identification

Near a reference temperature, R = R₀[1 + α(T−T₀)], and similarly for resistivity. Rearrange α = (R/R₀−1)/(T−T₀). This linear approximation can fail over a large temperature range. Do not confuse the resistance-temperature coefficient with thermal-expansion α. R₀ is resistance when used in the R equation, despite the source's occasional “resistivity” label.

Ohm's law means a linear V–I relation under stable material conditions. A lamp heating up need not have a constant resistance. Carbon/semiconductor temperature behavior can differ from metals.

The source includes resistor color bands. For a four-band resistor, the first two colors give digits, the third gives a power-of-ten multiplier, and the fourth gives tolerance. Digit sequence: black 0, brown 1, red 2, orange 3, yellow 4, green 5, blue 6, violet 7, gray 8, white 9. Gold/silver commonly represent multipliers 10⁻¹/10⁻² or tolerances ±5%/±10%. Read the band roles and orientation, not only the colors.

Electrical power, energy, and internal resistance

An amount dq crossing voltage V transfers energy dW = Vdq. Divide by dt:

$$P=VI,\quad P_R=I^2R=V_R^2/R,\quad E_{energy}=Pt.$$

The resistor substitutions require Ohm's law. A kilowatt-hour is an ENERGY unit: 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J. It is not “kilowatts per hour.” At fixed V, increasing R lowers resistor power; at fixed I, increasing R raises it.

A real source has emf ℰ and internal resistance r. For discharging current I, terminal voltage V = ℰ − Ir and I = ℰ/(R+r). During charging with current driven into the positive terminal, V = ℰ + Ir under that stated convention. Emf is energy supplied per charge, not a mechanical force.

Worked example — companion heating element. R = 20 Ω on 120 V gives I = 6 A and P = 720 W. In 15 min = 900 s, energy = 648,000 J = 0.18 kWh. Increasing R to 30 Ω at the same voltage gives 480 W, not greater power.

Derive series and parallel resistor rules

Series resistors have a common current if there is no branch at their connecting node. Vtot = ΣIRi gives Req = ΣRi. Therefore Vi/Vtot = Ri/Req: the voltage-divider relation.

Parallel resistors share the same two nodes and voltage. Itot = ΣV/Ri gives 1/Req = Σ1/Ri. For two, Req = R₁R₂/(R₁+R₂). Their current ratio is I₁/I₂ = R₂/R₁. An equivalent parallel resistance must be smaller than the smallest positive branch resistance.

Worked example. Put 3 Ω and 6 Ω in parallel across 12 V. Req = 2 Ω; branch currents are 4 A and 2 A; total is 6 A. Total power = 12 × 6 = 72 W, also 4²×3 + 2²×6 = 72 W. A series 4 Ω added before that group gives 6 Ω overall. The source current becomes 2 A, the parallel-group drop becomes 4 V, and its branch currents become 4/3 A and 2/3 A.

An ideal ammeter goes in SERIES and has zero resistance. An ideal voltmeter goes in PARALLEL and has infinite resistance. An ideal wire shorts points into one node. A shorted resistor has zero voltage, not its previous unmodified branch voltage. Do not reduce an arbitrary bridge as series/parallel when the required node conditions fail.

Kirchhoff rules: derive the equations and keep the signs

KCL follows conservation of charge at a steady node: ΣIin = ΣIout. KVL follows energy balance around a lumped DC loop: ΣΔV = 0. A resistor traversed with the chosen current gives −IR; traversed against it gives +IR. Across an ideal battery, negative-to-positive gives +ℰ and the reverse gives −ℰ.

Choose arbitrary current directions, write independent equations, and solve them simultaneously. A negative answer reverses the assumed direction; it is not a negative physical resistance. Do not change an assumed arrow midway through the algebra.

Worked example — companion p. 14 type. A loop has a 12 V aiding source, an opposing 6 V source, and 6 Ω total resistance. KVL gives 12 − 6 − 6I = 0, so I = 1 A. If both sources aided, the same resistance would give 3 A.

Primary multi-branch example, pp. 70–74. Preserve the source's chosen arrows. Its independent equations are I₃ = I₁ + I₂, 30I₁ + 41I₃ = 45, and −30I₁ + 21I₂ = 80. Solving without early rounding gives I₁ = −2335/2721 ≈ −0.85814 A, I₂ ≈ 2.58361 A, and I₃ ≈ 1.72547 A. Substitute all three into the ORIGINAL equations. The negative I₁ reverses its chosen arrow. The printed intermediate rounded expressions obscure this small difference.

Show the elimination. Substitute I₃ = I₁+I₂ into the first loop. This produces 71I₁+41I₂ = 45. Keep −30I₁+21I₂ = 80 as the second loop. Multiply the first equation by 30 and the second by 71. Add them to remove I₁:

$$\begin{aligned}2130I_1+1230I_2&=1350,\\-2130I_1+1491I_2&=5680,\\2721I_2&=7030.\end{aligned}$$

Thus I₂ = 7030/2721. Back-substitution gives I₁ = (21I₂−80)/30 = −2335/2721 and I₃ = 4695/2721. Check KCL: −0.858140+2.583609 = 1.725469 A. Check the loops with full precision: 30I₁+41I₃ = 45 V and −30I₁+21I₂ = 80 V. A small residual after using rounded displayed currents is a rounding effect; a large residual needs corrected signs or algebra.

RC charging and discharging: derive the exponentials

For an initially uncharged capacitor C charged through R from constant emf ℰ, KVL gives ℰ − R(dq/dt) − q/C = 0. Separate dq/(Cℰ−q) = dt/(RC). Integrate with q(0)=0:

$$q=C\mathcal E(1-e^{-t/RC}),\quad V_C=\mathcal E(1-e^{-t/RC}),\quad I=\frac{\mathcal E}{R}e^{-t/RC}.$$

For discharging through R, R(dq/dt)+q/C = 0, so q = Q₀e⁻ᵗᐟᴿᶜ and VC = V₀e⁻ᵗᐟᴿᶜ. The signed current depends on its reference arrow; its magnitude is V₀e⁻ᵗᐟᴿᶜ/R. The capacitor voltage cannot jump instantaneously in this ideal finite-current model.

$$\tau_{RC}=RC,\quad t_{discharge}=-RC\ln f,\quad t_{charge}=-RC\ln(1-f).$$

At one τ, discharge leaves 36.8%; charging reaches 63.2%. “63%” is rounded, so exactly 63.000% is not exactly one τ. For an arbitrary initial voltage V₀, VC(t) = Vf + (V₀−Vf)e⁻ᵗᐟᴿᶜ. A network's relevant R is the resistance seen by the capacitor with independent ideal sources appropriately deactivated, not always the sum of all printed resistors.

Worked example — primary p. 79. C = 35 μF, R = 120 Ω. τ = 0.00420 s. To reach 10% of the initial voltage, t = −0.00420 ln(0.10) = 0.009671 s = 9.67 ms. Do not enter 10 in place of 0.10.

Show the charging integration. The separated equation integrates from q = 0 to q(t), and from time 0 to t. The minus sign comes from differentiating Cℰ−q:

$$-\ln\left(\frac{C\mathcal E-q}{C\mathcal E}\right)=\frac{t}{RC}\Rightarrow 1-\frac q{C\mathcal E}=e^{-t/RC}.$$

For R = 2.0 kΩ, C = 10 μF, and ℰ = 12 V, τ = 0.020 s and Qfinal = 120 μC. At t = 0.020 s, q ≈ 75.854 μC, VC ≈ 7.58545 V, and I ≈ 2.20728 mA. Check KVL: RI+VC = 4.41455+7.58545 = 12 V. To reach 90%, solve t = −0.020 ln(0.10) ≈ 0.04605 s.

Transient energy extension. During a complete ideal RC charge from zero, the battery supplies ∫ℰI dt = Cℰ². The capacitor stores Cℰ²/2. The resistor dissipates the remaining Cℰ²/2, independent of positive R in this model. A smaller R changes the time and peak power; it does not remove that total dissipated energy.

Magnetic fields: the cross product fixes the direction

Like magnetic poles repel and unlike poles attract. Breaking a bar magnet makes smaller dipoles, not isolated ordinary north and south poles. Outside a bar magnet, conventional field lines go north to south; they continue through the magnet to form closed loops. A compass's north-seeking end gives the local field direction. Earth's geographic and magnetic pole descriptions are not identical.

Oersted's current-carrying wire deflects a compass, showing that current creates a magnetic field. Do not confuse this source-field right-hand grip rule with the force cross-product rule.

Force on a moving charge and on a wire

$$\mathbf F_B=q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B,\quad F_B=|q|vB\sin\theta.$$

θ is between velocity and B. For positive q, curl the right-hand direction from v toward B; reverse the result for negative q. A charge at rest has no magnetic force from this law. Parallel or antiparallel motion gives zero magnetic force, not maximum force. Perpendicular motion gives the maximum magnitude.

Derive wire force. A segment of length L and cross-sectional area A contains nAL carriers. Each has transverse force qvd×B. Sum and use I = n|q|Avd to obtain F = IL×B, with L directed along conventional current. For a curved wire use I∫dl×B. Only the length inside the field contributes.

Worked example — primary p. 14. I = 5.0 A east; L = 1.0 m; B = 1.20 T at 45° north of east. F = 5 × 1 × 1.2 sin45° = 4.243 N. East × north points up in an east/north/up coordinate system.

Worked example — primary p. 20. An electron moves +x at 8.0 × 10⁶ m/s. A 0.025 T field makes 60° to +x in the +y half of the xy plane. Magnitude = e v B sin60° = 2.775 × 10⁻¹⁴ N. Since +x × +y = +z, the electron's force is −z. Without specifying the y-half-plane, the angle alone does not fix that sign.

Circular and helical motion: derived extensions

A magnetic force does no work on a point charge because v·(v×B)=0. It changes direction, not speed, in a purely magnetic uniform field. For perpendicular motion, set |q|vB = mv²/r:

$$r=\frac{mv_\perp}{|q|B},\quad \omega_c=\frac{|q|B}{m},\quad T=\frac{2\pi m}{|q|B}.$$

For an oblique entry, vparallel remains constant, while vperpendicular circles. Helical pitch = vparallelT. These are nonrelativistic formulas. Combining kinetic energy K with r gives r = √(2mK)/(|q|B) for perpendicular entry. In crossed perpendicular E and B fields, straight undeflected motion requires |q|E = |q|vB, so v = E/B with force directions opposing.

Torque on a current loop

Opposite sides of a loop in uniform B have equal and opposite forces. Net force can vanish while torque remains. The lever-arm geometry yields:

$$\boldsymbol\mu=NI\mathbf A,\quad \boldsymbol\tau=\boldsymbol\mu\times\mathbf B,\quad \tau=NIAB\sin\theta,\quad U=-\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B.$$

Here θ is between the AREA NORMAL and B, not between the loop's plane and B. For angle φ to the plane, sinθ = cosφ. Maximum torque occurs when the normal is perpendicular to B. Zero torque occurs when the normal is parallel or antiparallel; the parallel orientation has the lower potential energy.

Worked example — primary p. 28. Diameter = 20.0 cm, so r = 0.100 m and A = π(0.100)². With N = 10, I = 3.00 A, B = 2.00 T, τmax = 1.885 N·m and τmin = 0. The primary's side-force label involving IAB should not be read as the wire-force law: force uses a LENGTH, while torque uses an AREA.

A galvanometer turns a current-carrying coil against a restoring effect. Analog meters build on that principle. A motor converts electrical energy to mechanical energy; a loudspeaker moves a coil and cone with a varying force.

Long wires, parallel-current force, and Ampere's law

Magnetostatic Ampere's law is ∮B·dl = μ₀Ienclosed. For a long straight wire, cylindrical symmetry makes B constant on a circular path. Therefore B(2πr) = μ₀I:

$$B=\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r},\quad \frac FL=I_2B_1=\frac{\mu_0 I_1I_2}{2\pi d}.$$

Use μ₀ ≈ 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A for course calculations. Same-direction parallel currents attract; opposite directions repel. The distance is between the wire axes, not wire length. Fields from several wires add as vectors.

Worked examples — primary pp. 33 and 36. A 25 A upward wire at r = 0.10 m gives B = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ T. At a point north of that wire, the direction is west. Two wires carrying 8.0 A, separated by 0.0030 m over 2.0 m length, exert F = 8.533 × 10⁻³ N. Their relative current directions determine attraction or repulsion.

Solenoids, cores, and scope of symmetry

For an ideal long solenoid, an Amperian rectangle has negligible external contribution. Enclosed current is (N/ℓ)I times its interior path length. Thus B = μ₀(N/ℓ)I, or μnI in an ideal linear core. ℓ is the SOLENOID length, not the total winding-wire length.

Worked example — primary p. 39. N = 420, ℓ = 0.12 m, I = 2.0 A. B = 4π × 10⁻⁷(420/0.12)(2) = 8.796 × 10⁻³ T near the center in the long-solenoid approximation.

An iron core can increase field strongly, but a constant relative permeability is only an approximation; real cores can saturate and show hysteresis. Ampere's law alone does not let you pull B outside the integral without sufficient symmetry. For a circular coil's center, a separate Biot–Savart integration gives B = μ₀NI/(2R); do not substitute the long-solenoid formula into a single-loop problem.

Derive the circular-coil result. Biot–Savart gives dB = μ₀I dl sinθ/(4πr²). At a loop's center, r = R and every tangent element is perpendicular to its radius. All field contributions point along the same axis. Integrate dl around circumference 2πR and multiply by N:

$$B_{center}=\frac{\mu_0NI}{4\pi R^2}\oint dl=\frac{\mu_0NI}{4\pi R^2}(2\pi R)=\frac{\mu_0NI}{2R}.$$

For 20 turns, 0.050 m radius, and 2.0 A, B ≈ 5.027 × 10⁻⁴ T. This formula describes thin coincident loops. A long coil uses the solenoid approximation instead. A magnetic field created by this coil can exert force on another wire; do not substitute the wire's own field into a simple external-field force calculation.

Worked trajectory extension. A proton enters perpendicular to 0.20 T at 1.0 × 10⁶ m/s. Use mp = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Its radius is 0.05212 m and period is 3.275 × 10⁻⁷ s. Doubling the speed doubles the radius but leaves the nonrelativistic period unchanged. A parallel velocity component adds a helical displacement instead of changing this perpendicular radius.

Induction: change in flux, not merely the presence of a magnet

An induced emf can exist without a closed conducting circuit. A closed path is required for the ordinary induced current examples. A stationary loop in a time-varying magnetic field can have emf even when no wire moves. Conversely, motion that leaves the relevant flux unchanged need not produce a net loop emf.

Magnetic flux and all three routes to a changing flux

$$\Phi_B=\int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf A,\quad \Phi_B=BA\cos\theta\ \text{for uniform }B.$$

The angle is between B and the AREA NORMAL. If a problem gives the angle to the plane, use the complementary angle. One weber is one T·m². A field perpendicular to the plane produces maximum flux magnitude; a field parallel to the plane produces zero flux.

If B occupies only an overlap area, integrate over that region. Primary p. 10 has flux BA₂, not BA₁, when the field is confined to the smaller perpendicular overlap area A₂. Treat “zero outside the field region” explicitly.

Worked example — primary p. 11. A 0.100 m square loop in 1.25 T has A = 0.0100 m². Maximum magnitude = 0.0125 Wb; minimum magnitude = 0. Signed flux can range from −0.0125 to +0.0125 Wb depending on the chosen normal.

Faraday's law and the full product derivative

Faraday's law is a fundamental induction law in this course. For N turns linking the same flux, ℰ = −N dΦB/dt. For nonidentical linked fluxes, sum the flux linkages instead of assuming NΦ. The finite average magnitude is N|ΔΦ|/Δt; it equals the instantaneous value only for a constant rate during the interval.

$$\mathcal E=-N\left(A\cos\theta\frac{dB}{dt}+B\cos\theta\frac{dA}{dt}-BA\sin\theta\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right).$$

This product-rule expression covers changing field strength, changing area, changing orientation, and simultaneous changes. Set only the quantities explicitly held constant to zero.

Worked example — primary p. 18. N = 200; square side = 0.180 m; R = 2.0 Ω; ΔB = 0.500 T in 0.80 s, with perpendicular field. A = 0.0324 m². |ℰ| = 200(0.0324)(0.500)/0.80 = 4.05 V. Current magnitude = 4.05/2.0 = 2.025 A. The source's 4.1 V is rounded.

Lenz's law with four direction cases

Choose the external field direction, then determine whether its flux increases or decreases. The induced field OPPOSES THE CHANGE, not necessarily the external field. Use the right-hand grip rule to convert the required induced field into conventional current.

External flux seen by the observer Required induced field Induced current as seen by that observer
Into page, increasing Out of page Counterclockwise
Into page, decreasing Into page Clockwise
Out of page, increasing Into page Clockwise
Out of page, decreasing Out of page Counterclockwise

Primary p. 28 prints a decreasing inward-field example with the wrong outward induced field. For its stated inward and decreasing flux, the correction is inward induced field and clockwise current. Always check the actual loop position and arrow before applying a memorized wire-direction answer.

Motional emf from two derivations

Flux derivation. A rod of length ℓ slides distance dx in a perpendicular uniform B. The loop's area change is dA = ℓdx, so |ℰ| = Bℓ(dx/dt) = Bℓv.

Charge-force derivation. Charges in an open moving rod redistribute until qE balances the magnetic qvB. Thus E = vB, and voltage magnitude Eℓ = Bℓv. The general expression is ∫(v×B)·dl; the product Bℓv needs the stated perpendicular geometry.

For a sliding rod in a closed resistance R: I = Bℓv/R. Magnetic drag magnitude is F = IℓB = B²ℓ²v/R. Required mechanical power at constant speed is Fv = I²R, which verifies energy conservation.

Worked example — primary p. 35. Speed 1000 km/h = 277.778 m/s. A 70 m wingspan moving perpendicular to a nearly vertical 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ T field develops about 0.9722 V between tips. Which tip is positive depends on the velocity and field vector directions.

Generators, microphones, transformers, and eddy currents

For a rigid rotating N-turn coil, Φ = BAcos(ωt + φ). Differentiate to derive ℰ(t) = NBAωsin(ωt + φ), with peak NBAω. A GENERATOR converts mechanical input into electrical output. Primary p. 14 reverses that conversion in its wording; it describes motor action instead. A moving-coil microphone converts mechanical vibration into an electrical signal.

An ideal transformer links both windings to the same changing core flux. Dividing their Faraday equations gives Vs/Vp = Ns/Np. With ideal power conservation, VpIp = VsIs and Is/Ip = Np/Ns. These are compatible AC magnitudes, usually rms values. A transformer does not directly convert AC into DC; a rectifier and other power-supply stages are required.

Worked example — primary p. 19. Ideal 120 V to 9 V AC, Ns = 30, Is = 0.400 A gives Np = 400 turns, output power = 3.6 W, and input current = 0.030 A. The slide's description of 9 V DC includes an implicit rectifier; the transformer equations alone address the AC stage.

Eddy currents circulate in bulk conductors. Their opposing magnetic effects can slow moving metal. Energy becomes heat. A changing current in one coil can induce emf in a nearby coil even without a conducting connection between them.

Mutual/self-inductance and stored magnetic energy

Define mutual inductance through ℰ₂ = −M dI₁/dt. For a linear coil, flux linkage NΦ = LI, giving ℰself = −L dI/dt. L and M have units henry = V·s/A = Ω·s. Constant permeability and fixed geometry are part of the simple constant-L model.

For an ideal long solenoid, B = μNI/ℓ. Flux per turn is BA. Therefore L = NΦ/I = μN²A/ℓ. Derive stored energy by P = LI(dI/dt) and integrate: U = ∫₀ᴵLi di = LI²/2. Inductors resist a CHANGE in current; they do not simply “oppose current” in every situation.

Substitute L = μN²A/ℓ into U = LI²/2 and replace NI/ℓ with B/μ. This gives U = B²Aℓ/(2μ). Divide by the solenoid's internal field volume Aℓ to get magnetic energy density uB = B²/(2μ). Compare the electric result uE = εE²/2. Both formulas assume the specified linear material model.

RL growth, decay, and arbitrary initial current

KVL for an ideal L in series with total R gives ℰ = L(dI/dt)+RI. Separate and integrate with I(0)=0:

$$I(t)=\frac{\mathcal E}{R}(1-e^{-tR/L}),\quad \tau_{RL}=L/R.$$

After removing the source while retaining a closed R–L path, I = I₀e⁻ᵗᴿᐟᴸ. More generally I(t)=If+(I₀−If)e⁻ᵗᐟτ. Inductor current is continuous in this ideal finite-voltage model. At DC steady state, an ideal inductor has zero voltage; real winding resistance remains.

For fraction f of final growth, t = −(L/R)ln(1−f). For remaining decay fraction f, t = −(L/R)ln f. RC increases its time constant with R; RL decreases its time constant with R. Do not interchange them.

Worked example — primary p. 44. L = 87.5 mH = 0.0875 H and R = 0.250 Ω. τ = 0.350 s. Growth reaches 1−e⁻¹ = 63.212% at 0.350 s. Exactly 63% requires −0.350ln(0.37) ≈ 0.348 s. The printed “63%” convention is a rounding approximation.

Show the RL integration. Rearrange the loop equation to dI/(ℰ−RI) = dt/L. Integrate using I(0) = 0. The left side contributes −ln[(ℰ−RI)/ℰ]/R. Therefore:

$$-\ln\left(1-\frac{RI}{\mathcal E}\right)=\frac{Rt}{L}\Rightarrow I=\frac{\mathcal E}{R}(1-e^{-Rt/L}).$$

For ℰ = 12 V, R = 6 Ω, and L = 3 H, τ = 0.5 s and Ifinal = 2 A. At one time constant, I ≈ 1.26424 A. The resistor drop is 7.58545 V; the inductor drop is ℰe⁻¹ ≈ 4.41455 V. Their sum remains 12 V. Initial current is zero, while the initial inductor drop is the full 12 V. At long times, the ideal inductor drop tends to zero and stored energy tends to ½(3)(2²) = 6 J.

Switching check. A source-free decay needs a closed path. Opening the only path does not make a real inductor's current disappear without consequence. Its induced voltage can become large and cause a spark or flow through a protection component. The simple exponential requires a valid R–L discharge path.

Light and optics: measure angles from the normal

Electromagnetic waves in vacuum satisfy c = fλ, with c = 299,792,458 m/s exactly. Use 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s when the problem's precision supports that rounding. In a medium, v = c/n and v = fλmedium. The frequency stays the same across a stationary interface; speed and wavelength can change.

Wave quantities and wavelength conversion

$$\lambda_0=c/f,\quad f=c/\lambda_0,\quad n=c/v,\quad \lambda_{medium}=\lambda_0/n.$$

Worked example — primary p. 8. With c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s, 4.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz corresponds to 750 nm. A frequency of 7.9 × 10¹⁴ Hz corresponds to about 380 nm. Higher frequency means shorter wavelength in the same medium. Convert 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m before calculating energy or speed.

The source introduces Newton's particle account, Huygens' wave account, and Young's interference evidence. Modern light has wave and particle behavior; neither everyday particles nor classical waves alone explain all observations. Ray optics approximates propagation by rays when wavelength is small compared with the relevant geometry.

Photon energy and a necessary source correction

$$E_\gamma=hf=hc/\lambda_0,\quad K_{max}=hf-\phi,\quad f_0=\phi/h,\quad eV_{stop}=K_{max}.$$

The source introduces E = hf. The photoelectric relations above extend that idea to explain its experiment correctly. Photon energy must first supply work function φ; excess energy becomes maximum electron kinetic energy. If hf < φ, the model predicts no single-photon photoemission regardless of ordinary intensity increases.

Primary p. 10 incorrectly says electron kinetic energy is independent of frequency. Maximum kinetic energy INCREASES with frequency above threshold. At fixed frequency, higher intensity increases available photon rate, not each photon's energy. See the supplementary photoelectric reference in the source notes.

Worked extension. At λ = 500 nm, Eγ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)(3.00 × 10⁸)/(500 × 10⁻⁹) = 3.976 × 10⁻¹⁹ J ≈ 2.482 eV. For φ = 2.00 eV, Kmax ≈ 0.482 eV and stopping-potential magnitude ≈ 0.482 V. The boundary wavelength hc/φ is about 621 nm. A longer wavelength cannot supply the required photon energy in this model.

Reflection and surfaces

The law of reflection is θr = θi, with both angles measured from the surface NORMAL. A 30° angle to the surface is a 60° incidence angle. Incident ray, reflected ray, and normal lie in one plane. A smooth surface gives specular reflection; a rough surface gives diffuse reflection through differing local normals. Diffuse reflection does not mean each microscopic reflection violates the law.

Derivation extension — stationary travel time. For a reflection point sliding along a flat mirror in one uniform medium, total travel time is proportional to the sum of incoming and outgoing path lengths. Differentiate that sum with respect to the reflection point. Setting its derivative to zero gives equal sine ratios and hence θi = θr in the ordinary reflection range.

Let the endpoints have horizontal separation d and heights h₁,h₂ above the mirror. Put the reflection point at horizontal coordinate x. The path length is √(x²+h₁²)+√((d−x)²+h₂²). Its derivative vanishes when x/√(x²+h₁²) = (d−x)/√((d−x)²+h₂²). These ratios are sinθi and sinθr, measured from the vertical normal. Their equality gives the reflection law.

Snell's law: derive it from phase matching

At a stationary planar boundary, the wave phase must agree along the interface. Tangential wavevector components match: k₁sinθ₁ = k₂sinθ₂. Since k = 2πn/λ₀ at a common frequency:

$$n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2,\quad \theta_2=\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{n_1}{n_2}\sin\theta_1\right).$$

Rearrange to n₂ = n₁sinθ₁/sinθ₂ or θ₁ = asin(n₂sinθ₂/n₁) when the physical domain permits. The sine argument must lie between −1 and +1. In the ordinary incidence range, n₂ > n₁ bends toward the normal; n₂ < n₁ bends away. At normal incidence, direction stays normal although speed and wavelength can change.

Independent derivation extension — stationary time. With one endpoint above and the other below the interface, travel time is t(x) = (n₁/c)√(x²+h₁²)+(n₂/c)√((d−x)²+h₂²). Differentiate with respect to x. Setting dt/dx = 0 gives n₁sinθ₁−n₂sinθ₂ = 0. This reproduces Snell's law without assuming equal speeds in the two media. It applies to the stated uniform, stationary media and planar boundary.

Worked example — primary p. 25. Air n₁ = 1.00 to water n₂ = 1.33, θ₁ = 45°. θ₂ = asin(sin45°/1.33) ≈ 32.12°. A value measured from the surface would be its complement.

Several interfaces and the water–glass–air answer

For parallel boundaries, the refracted angle inside the middle layer equals its incidence angle at the next boundary. Applying Snell twice gives n₁sinθ₁ = n₂sinθ₂ = n₃sinθ₃. The middle index cancels from the final angle relation, but not from the internal angle or lateral displacement.

Worked correction — primary pp. 26–27. Water n = 1.3, glass n = 1.5, air n = 1.0; start at 20° from the normal in water. Reflection in water gives θA = 20°. Glass refraction gives θB = asin[(1.3/1.5)sin20°] ≈ 17.24°. For parallel faces θC = 17.24°. Exit into air gives θD = asin[1.3sin20°] ≈ 26.40°, not the printed 20°. Returning to the original angle would require equal first and final indices, which this drawing does not have.

Parallel-slab extension. For slab thickness t, internal angle r, and external incidence i, perpendicular lateral displacement is s = t sin(i−r)/cos r. Derive it by drawing the internal path of length t/cos r and projecting its deviation from the original ray. This formula assumes parallel faces and an exit into the original medium without total internal reflection.

Critical angle and total internal reflection

Set the transmitted angle to 90° in Snell's law:

$$\sin\theta_c=n_2/n_1,\quad \theta_c=\sin^{-1}(n_2/n_1),\quad n_1>n_2.$$

Total internal reflection requires travel from larger n to smaller n AND θi > θc. At θi = θc, the ideal refracted ray grazes the boundary; it is the threshold, not a greater-angle case. If n₁ ≤ n₂, there is no real critical angle for this direction of travel.

Worked example. Glass n = 1.50 to air n = 1.00 gives θc ≈ 41.81°. At 30°, refraction remains possible. At 50°, Snell's proposed transmitted sine exceeds one, so no propagating refracted ray exists in the elementary model. Use reflection rather than reporting a complex physical ray angle.

An optical fiber uses a higher-index core and lower-index cladding. Suitable core rays meet the boundary above its critical angle. The ideal ray picture guides them by total internal reflection. Real fibers still have attenuation, absorption, scattering, and bend losses; primary p. 31's “no absorption” wording is an idealization.

Fiber acceptance extension. In the meridional-ray model, let n₀ be the entrance-medium index, ncore the core index, and nclad the cladding index. For a ray inside the core at angle θ to its axis, wall incidence is 90°−θ. At the limiting ray, cosθ = nclad/ncore. Use sin²θ+cos²θ = 1, then apply Snell at the flat entrance:

$$n_0\sin\theta_{accept}=\sqrt{n_{core}^2-n_{clad}^2}=NA.$$

For a core index 1.48 and cladding index 1.46, NA ≈ 0.24249. From air, the limiting entrance half-angle is about 14.03°. This is an angle to the fiber AXIS at the entrance, not the wall incidence angle. This ideal extension does not describe every skew ray, bend, or waveguide mode.
